The Roman Dodecahedron: Ancient Mystery That Still Baffles Experts

It was the year 1739, in the English village of Aston, in Hertfordshire. A farmer was plowing his field when his plow hit something hard. He dug it out. It was a strange object that he had never seen before.

It was a small, hollow, twelve-sided shape made of bronze. It was about the size of a cricket ball. Each flat face was a five-sided shape called a pentagon. Each face had a round hole, and the holes were different sizes. Small round knobs stuck out from every corner.

The object was clearly ancient, because a layer of age, called patina, covered its surface. It was also clearly made on purpose. Its exact shape could not have come from nature.

The farmer took it to the Society of Antiquaries in London. The learned gentlemen there studied it and were baffled. They had no idea what it was. No writing and no record gave any clue.

Since then, more than 130 similar objects have been found across the lands of the old Roman Empire. They turned up in England, France, Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Austria, Switzerland, and Hungary.

They are between 4 and 11 centimeters across. They weigh from 35 grams to 1 kilogram. All are made of a copper mix, all were cast with the lost-wax method, and all have twelve pentagon faces with round holes of different sizes. All have small knobs at the corners. And nobody has ever clearly explained what they were used for.

The Roman dodecahedron is one of the longest-lasting puzzles of classical archaeology. Its mystery is not that it is strange or fantastic. It is exactly the opposite. It is so ordinary.

These are bronze objects, made in large numbers by skilled metalworkers. They were spread across one part of the Roman Empire over about three centuries. Someone made them. Someone used them. Someone valued them enough to bury them with the dead, or to place them in temples and homes.

And yet the Romans loved writing, record-keeping, and paperwork. They left not one word about what these objects were for. The dodecahedrons sit in museum cases from Tongeren to Bonn to London. They are silent and puzzling, daring scholars to crack their secret.

Every Roman dodecahedron has the same basic form. A regular dodecahedron is a shape with twelve flat faces. Each face is a perfect, or nearly perfect, pentagon.

Each face has a round hole cut in its center. The holes open into the hollow inside. At each of the twenty corners, a small ball-shaped knob sticks out. The result is beautiful. It looks like something from a math book, not from the ancient world.

The dodecahedron is one of the five Platonic solids. These are regular shapes described by the Greek thinker Plato in his book Timaeus, about 360 BCE. Plato linked the dodecahedron to the cosmos itself. He wrote that “God used it for arranging the constellations on the whole heaven.”

The dodecahedrons are very alike, yet each one is a little different. They range in size from 4 to 11 centimeters. The holes range from 6 to 40 millimeters wide. The key point is that no two dodecahedrons have the same pattern of holes. Each has its own mix of larger and smaller holes. This has fed endless guessing about their purpose.

The knobs at the corners are not perfectly regular. This suggests they were shaped by hand and not just poured into a mold. The outsides are neatly finished and were probably polished in Roman times. The insides are rough and unfinished. Some scholars think this means the inside was never meant to be seen.

No dodecahedron has ever been found with letters, numbers, or any writing on it. They rarely show signs of wear. So they were probably not used hard or rubbed often.

Where they were found is one of the most puzzling parts. More than 130 have been found over a wide area of the old Roman Empire. They have turned up in towns, temples, villas, and graves.

But not one has ever been found in Italy. There are none from Rome, Pompeii, or Ostia. There are none from the Italian lands at the heart of the Roman world. There are also none from the eastern provinces, such as Greece, Turkey, Syria, or Egypt.

This pattern rules out some explanations at once. If they were standard Roman army equipment, we would find them everywhere in the Empire. If they were standard religious tools, we would find them in temples everywhere.

They appear only in the northwestern provinces, mostly in places with Gallo-Roman culture. This strongly suggests a purpose special to the Celtic and Germanic peoples who lived there. Or it may come from the mixed culture that grew up when Roman and Celtic ways met.

Most finds come from Gaul, which is now France and Belgium, and from Britannia, which is now England. Important finds were made at Avenches, Switzerland, the Roman city of Aventicum. Others came from Tongeren, Belgium, where the Gallo-Roman Museum has one of the best collections. Many sites in Germany along the Rhine frontier also gave finds.

Most were found in everyday places, such as homes, workshops, and temples, and not in army bases. Many were found with coins and pottery. This suggests people treasured them as personal things. Some were found in graves, so they may have been important enough to go with the dead into the afterlife.

Most date from the 2nd to the 4th century CE. That was when Gallo-Roman culture was at its peak.

Over three centuries, scholars have offered dozens of ideas. One common idea is that they were candleholders or lamp stands. But no dodecahedron has wax on it. And the different hole sizes make little sense for a candleholder.

Another idea is that they were tools for surveying, or for judging distances. G.M. de Croot suggested this in 1991. The holes of different sizes would be peepholes for estimating distance. But this does not explain why each dodecahedron has its own pattern.

Some think they were religious or cult objects. Plato linked the shape to the cosmos, and many were found in or near temples. But no known Roman or Celtic religious practice uses twelve-sided objects.

Others say they were gaming dice, since a dodecahedron can be rolled. But no game with such dice is known from the Roman world. And the knobs would make it roll in an uneven way.

A further idea is that they were astronomical calculators. The hole sizes would stand for different angles for sighting the sun or stars. This is appealing, and it fits Plato’s link to the cosmos. But there is no solid proof.

Another explanation has come forward as perhaps the best so far. It is the knitting nancy, or finger-ring-making, theory. It says the dodecahedrons were used to make bronze wire finger rings. These were a common kind of jewelry in the Roman world, especially in the northwest.

A wire would be fed through a hole and wrapped around the knobs in a spiral. This would form a coiled ring. The different hole sizes would fit different thicknesses of wire. The different sizes of dodecahedron would make rings of different widths.

Modern craftspeople have tried it, and the method works. It makes handsome rings that can be worn. But critics say that not all dodecahedrons suit this job. Some have holes too small for wire work. And the theory does not explain why the objects turn up in so many places, including temples and graves.

The most frustrating part may be the complete lack of writing about them. The Romans wrote a great deal. They wrote laws, army reports, engineering handbooks, religious texts, personal letters, graffiti, and shopping lists. Yet in all the Roman writing that survives, not one word describes a dodecahedron. The silence is total.

There are several ideas about why. Maybe the objects were so common that nobody thought to write about them. Maybe the knowledge was spoken, not written, and passed on by training. Maybe their purpose was secret or sacred and was kept out of the records on purpose. Or maybe the texts once existed and were lost. Most Roman writing has not survived.

In 2023, researchers at Amsterdam University began to use 3D scanning. They made detailed digital models of Roman dodecahedrons from museums across Europe. The project was led by the 4D Research Lab. It used structured-light scanning, which shines patterns of light on an object to measure it. The models were accurate to less than a millimeter, so they could be compared by computer.

The scans showed that the objects are less perfect than they look. The knobs are a little uneven. The holes are not exactly centered. The faces are not perfectly flat. This suggests that each one was made by hand, one at a time.

The scans also showed signs of repair and change on some of them. So these objects were valuable. People kept them for a long time, instead of throwing them away and buying new ones.

The Roman dodecahedron reminds us that the past is not a puzzle to be solved. It is more like a landscape to be explored. We have more than 130 of these objects, from three centuries and nine countries. We have exact measurements, chemical tests, 3D scans, and many years of scholars arguing.

Yet the main question, “What is it for?”, still has no answer. The people who made them lived, worked, loved, and died in the Roman provinces of Gaul and Britannia. They used these objects for reasons that were clear to them. It was so clear that nobody wrote it down. The knowledge passed from hand to hand and from one generation to the next. Until it did not.

Now we are left with the objects themselves. They are beautiful, puzzling, and silent. Perhaps their greatest gift is not the answer they hide. It is the curiosity they spark. Even in an age of satellites and supercomputers, the ancient world can still make us say: “We don’t know.”
