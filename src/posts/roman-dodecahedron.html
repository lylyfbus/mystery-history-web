The Roman Dodecahedron: Ancient Mystery That Still Baffles Experts

Something strange in a field

It was the year 1739, in the English village of Aston, in Hertfordshire. A strange object was dug up from a field there. Nobody had seen anything like it before. It was found together with some Roman coins.

It was a small, hollow, twelve-sided shape made of bronze. Each flat face was a five-sided shape called a pentagon. Each face had a round hole, and the holes were different sizes. Small round knobs stuck out from every corner.

The object was clearly ancient, because a layer of age, called patina, covered its surface. It was also clearly made on purpose. Its exact shape could not have come from nature.

That year, it was shown to the Society of Antiquaries in London. The scholars there could not say what it was. No writing and no record gave any clue. Let us look at the object more closely.

A shape from a math book

The object from Aston is a puzzle of classical archaeology. Its mystery is not that it is fantastic. It is almost the opposite. It is so ordinary.

A shape with twelve flat faces is called a dodecahedron. In every Roman dodecahedron, each face is a perfect, or nearly perfect, pentagon. A round hole is cut in the center of each face. The holes open into the hollow inside. At each of the twenty corners, a small ball-shaped knob sticks out.

It looks like something from a math book, not from the ancient world. The Greek thinker Plato wrote about this shape in his book Timaeus, about 360 BCE. He wrote that “the god used it for arranging the constellations on the whole heaven.”

These objects are very alike, yet each one is a little different. They range in size from 4 to 11 centimeters. The holes range from 6 to 40 millimeters wide, and the hole sizes differ from one object to the next.

The outsides are neatly finished and were probably polished in Roman times. The insides are rough and unfinished. So who made them, and where did they go?

Found far from Rome

Since 1739, about 130 similar objects have been found across the lands of the old Roman Empire. They turned up in England, France, Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Austria, Switzerland, and Hungary. They weigh from 35 grams to 1 kilogram. They are made of a copper mix and were cast with the lost-wax method.

But none has been found in Italy. Most come from Gaul, which is now France and Belgium, and from Britannia, which is now England. Others come from Germany and other lands along the Rhine. Most date from the 2nd to the 4th century CE.

This pattern makes some ideas less likely. If they were standard Roman army equipment, we might find them everywhere in the Empire. They appear mostly in the northwestern provinces, in places with Gallo-Roman culture. That is the mixed culture that grew up when Roman and local ways met. So the purpose may have been local.

Some were found with coins and other valuables. This suggests people treasured them. Some were found in graves.

The silence of the Romans

Here is the most frustrating part. The Romans wrote a great deal. They wrote laws, army reports, engineering handbooks, religious texts, personal letters, graffiti, and shopping lists. Yet no Roman text is known that says what these objects were for.

There are several ideas about why. Maybe nobody thought to write about them. Maybe the knowledge was spoken, not written, and passed on by training. Maybe their purpose was secret or sacred. Or maybe the texts once existed and were lost. Most Roman writing has not survived.

Almost none of the objects has letters or numbers on it. Some show very little wear. So they may not have been used hard.

Without any words to help, scholars have had to guess. They have offered more than fifty ideas. Let us start with two that do not fit well.

Candles and dice

One idea is that they were candleholders. Wax was found in only one of them. And the different hole sizes make little sense for a candleholder.

Another idea is that they were gaming dice, since a dodecahedron can be rolled. But no game with such dice is known from the Roman world.

Neither idea explains the knobs, the hollow inside, or the holes that change from one object to the next. Maybe the answer lies in measuring.

Measuring the land and the sky

Some say they were tools for surveying, or for judging distances. The holes of different sizes would be peepholes for estimating distance. But this does not explain why each object has its own pattern of holes.

A Dutch scientist, G.M.C. Wagemans, had an idea from astronomy. He said they could help farmers pick the day to sow winter grain. This is appealing, but there is no solid proof.

Some think they were religious objects. Plato linked the shape to the cosmos. But no Roman or Celtic religious practice is known to use twelve-sided objects.

So these ideas are not proven. One more idea is much more surprising.

Were they for knitting?

The knitting idea was suggested by Martin Hallett. It says the dodecahedrons were used to knit gloves.

Yarn would be fed through a hole and wrapped around the knobs. The different hole sizes would fit different thicknesses of yarn. The different sizes of dodecahedron would make gloves of different sizes.

Hallett tried it with a 3D-printed copy. But no proof shows that Romans knitted gloves this way. Other experts remain doubtful.

A new find near Lincoln

The story is still growing. In 2023, volunteers digging near Lincoln, England, found a new dodecahedron in a pit. It is about 8 centimeters across. The University of Lincoln made a 3D scan of it. Tests are planned at Newcastle University.

The object is in excellent condition and shows no sign of wear. It is the first found in the English Midlands. New finds and new tests may add clues. But they have not yet solved the puzzle.

So what do we know? Someone made them, and someone used them. They were made by skilled metalworkers and spread across one part of the Roman Empire over about three centuries. Some were kept with coins and other valuable things, and some were buried in graves. We know about 130 of these objects exist, from about three centuries and several countries. We know exactly what they look like. We do not know what they were for.

Think again of that field in Aston in 1739. The scholars who held that bronze object had no answer. We still have none. The people who made them probably knew what the objects were for. Maybe it was never written down. For now, the best answer is a simple one: we do not know.
