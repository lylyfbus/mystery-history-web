Unknown Phenomena

Bigfoot: The Elusive Creature That Keeps Eyewitnesses Coming Forward

Bigfoot — the most famous and most debated cryptid on Earth, reportedly seen by thousands across North America

On October 20, 1967, two men rode their horses through the woods near Bluff Creek, California. Their names were Roger Patterson and Robert “Bob” Gimlin. Beside the creek, they saw something large and hairy crouching on the bank. Patterson jumped from his horse and grabbed his movie camera.

What he filmed that day is still argued about. Was it Bigfoot? To answer, we must start in the forest.

Deep in the old forests of the Pacific Northwest, the Douglas firs and western red cedars grow very tall. The valleys are misty and the slopes are steep. In some places, sunlight hardly reaches the ground. And something may be watching there.

For hundreds of years, perhaps thousands, people have reported meeting a huge, hairy creature. It walks upright on two legs like a human. But dark brown or black hair covers it, like an ape. Witnesses say it stands between six and nine feet tall. Some say it weighs hundreds of pounds.

It leaves enormous footprints. Some are said to be up to 24 inches (60 centimeters) long. It screams in the night with a voice like no other animal.

People say it has been seen in almost every state of the United States, and in most of Canada. A group called the Bigfoot Field Researchers Organization, or BFRO, keeps a long list of reported sightings. It counts thousands.

People have searched for decades. There are thousands of witness reports, casts of footprints, sound recordings, photographs, and one very famous film. Yet nobody has ever produced a body, a bone, or a DNA sample that passed the tests. Bigfoot is also called Sasquatch. It is one of the most famous and most argued-about cryptids. A cryptid is an animal that people report but science has not proved.

The home of the creature is the wide, rough wilderness of the Pacific Northwest. That means the thick, rainy forests of Northern California, Oregon, Washington State, and British Columbia. Some mountains there rise higher than 14,000 feet. Old forests cover millions of acres.

This land is so large and so far from towns that the idea is possible, at least in theory. A big, clever, shy animal could perhaps stay hidden from modern science. Black bears, cougars, elk, wolves, and other large animals live there. Huge areas have no roads and have never been fully explored.

But Bigfoot sightings do not come only from the Pacific Northwest. Reports come from the Appalachian Mountains in the eastern United States, the Ozarks, and the Rocky Mountains. In the swamps of Florida, the creature is called the Skunk Ape. The Ohio countryside has the Ohio Grassman.

In the pine woods of Arkansas, the Fouke Monster inspired the 1972 film The Legend of Boggy Creek.

The idea of a large, hairy, wild creature shaped like a human is much older than the name Bigfoot. Indigenous peoples across North America have told of such beings for centuries. Indigenous peoples are the first peoples of the land. Their stories began long before Europeans arrived.

The word “Sasquatch” was first written down in the 1920s by a Canadian writer named J. W. Burns. It is thought to come from the word “sásq’ets” in the Halkomelem language. The Coast Salish peoples of the Pacific Northwest use this word. Among them are the Sts’ailes (Chehalis) people of British Columbia, who have a long and deeply held belief in the creature.

For the Sts’ailes, Sasquatch is not a myth. It is a real, spiritual being and a guardian of the forest. People should respect it and stay away from it. Many other Indigenous nations across North America have similar traditions, each with its own names and stories. They usually describe creatures that are large, hairy, shy, and walk on two legs. That is close to the modern picture of Bigfoot.

The modern Bigfoot story began in 1958. It started at a lonely logging camp near Bluff Creek, California, in Del Norte County. Jerry Crew drove a bulldozer for the Wallace Brothers Construction Company. Near his work site, he found a line of enormous footprints in the mud. They were 16 inches (40 centimeters) long.

Crew made plaster casts of the footprints. A cast is a hard copy made by pouring plaster into the print. He took them to the Humboldt Times newspaper. A newspaper writer named Andrew Genzoli put the story on the front page on October 6, 1958. Many sources say he was the first to use the word “Bigfoot” in print.

News services picked up the story, and it spread quickly across the country.

One of the owners of the logging company was Ray Wallace. He became an important person in the Bigfoot story. In 2002, Wallace’s family said that Ray had made the Bluff Creek footprints himself. They said he used carved wooden feet. One researcher said the wooden feet did not match the plaster casts.

Doubters said this proved that the whole Bigfoot story was a trick. Bigfoot researchers did not agree. They accepted that the claim hurt some of the footprint evidence. But they said it could not explain thousands of other sightings and tracks across the continent, both before and after 1958.

Now back to Patterson and Gimlin in 1967. Patterson had rented a Cine-Kodak K-100 16mm movie camera. He filmed the creature as it stood up and walked away across a bar of sand. It looked back over its right shoulder for a moment. Then it walked into the trees.

The film is about 59.5 seconds long. It is shaky, but the shape is clear. It became the most studied piece of evidence in the search for Bigfoot. Frame 352 shows the creature in the middle of a step, with its head turned toward the camera. It is one of the best-known Bigfoot pictures.

Patterson and Gimlin guessed the creature was 6 to 7 feet (1.8-2.1 meters) tall. It has a strong, heavy body, and the filmmakers said it was female. Patterson died of a cancer called Hodgkin’s lymphoma in 1972. He said until the end that the film was real. Gimlin said for many years that he saw a real creature.

Decades of study have not ended the argument. Some people say the walk and the muscles show a real living body. They say no costume of 1967 could do that. Others say it is simply a person in a suit. Many experts doubt the film. Nobody has proved it true.

Long before Jerry Crew found his footprints, the forests of the Pacific Northwest already had strange stories. One of the best-known early stories came from 1924. It happened near Mount St. Helens, Washington.

The story says a group of miners was working in a rough canyon. They said they met large, hairy “ape-men.” One of the miners shot at the creatures. Then, they said, the creatures threw a rain of rocks at the miners’ cabin.

The miners said the “ape-men” were about seven feet tall. The story spread fast through nearby towns and into the newspapers. The place is now called “Ape Canyon.”

Doubters have other ideas. Forest Service rangers looked into the story and found nothing. They thought the miners may have thrown the stones themselves. Either way, the event became one of the first great legends of Bigfoot.

What makes Bigfoot different from many other cryptid legends? It is the great number of witnesses, and the kind of people they are. Not all of them want attention or like to play tricks. They include police officers, soldiers, park rangers, hunters, loggers, biologists, and ordinary families who were in the wrong place at the right time.

The BFRO list sorts sightings by state, by date, and by how much it trusts the witness. Many reports come from people who work outdoors. Many of them say that what they saw was not a bear and not a person in a costume. It was not any animal they could name.

Most reports describe a creature between 6 and 9 feet tall, covered in dark hair. It walks upright on two legs, in a way that does not look human. Some witnesses report a very strong, bad smell. They compare it to wet dog or rotting meat.

Many witnesses say they felt watched just before they saw it. Others say the forest went strangely silent. It was as if every bird and animal nearby had stopped making noise.

Most scientists think Bigfoot stories come from three things. People mistake other animals for it, people play tricks, and people pass on old stories. But a small number of trained researchers say the subject should be studied seriously.

One of the first was Dr. Grover Krantz (1931-2002). He was a professor at Washington State University who studied the human body and its history. Krantz spent decades looking at casts of Bigfoot footprints. Some people say that casts show dermal ridges. These are tiny lines on the skin of the foot, like fingerprints. He argued that the ridges fitted a real foot.

Krantz had an idea about what Bigfoot might be. He thought it could be a living group of Gigantopithecus blacki. That was a giant ape that lived in Asia until about 300,000 years ago and then died out. Critics pointed to a problem. No fossils of Gigantopithecus have ever been found in North America.

After Krantz, the best-known university researcher was Dr. Jeff Meldrum. He was a professor of anatomy and anthropology at Idaho State University. Anatomy is the study of how a body is built. In 2006 he wrote the book Sasquatch: Legend Meets Science. Meldrum examined hundreds of footprint casts. He argued that the way the feet seem to move fits a large ape that walks on two legs. Most scientists do not agree.

Perhaps the biggest surprise came from Jane Goodall, the most famous expert on apes in the world. In a 2002 interview on NPR radio, she said, “I’m sure that they exist.” Many scientists disagree.

Besides the Patterson-Gimlin film, a few pieces of physical evidence have kept the argument alive. There are hundreds of footprint casts. Some people say they show dermal ridges and other small details. Researchers like Krantz and Meldrum thought such details were hard to fake.

Then there is the Skookum Cast. A BFRO team found it in 2000 in the Gifford Pinchot National Forest in Washington State. It is a large print of a body in a patch of mud. Supporters say it shows the hip, thigh, and forearm of a large ape. Others doubt this.

There are also sound recordings said to be Bigfoot calls. People describe deep howls, whoops, and screams unlike any known animal. Some researchers say the sounds fit a large, unknown ape. Others say known animals can make them.

But the main things are missing. There is no proven DNA, no body, and no fossils. There is no type specimen, which is the first example that scientists use to describe a new animal. This gap keeps Bigfoot outside accepted science.

An honest talk about Bigfoot must also face the tricks. The history of Bigfoot is full of fakes made on purpose. They have confused the picture and given the doubters plenty to say.

A famous one was the Georgia Bigfoot trick of 2008. Two men, Matt Whitton and Rick Dyer, said they had found a dead Bigfoot in a forest in northern Georgia. They held a meeting for reporters, and a Bigfoot group paid them money for the “discovery.”

At last the “body” thawed and was examined. The hair was fake, the head was hollow, and the feet were rubber. The two men then admitted it was a hoax.

In 2014, Dyer tried again. He said he had shot a Bigfoot near San Antonio, Texas, in 2012. He traveled around North America showing a “body.” Later that year he admitted it was a costume.

There are more. There are Ray Wallace’s wooden feet. There is the “Minnesota Iceman” of 1968, a frozen “body” shown at fairs. One scientist said it was a rubber model. And there are more fake YouTube videos than anyone can count. It can be hard to tell an honest report from an invented one.

The tricks have hurt Bigfoot research badly. But they have not ended the story, because the sightings keep coming. Every year, new reports go to the BFRO and other groups.

The sightings have continued for decades. If Bigfoot were only a trick, some people say, the story should have died out long ago. But skeptics have another answer. A 2009 study found that Bigfoot reports match the places where black bears live. That suggests that many people may be seeing bears.

Today Bigfoot is more than a cryptid. It is a real symbol of American culture. The 1987 film Harry and the Hendersons showed Bigfoot as a gentle giant who goes to live with a family. The Animal Planet television series Finding Bigfoot began in 2011 and ran for several years.

The town of Willow Creek, California, calls itself the “Bigfoot Capital of the World.” Every year it holds a festival called Bigfoot Daze. The creature appears on T-shirts, beer labels, road signs, and restaurant menus across the Pacific Northwest.

So Bigfoot has become big business. Researchers notice how odd that is. They worry that all the selling makes people take the subject even less seriously. But the success also shows something deeper. People are hungry for mystery. They want to believe that the modern world, with its maps and satellite pictures, still holds secrets.

“Bigfoot” and “Sasquatch” are the best-known names. But the creature has many local names across North America. The Skunk Ape lives in the stories of Florida and the swamps of the southeast. It is said to be smaller than the Bigfoot of the Pacific Northwest, usually 5-7 feet tall. Reports say it has a very strong smell, like a skunk.

The Ohio Grassman has been reported in the Ohio countryside. It is said to travel in family groups. The Fouke Monster of Fouke, Arkansas, was reported in the early 1970s. Those reports led to the 1972 film The Legend of Boggy Creek, which mixed facts and acting.

Momo, the Missouri Monster, was reported in the town of Louisiana, Missouri, in 1972. Canada has its own kinds too, such as the Saskatchewan Wildman and “Old Yellow Top” in Ontario.

This suggests that Bigfoot is not one legend from one place. All across the continent, people tell of meeting something large, hairy, and unexplained in the wild.

In the end, the Bigfoot story is about the limits of what humans know. We have mapped the ocean floor. We have photographed the surface of Mars. We have read the full code of human DNA. Yet we cannot say for sure whether a large ape that walks on two legs lives in the forests of North America.

The evidence is exciting, but it is not enough. There are thousands of reports. There are hundreds of footprint casts. There is one film that people still argue about after more than fifty years. And there are a few objects like the Skookum Cast.

On the other side, there is no body, no fossil, and no proven DNA. That is the hard evidence that science demands. Nobody has ever produced it.

For science, the most likely answer is a mix. People mistake other animals for Bigfoot. People play tricks. People repeat old stories. And the human mind likes to see patterns that are not there.

But a small chance remains that something real is out there. That chance still pulls researchers into the forest, and it still brings new witnesses forward. The forests of the Pacific Northwest are huge and dark, and large parts are unexplored.

One day, someone may produce a body. Or someone may prove that Bigfoot is only a myth. Until then, the search will go on. People have always wanted to know what hides in the shadows. Is something really there?
