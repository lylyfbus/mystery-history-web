Unknown Phenomena

Bigfoot: The Elusive Creature That Keeps Eyewitnesses Coming Forward

Bigfoot — the most famous and most debated cryptid on Earth, reportedly seen by thousands across North America

On October 20, 1967, two men rode their horses through the woods near Bluff Creek, California. Their names were Roger Patterson and Robert “Bob” Gimlin. Beside the creek, they saw something large and hairy crouching on the bank. Patterson jumped from his horse and grabbed his movie camera.

What he filmed that day is still argued about. Was it Bigfoot? To answer, we must start in the forest.

Deep in the old forests of the Pacific Northwest, the Douglas firs and western red cedars grow very tall. The valleys are misty and the slopes are steep. In some places, sunlight hardly reaches the ground. And something may be watching there.

For hundreds of years, perhaps thousands, people have reported meeting a huge, hairy creature. It walks upright on two legs like a human. But dark brown or black hair covers it, like an ape. It is said to stand between six and nine feet tall. It may weigh 500 to 800 pounds or more.

It leaves enormous footprints. Some are up to 24 inches (60 centimeters) long. It screams in the night with a voice like no other animal.

People say it has been seen in every state of the continental United States except Hawaii, and in every Canadian province. A group called the Bigfoot Field Researchers Organization, or BFRO, keeps a list of more than 5,000 reported sightings.

People have searched for decades. There are thousands of witness reports, casts of footprints, sound recordings, photographs, and one very famous film. Yet nobody has ever produced a body, a bone, or a DNA sample that passed the tests. Bigfoot is also called Sasquatch. It is the most famous and most argued-about cryptid on Earth. A cryptid is an animal that people report but science has not proved.

The home of the creature is the wide, rough wilderness of the Pacific Northwest. That means the thick, rainy forests of Northern California, Oregon, Washington State, and British Columbia. Mountains there rise higher than 14,000 feet. Old forests cover millions of acres.

This land is so large and so far from towns that the idea is possible, at least in theory. A big, clever, shy animal could perhaps stay hidden from modern science. Black bears, cougars, elk, wolves, and other large animals live there. Huge areas have no roads and have never been fully explored.

But Bigfoot sightings do not come only from the Pacific Northwest. Reports come from the Appalachian Mountains in the eastern United States, the Ozarks, and the Rocky Mountains. In the swamps of Florida, the creature is called the Skunk Ape. The Ohio countryside has the Ohio Grassman.

In the pine woods of Arkansas, the Fouke Monster inspired the 1972 film The Legend of Boggy Creek.

The idea of a large, hairy, wild creature shaped like a human is much older than the name Bigfoot. Indigenous peoples across North America have told of such beings for centuries. Indigenous peoples are the first peoples of the land. Their stories began long before Europeans arrived.

The word “Sasquatch” comes from the word “sásq’ets” in the Halkomelem language. The Coast Salish peoples of the Pacific Northwest use this word. Among them are the Sts’ailes (Chehalis) people of British Columbia, who have a long and deeply held belief in the creature.

For the Sts’ailes, Sasquatch is not a myth. It is a real, spiritual being and a guardian of the forest. People should respect it and stay away from it. Many other Indigenous nations across North America have similar traditions, each with its own names and stories. They usually describe creatures that are large, hairy, shy, and walk on two legs. That is close to the modern picture of Bigfoot.

The modern Bigfoot story began in 1958. It started at a lonely logging camp near Bluff Creek, California, in Del Norte County. Jerry Crew drove a bulldozer for the Wallace Brothers Construction Company. Near his work site, he found a line of enormous footprints in the mud. They were 16 inches (40 centimeters) long.

Crew made plaster casts of the footprints. A cast is a hard copy made by pouring plaster into the print. He took them to the Humboldt Times newspaper. A journalist named Andrew Genzoli published the story on October 5, 1958. In it he used the word “Bigfoot.” It was the first time the name appeared in print.

News services picked up the story, and it spread quickly across the country.

The owner of the logging company was Ray Wallace. He became an important person in the Bigfoot story. Wallace died in 2002. Then his family said that Ray had made the Bluff Creek footprints himself. They said he used carved wooden feet tied to his boots.

Doubters said this proved that the whole Bigfoot story was a trick. Bigfoot researchers did not agree. They accepted that the claim hurt some of the footprint evidence. But they said it could not explain thousands of other sightings and tracks across the continent, both before and after 1958.

Now back to Patterson and Gimlin in 1967. Patterson had rented a Cine-Kodak K-100 16mm movie camera. He filmed the creature as it stood up and walked away across a bar of sand. It looked back over its right shoulder for a moment. Then it disappeared into the trees.

The film is about 59.5 seconds long. It is shaky, but the shape is clear. It became the most studied piece of evidence in the history of the search for unknown animals. Frame 352 shows the creature in the middle of a step, with its head turned toward the camera. It is one of the best-known pictures of the 20th century.

The creature in the film seems to be 6 to 7 feet (1.8-2.1 meters) tall. It has a strong, heavy body and appears to be female. Patterson died of a cancer called Hodgkin’s lymphoma in 1972. He never said the film was fake. Gimlin always said the film was real.

Decades of study have not ended the argument. Some experts say the walk and the muscles show a real living body. They say no costume of 1967 could do that. Others say it is simply a person in a well-made suit. Nobody has proved the film false. Nobody has proved it true.

Long before Jerry Crew found his footprints, the forests of the Pacific Northwest already had strange stories. One of the earliest and most dramatic came from 1924. It happened near Mount St. Helens, Washington.

The story says a group of miners was working in a rough canyon. They met several large, hairy “ape-men.” One of the miners shot at the creatures. Then, all night long, the creatures threw a rain of rocks at the miners’ cabin.

The miners said the “ape-men” were enormous. They said the rocks were too heavy for a human to throw. At dawn the miners ran from the canyon. The story spread fast through nearby towns and into the newspapers. The place was later named “Ape Canyon,” and it still has that name today.

Doubters have other ideas. Maybe the miners met a group of men who lived alone in the wild. Maybe they simply made a story bigger. Either way, the event became one of the first great legends of Bigfoot.

What makes Bigfoot different from many other cryptid legends? It is the great number of witnesses, and the kind of people they are. They are not all people who want attention or like to play tricks. They include police officers, soldiers, park rangers, hunters, loggers, biologists, and ordinary families who were in the wrong place at the right time.

The BFRO list sorts sightings by state, by date, and by how much the witness can be trusted. Many reports come from people who work outdoors. They know what bears look like, and they know the local animals. They insist that what they saw was not a bear and not a person in a costume. It was not any animal they could name.

Most reports describe a creature between 6 and 9 feet tall, covered in dark hair. It walks upright on two legs, in a way that does not look human. It has a very strong, bad smell. People compare the smell to wet dog, rotting meat, and sulfur together.

Many witnesses say they felt watched just before they saw it. Others say the forest went strangely silent. It was as if every bird and animal nearby had stopped making noise.

Most scientists think Bigfoot is a mix of three things. People mistake other animals for it, people play tricks, and people pass on old stories. But a small number of trained researchers say the subject should be studied seriously.

One of the first was Dr. Grover Krantz (1931-2002). He was a professor at Washington State University who studied the human body and its history. Krantz spent decades looking at casts of Bigfoot footprints. Some casts show dermal ridges. These are tiny lines on the skin of the foot, like fingerprints. He argued that the ridges fitted a real foot and could not have been faked.

Krantz had an idea about what Bigfoot might be. He thought it could be a living group of Gigantopithecus blacki. That was a giant ape that lived in Asia until about 100,000 years ago and then died out. Critics pointed to a problem. No fossils of Gigantopithecus have ever been found in North America.

After Krantz, the best-known university researcher was Dr. Jeff Meldrum. He was a professor of anatomy and anthropology at Idaho State University. Anatomy is the study of how a body is built. In 2006 he wrote the book Sasquatch: Legend Meets Science. Meldrum examined hundreds of footprint casts. He argued that the way the feet seem to move fits a real, large ape that walks on two legs.

Perhaps the biggest surprise came from Jane Goodall, the most famous expert on apes in the world. In a 2002 interview on NPR radio, she said she was “open-minded” about Bigfoot. She said she had met “people whom I respect who have had encounters” that they could not explain.

Besides the Patterson-Gimlin film, a few pieces of physical evidence have kept the argument alive. There are hundreds of footprint casts. Some show dermal ridges and other small details. Researchers like Krantz and Meldrum say these are impossible to fake.

Then there is the Skookum Cast. A BFRO team found it in 2000 in the Gifford Pinchot National Forest in Washington State. It is a large print of a body in a patch of mud. It seems to show the hip, thigh, and forearm of a large ape, and marks of hair can be seen in the cast.

There are also sound recordings said to be Bigfoot calls. People describe deep howls, whoops, and screams unlike any known animal. Some researchers say the sounds fit a large, unknown ape.

But the main things are missing. There is no proven DNA, no body, and no fossils. There is no type specimen, which is the first example that scientists use to describe a new animal. This gap keeps Bigfoot outside accepted science.

An honest talk about Bigfoot must also face the tricks. The history of Bigfoot is full of fakes made on purpose. They have confused the picture and given the doubters plenty to say.

The worst was the Georgia Bigfoot trick of 2008. Two men, Matt Whitton and Rick Dyer, said they had found a Bigfoot body. They said it was in a freezer near the Appalachian town of Clayton, Georgia. They held a meeting for reporters. They showed photographs of a dark, hairy shape pushed into a freezer, and they demanded money for the “discovery.”

At last the “body” was examined. It was a rubber Bigfoot costume filled with animal parts.

Dyer tried again in 2012. He said he had shot and killed a Bigfoot near San Antonio, Texas. He traveled around the country showing a “body.” Later he admitted it was a costume.

There are more. There are Ray Wallace’s wooden feet. There is the “Minnesota Iceman” of the 1960s, a carnival show that was almost certainly a rubber model. And there are more fake YouTube videos than anyone can count. It is now very hard to tell an honest report from an invented one.

The tricks have hurt Bigfoot research badly. But they have not ended the story, because the sightings keep coming. Every year, hundreds of new reports go to the BFRO and other groups. Many come from trusted witnesses who have nothing to gain and much to lose by speaking.

The sightings continue across decades, across continents, and across cultures. This is one of the most puzzling parts of the whole subject. If Bigfoot were only a trick, the story should have died out decades ago. If people were only seeing bears, most reports should come from places with many bears. Reports from other places should be rare.

That is not what happens. Sightings come from every kind of land. They come from people with every level of outdoor skill, and from every kind of person.

Today Bigfoot is more than a cryptid. It is a real symbol of American culture. The 1987 film Harry and the Hendersons showed Bigfoot as a gentle giant who goes to live with a family. The Animal Planet television series Finding Bigfoot (2011-2017) ran for nine seasons.

Bigfoot is the official state cryptid of Washington State. The town of Willow Creek, California, calls itself the “Bigfoot Capital of the World.” Every year it holds a festival called Bigfoot Daze. The creature appears on T-shirts, beer labels, road signs, and restaurant menus across the Pacific Northwest.

So Bigfoot has become big business. Researchers notice how odd that is. They worry that all the selling makes people take the subject even less seriously. But the success also shows something deeper. People are hungry for mystery. They want to believe that the modern world, with its maps and satellite pictures, still holds secrets.

“Bigfoot” and “Sasquatch” are the best-known names. But the creature has many local names across North America. The Skunk Ape lives in the stories of Florida and the swamps of the southeast. It is said to be smaller than the Bigfoot of the Pacific Northwest, usually 5-7 feet tall. It has a very strong smell, like rotten eggs or a skunk.

The Ohio Grassman has been reported in the Ohio countryside since the 1800s. It is said to travel in family groups. The Fouke Monster of Fouke, Arkansas, was seen many times in the 1970s. Those reports led to the 1972 film The Legend of Boggy Creek, which mixed facts and acting and had a strong effect on later films.

Momo, the Missouri Monster, was reported in the town of Louisiana, Missouri, in 1972. Canada has its own kinds too, such as the Saskatchewan Wildman and “Old Yellow Top” in Ontario.

This suggests that Bigfoot is not one legend from one place. All across the continent, people tell of meeting something large, hairy, and unexplained in the wild.

In the end, the Bigfoot story is about the limits of what humans know. We have mapped the ocean floor. We have photographed the surface of Mars. We have read the full code of human DNA. Yet we cannot say for sure whether a large ape that walks on two legs lives in the forests of North America.

The evidence is exciting, but it is not enough. There are thousands of reports from trusted witnesses. There are hundreds of footprint casts with details that may be real. There is one film that nobody has proved false in more than fifty years. And there are a few objects like the Skookum Cast.

On the other side, there is no body, no fossil, and no proven DNA. That is the hard evidence that science demands. Nobody has ever produced it.

For science, the most likely answer is a mix. People mistake other animals for Bigfoot. People play tricks. People repeat old stories. And the human mind likes to see patterns that are not there.

But a small chance remains that something real is out there. That chance still pulls researchers into the forest, and it still brings new witnesses forward. The forests of the Pacific Northwest are huge and dark, and large parts are unexplored.

One day, someone may produce a body. Or someone may prove that Bigfoot is only a myth. Until then, the search will go on. People have always wanted to know what hides in the shadows. Is something really there?
