The Great Molasses Flood: The Day a Deadly Wave of Syrup Destroyed Boston

It was January 15, 1919, at about 12:30 in the afternoon. People and workers in Boston’s North End heard a low, grinding rumble. Many thought it was a train on the Boston Elevated Railway. A few seconds later the sound grew into a deafening roar.

Then a wall of dark, thick liquid burst from a huge storage tank at 529 Commercial Street. It was molasses, a sticky brown syrup. There were 2.3 million gallons of it, weighing about 13,000 tons. It rushed through the crowded neighborhood at an estimated 35 miles per hour.

At its peak the wave was 25 feet high and 160 feet wide. It was like a tsunami made of syrup. It smashed buildings and overturned horse-drawn wagons and motor vehicles. It crushed railway freight cars. It trapped men, women, and children in the thick, sticky mass.

When the molasses finally stopped moving, 21 people were dead and 150 more were injured. Whole buildings had been swept off their foundations. The firehouse of Engine 31 was wrecked, and several firefighters were trapped under tons of brick and timber. A section of the Boston Elevated Railway tracks had collapsed. Horses, wagons, dogs, and people were stuck in the thick brown liquid. Some were alive and struggling. Others could not be saved.

This was not an act of God, an earthquake, or an attack. It was a failure of engineering. The tank should never have been built the way it was. It was overseen by a man with no engineering training. And it was filled to the top without ever being properly tested.

The disaster happened at a plant of the Purity Distilling Company. This company belonged to the United States Industrial Alcohol Company, or USIA. The plant stood on Commercial Street near Keany Square in the North End of Boston.

The North End was, and still is, one of the oldest neighborhoods in Boston. It was crowded, with narrow streets, tenement buildings, and small shops. Wave after wave of immigrants had made their homes there. In 1919 most of the people were Italian-American. They were working families who lived in the shadow of the industrial waterfront.

The company had built its huge tank on the waterfront in 1915. World War I was at its height, and the demand for industrial alcohol was enormous. This alcohol was made by fermenting molasses. It was used in drinks, and, very importantly, to make weapons and explosives for the war.

The tank was a business tool in a time of war. It was built in a hurry. The company watched over it carelessly and ran it recklessly. The neighborhood around it was treated as an afterthought. Then the tank failed, and the neighborhood paid the price.

The story begins with Arthur P. Jell. He was the treasurer of the Purity Distilling Company, and he oversaw the building of the tank in 1915. Jell was a businessman, not an engineer. He had no training in how to design strong structures. He had no skill in tank design and no experience in industrial building.

Even so, he was put in charge of one of the largest liquid storage tanks in Boston. It stood 50 feet tall and was 90 feet wide. It could hold 2.3 million gallons. The Hugh Nawn Construction Company built it on a concrete base three feet thick. The Hammond Iron Works put up the steel walls. The work was finished in 1915. Within two days, the first load of molasses was being pumped in.

From the very start, the tank had problems. Soon after it was filled, neighbors saw molasses seeping out from the seams and rivets, the metal pins that hold steel plates together. The leaks were so steady that children from the North End came to the base of the tank with pails and buckets. They collected the free molasses that oozed from the joints.

Residents complained about the leaks. The company did not repair the tank. It did not check whether the rivets were strong enough. Instead, it painted the tank brown, so that the leaking molasses would be harder to see. This was a cynical answer, and it showed how the company thought about safety.

The tank was never given a hydrostatic test. That is the usual engineering step of filling a new tank with water first, to see whether it is strong enough. No independent engineer inspected it. Nobody did the math to check that the steel walls and rivets could bear the huge weight of the molasses.

Experts studied the case again many decades later. They confirmed what should have been clear at the time. The steel walls were too thin. The rivets were not strong enough. The design could not handle the loads it was meant to carry.

The demand for molasses from 1915 to 1919 was driven by World War I. Molasses can be fermented to make ethanol, a kind of alcohol. Ethanol was a key ingredient for making explosives, including TNT and smokeless powder. USIA was a major supplier of industrial alcohol for the war. When the United States entered the war in 1917, the demand rose even more.

The molasses in the North End tank came by ship from Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the West Indies. It was stored in the tank. Then it went by rail to distilleries, where it was turned into alcohol. The profits from wartime molasses were huge. That gave the company a strong reason to cut corners on safety and construction. The choice had deadly results.

There is a sad twist. The war had ended two months before the disaster, on November 11, 1918. But the tank was still full. The company was stockpiling molasses because it expected Prohibition. This was a ban on alcoholic drinks, due to start in January 1920. The company thought it would push up the price of industrial alcohol even further.

The weather on January 15, 1919, played a big part. The week before had been bitterly cold, with temperatures well below freezing. On the 15th, the temperature jumped to an unusually warm 40 degrees Fahrenheit. That was a swing of more than 40 degrees.

This sudden change affected the molasses in two ways. First, the old molasses had been cold and thick. Now it began to warm up and expand. Second, a new shipment of warm molasses had just come from a tanker ship. It had been pumped into the tank, which raised the heat and the volume even more.

The heat and the extra volume put huge stress on the already weak walls and rivets. At about 12:30 PM, the tank gave way. Witnesses said they heard a sound like machine-gun fire, or like cloth being ripped. It was the sound of rivets popping out of the steel plates, one after another, as the walls broke apart.

The collapse was terrible and almost instant. The tank burst and let out its 2.3 million gallons of molasses. The wave was about 25 feet high and 160 feet wide, and it raced along at 35 miles per hour.

The nature of molasses made the wave extra dangerous. Molasses is about 1.4 times heavier than water, and it is far thicker. So a wave of molasses has much more crushing force than a water wave of the same size. It also does not drain away. It surrounds and holds everything in its path.

The wave destroyed the Engine 31 firehouse and trapped several firefighters inside. It hit the Boston Elevated Railway tracks and brought down a section of the raised structure. It swept away horse-drawn wagons, motor trucks, and cars, and carried them blocks from where they began. It flooded homes and shops to a depth of two to three feet. People were trapped in their kitchens and living rooms.

Those caught in the wave could hardly move in the thick, sticky liquid. People who fell often could not get up again. Many died in the molasses, which is a strange and terrible way to die.

The 21 victims ranged in age from a 10-year-old schoolboy to a 76-year-old man. Many were Italian immigrants who lived in the crowded tenements near the tank. Among the dead were longshoremen and warehouse workers who had been eating lunch near the waterfront. There was also a firefighter from Engine 31, who died when the firehouse fell. And there was a young girl who had been walking home from school.

Some victims were killed at once by the force of the wave or by falling buildings. Others were trapped in the molasses and could not breathe. The 150 survivors who were hurt had broken bones, crush injuries, and chills from lying in the cold molasses. Some also had infections from wounds dirtied by the sticky liquid.

Many people came to help. The Boston Police Department, the U.S. Navy, the Red Cross, and hundreds of volunteers waded into the molasses to pull survivors from the wreckage. The rescue and recovery work went on for several days. Workers searched collapsed buildings and drained molasses from basements and cellars.

Right after the disaster, USIA began a loud public campaign to avoid the blame. The company said that anarchists had bombed the tank. Anarchists are people who want to destroy governments. In 1919 that did not sound impossible, because a series of anarchist bombings had shaken American cities.

Many American-born people also looked at the Italian immigrants of the North End with suspicion. The anarchist story played on this unfair prejudice. The company pointed to recent bomb threats against factories. It said that no properly built tank could have failed on its own. The plan was to turn the story from the company’s carelessness to a foreign attack. This used the fear of foreigners that was common then, and it was meant to avoid the blame.

The victims’ families did not believe the anarchist story. They brought three separate lawsuits against USIA. These were later joined into one huge court case. At that time it was the longest trial in the history of Massachusetts.

The court chose Hugh W. Ogden to look into the disaster. He was a respected lawyer and former judge, named as a special auditor. Ogden worked slowly and carefully from 1919 to 1925. He examined the wreckage of the tank. He questioned witnesses and engineers. He read the building records. He also ordered independent studies of the tank’s strength.

His report, issued in 1925, was harsh. Ogden found that the tank had been poorly designed, badly built, and never tested. The steel walls were too thin, well below the thickness that engineering rules required for a tank that size. There were not enough rivets, and they were not good enough. Arthur Jell, who oversaw the building, had no engineering training. The tank had never had a hydrostatic test. The company had ignored the constant leaks and had chosen to paint the tank brown instead of fixing it.

Ogden’s conclusion was clear. The disaster was caused by the company’s carelessness. It was not caused by anarchists or saboteurs.

The flood changed engineering rules in the United States for good. Before 1919, most cities had almost no rules for how to build and inspect large industrial storage tanks. Owners built them as they wished. Nobody had to ask an independent engineer to review them. Nobody had to test them for safety or inspect them later.

The disaster showed how deadly that freedom could be. After the flood and the Ogden report, Massachusetts and other states passed laws. These laws required engineers to certify large storage tanks and industrial buildings, and to inspect them often. The American Society of Civil Engineers, or ASCE, also updated its standards for tank design and building. It now required careful strength calculations, tests of materials, and hydrostatic testing before a tank could be used.

These standards trace their roots straight back to the molasses flood. They are still in use today. They govern everything from water towers to tanks of chemicals across the country.

The families of the victims received payments of about $7,000 for each victim. That is equal to about $115,000 in 2024 dollars. By today’s standards it was not a lot of money. But it was an important admission that the company was responsible.

One of the longest-lived legends about the flood is about a smell. It is said that on hot summer days the North End of Boston still smells of molasses. According to local folklore, molasses soaked into the soil, the cobblestones, and the foundations of buildings over the years. The smell, they say, comes back when the summer heat warms the ground.

For decades after the disaster, residents said they could smell molasses. The story has been told in countless articles, books, and documentaries. Is the smell real, or is it only a shared memory and the power of suggestion? That is hard to say. The area has been rebuilt many times since 1919. The original buildings, streets, and soil have long since been replaced.

The site of the tank is now Langone Park and Puopolo Sports Field. It is a small waterfront park with a memorial plaque for the disaster. But the legend lives on. It shows how even strange, absurd disasters leave marks on the communities they strike.

The Great Boston Molasses Flood of 1919 is often remembered as a curiosity. It seems like a bizarre footnote in history, so strange that it sounds like an urban legend. But it was real, and it was deadly. Twenty-one people lost their lives because a company cut corners on construction, ignored warning signs, and put profits before safety.

The disaster was not an act of God or an accident nobody could predict. It was the result of carelessness and greed, and it could have been foreseen. The legal fight that followed lasted six years. It ended with Hugh Ogden’s landmark report. The report set important examples for company responsibility and for engineering rules. We still live with them today.

The flood taught America that industrial buildings must be designed by qualified engineers, tested before use, and inspected regularly. These lessons seem obvious now. But they were not normal practice before January 15, 1919.

The victims were Italian immigrants, firefighters, longshoremen, and children walking home from school. They deserve to be remembered as people who suffered a tragedy that could have been prevented. They are not characters in a funny story. The sweet smell of molasses, said to linger more than a century later, is not a joke. It is a warning. And people in Boston still wonder whether that smell is real.
