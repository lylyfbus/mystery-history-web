Mothman: The Winged Creature That Terrorized a West Virginia Town for 13 Months

It was the evening of November 15, 1966. Two young couples were driving through an old TNT plant outside Point Pleasant, West Virginia. This small town sits on the banks of the Ohio River. The friends were Roger and Linda Scarberry and Steve and Mary Mallette.

They were not looking for monsters. They were just cruising around, as young people in country towns did on a Tuesday night. Then they saw something standing in the shadows near the old bunkers where explosives had once been kept.

It was seven feet tall. It had wings folded against its back. And its eyes were large, hypnotic, and glowing an impossible red. They stared straight at the four friends.

The creature chased their car as they fled. They said it kept up with them at more than 100 miles per hour. It did not run. It glided. When they reached the Point Pleasant city limits, it turned and vanished into the dark.

The four shaken witnesses told the local sheriff. By the next morning the story was on the front page of the Point Pleasant Register. The headline read: “Couples See Man-Sized Bird... Creature... Something.”

Over the next thirteen months, many people in the Point Pleasant area said they saw the same impossible being. Some accounts say more than one hundred people did. Then, on December 15, 1967, the Silver Bridge fell into the Ohio River. Forty-six people died. The sightings stopped. Whatever the Mothman was, or whatever the people of Point Pleasant believed it to be, it left behind one of the strangest chapters in American folklore.

The Point Pleasant sighting was not, strictly speaking, the first. On November 12, 1966, three days before the Scarberry-Mallette meeting, some gravediggers had a strange experience. Five men were digging a grave in a cemetery in Clendenin, West Virginia, about fifty miles from Point Pleasant. One of them, Kenneth Duncan, said a human-like figure glided through the trees over their heads. He told his story only after the Point Pleasant news came out. Some people think he may have seen a startled flying squirrel.

The November 15 sighting was different. It exploded into the news. The four friends told a deputy sheriff what they had seen. Accounts differ on its color. Some say gray and some say black. They said it stood six to seven feet tall. Its wingspan was about ten feet.

The most disturbing thing was its eyes. They were large and round, and they gave off a hypnotic red glow. Witnesses said it felt as if the creature was looking through them, not at them.

In the weeks that followed, sightings multiplied. Another report came from a woman named Marcella Bennett. She said she was visiting friends near the TNT plant. She told of a large gray creature that rose from the ground and moved toward her. She said she was so scared that she dropped her baby daughter.

Other people said they saw a creature with glowing red eyes near their homes or their cars.

By the end of November, newspaper and TV reporters from around the country had come to Point Pleasant. The name “Mothman” had entered the American language. Newspapers began to use it. It was probably inspired by the villain Killer Moth from the popular Batman comics and TV show.

The West Virginia Ordnance Works was known locally as the “TNT plant.” It was a huge factory from World War II near Point Pleasant. During the war it made trinitrotoluene, or TNT, an explosive, for the army. After the war the plant was closed and mostly abandoned.

It left behind a huge group of concrete igloos. These were bunkers covered with earth, once used to store explosives. Forest and wet land surrounded them. The place was dark, lonely, and eerie even before anyone saw a monster there. It became the center of the Mothman legend, and most of the earliest sightings happened in or near it.

The Mothman sightings did not stand alone. During those same thirteen months, Point Pleasant and the nearby region had many other strange events. There were UFO sightings, visits from mysterious government agents, odd phone calls, and electrical problems. There were also reports of a strange being called Indrid Cold, the “Grinning Man.”

On November 2, 1966, two weeks before the first big Mothman sighting, an appliance salesman named Woody Derenberger said he was driving on Interstate 77 near Parkersburg, West Virginia. He said a strange vehicle pulled up beside his car. It was dark and unusual in design.

Derenberger said the driver stepped out. He described a tall man with a wide, fixed grin. The man said his name was Indrid Cold. Derenberger said they spoke without words, mind to mind. No one else saw this, so it remains one man’s story.

Derenberger’s story caught the attention of John A. Keel. Keel was a journalist and paranormal investigator from New York. He had been studying UFO sightings and strange events since the early 1960s. He became the best-known writer on the Point Pleasant events. He visited the town again and again between 1966 and 1967 and talked with many witnesses.

Keel wrote down more than the Mothman sightings. He recorded a whole world of weirdness. There were lights in the sky. There were phone calls from unknown numbers with strange electronic sounds. Some visitors said they were government agents and told witnesses to stop talking. And a heavy feeling of dread settled over the town like fog.

Keel wrote that several witnesses were visited by people who claimed to be government agents. He described their behavior as odd. They wore badly fitting suits and spoke in stiff, robot-like language. Witnesses said they knew details that no one had been told.

These visits were called “Men in Black” visits. Similar stories appear in other UFO cases of the 1950s and 1960s. So Keel argued that the Mothman, the UFOs, and the Men in Black were all signs of the same thing.

Keel’s 1975 book was called The Mothman Prophecies. It argued that the events in Point Pleasant were not random. He saw them as part of a bigger pattern of paranormal activity, which means events that science cannot explain. He called Point Pleasant a “window area,” a place where the wall between dimensions had grown thin.

Many people at the time thought UFOs were spaceships from other planets. Keel did not. He came up with the “ultraterrestrial hypothesis.” This is the idea that the beings behind UFOs, strange creatures, and other odd events are not visitors from other planets. He thought they were beings from other dimensions who had always lived alongside humans. They showed up in different forms at different times. They were fairies in medieval Europe, airships in the 1890s, flying saucers in the 1940s, and Mothman in the 1960s.

You may or may not accept Keel’s theories. But his book is still the best-known record of the Mothman story. Keel wrote it from the point of view of a believer, and many parts rest on witness stories alone.

On December 15, 1967, at about 5:00 p.m., it was the evening rush hour. The Silver Bridge carried U.S. Route 35 over the Ohio River. It linked Point Pleasant, West Virginia, with Gallipolis, Ohio. Without warning, the bridge collapsed.

The bridge was full of cars and trucks. In seconds the whole structure fell into the freezing river. Forty-six people died. Sources give slightly different counts, but about thirty-one or thirty-two vehicles fell into the river. Two of the victims were never found.

The National Transportation Safety Board, or NTSB, investigated. It found that the collapse began with a crack in a single part. This was the lower part of the eye of eyebar 330. An eyebar is a flat metal bar with a round hole, or “eye,” at each end.

The Silver Bridge was built in 1928. Its suspension chains were made of eyebars joined by pins through the eyes. The design was common in its day. But it had little backup. If one eyebar broke, the whole chain would come apart.

The NTSB found that the crack was caused by stress corrosion cracking and corrosion fatigue. This is the slow, hidden growth of a crack in metal that is stressed again and again over many years. It could not be found with the inspection tools of the time. When it finally gave way, the result was a catastrophe.

The Mothman sightings stopped after the bridge fell. Some people believed the creature was a harbinger of disaster, a warning of death. For them, the timing was a sign. Keel’s book linked the sightings to the collapse in just this way.

Skeptics, who ask for proof before they believe, saw a flaw in this logic. People had been seeing the Mothman for thirteen months. They lived in a small town where almost every landmark was within walking distance of the bridge. So any pattern could be found afterward. Still, the link between the Mothman and the Silver Bridge had already become folklore. It was now part of the legend.

The most common skeptical explanation is that witnesses saw sandhill cranes. This idea came from Robert Smith, a wildlife scientist at West Virginia University. He said a sandhill crane may have wandered off its usual path. These are large birds. They stand about four feet tall and have wingspans of about six or seven feet. They also have red skin on the face.

Some people have also suggested large owls, whose eyes can shine in car headlights. Or it might have been a mix of mistakes and mass suggestion. The heavy news coverage and the isolation of the area probably made the effect stronger. Witnesses saw unclear shapes and understood them in light of the growing legend.

The idea of a strange creature that appears before a disaster is not unique to Point Pleasant. In Irish folklore, the bean-sidhe, or banshee, is said to wail before a death in the family.

Other cases are much like the Mothman. The Flatwoods Monster of West Virginia (1952) and the Owlman of Cornwall, England (1976) have much in common with it. They had human-like shapes and glowing eyes, and they appeared near small communities. Do these likenesses point to a real phenomenon found in many cultures? Or do they just show that people everywhere link the unknown with bad omens? That is a matter of opinion.

Today the Mothman is no longer just a mystery. It is a business. Point Pleasant, with about 4,000 people, has built a whole tourist trade around a creature that may or may not have existed. The Mothman Museum opened in 2006. A twelve-foot metal statue of the creature stands in town. The Mothman Festival is held every September. Its organizer says it brings 10,000 to 12,000 visitors. The 2002 film The Mothman Prophecies, starring Richard Gere, introduced the legend to the world.

But behind the souvenirs and the movies lies a real tragedy. Forty-six people died when the Silver Bridge collapsed. Their deaths have been overshadowed by the myth of a monster.

The people of Point Pleasant lived through thirteen months of real fear and then a real disaster. They deserve to be remembered as more than characters in a ghost story. Their fear was real. Their loss was real.

And what flew through the West Virginia night in 1966? Was it a crane, an owl, or something else entirely? Nobody knows. The shadow it cast over that small river town has never fully lifted.
