Mothman: The Winged Creature That Terrorized a West Virginia Town for 13 Months

It was the evening of November 15, 1966. Two young couples were driving through an old TNT plant outside Point Pleasant, West Virginia. This small town sits on the banks of the Ohio River. The friends were Roger and Linda Scarberry and Steve and Mary Mallette.

They were not looking for monsters. They were just cruising around, as young people in country towns did on a Tuesday night. Then they saw something standing in the shadows near the old bunkers where explosives had once been kept.

It was seven feet tall. It had wings folded against its back. And its eyes were large, hypnotic, and glowing an impossible red. They stared straight at the four friends.

The creature chased their car as they fled. They said it kept up with them at more than 100 miles per hour. It did not run. It glided. When they reached the Point Pleasant city limits, it turned and vanished into the dark.

The four shaken witnesses told the local sheriff. By the next morning the story was on the front page of the Point Pleasant Register. The headline read: “Couples See Man-Sized Bird... Creature... Something.”

Over the next thirteen months, more than one hundred people in the Point Pleasant area said they saw the same impossible being. Then, on December 15, 1967, the Silver Bridge fell into the Ohio River. Forty-six people died. The sightings stopped. Whatever the Mothman was, or whatever the people of Point Pleasant believed it to be, it left behind one of the strangest chapters in American folklore.

The first Mothman sighting was not, strictly speaking, the first. On November 12, 1966, three days before the Scarberry-Mallette meeting, some gravediggers had a strange experience. They were working in a cemetery in Clendenin, West Virginia, about sixty miles from Point Pleasant. They said a huge figure flew over their heads. They called it a “brown human being” that moved from tree to tree. This report got little notice.

The November 15 sighting was different. It exploded into the news. The four friends told Deputy Sheriff Millard Halstead what they had seen. The creature had grayish-brown skin. It stood between six and seven feet tall. Its wingspan was about ten feet.

The most disturbing thing was its eyes. They were large and round, and they gave off a hypnotic red glow. Witnesses said it felt as if the creature was looking through them, not at them.

In the weeks that followed, sightings multiplied. On November 16, 1966, just one day after the first report, a woman named Marcella Bennett was visiting friends near the TNT plant. She saw a large gray creature rise from the ground and glide toward her. She was so scared that she dropped her baby daughter.

On November 25, a young boy said he saw a creature with glowing red eyes outside his home. On November 27, a woman driving near the TNT plant said a winged creature followed her car for miles.

By the end of November, newspaper and TV reporters from around the country had come to Point Pleasant. The name “Mothman” had entered the American language. It was apparently invented by a newspaper copy editor.

The West Virginia Ordnance Works was known locally as the “TNT plant.” It was a factory from World War II that covered over 8,000 acres near Point Pleasant. During the war it made trinitrotoluene, or TNT, an explosive, for the army. After the war the plant was closed and mostly abandoned.

It left behind a huge group of concrete igloos. These were bunkers covered with earth, once used to store explosives. Dense forest, marshland, and poisoned groundwater surrounded them. The place was dark, lonely, and eerie even before anyone saw a monster there. It became the center of the Mothman legend, and most of the earliest sightings happened in or near it.

The Mothman sightings did not stand alone. During those same thirteen months, Point Pleasant and the nearby region had many other strange events. There were UFO sightings, visits from mysterious government agents, odd phone calls, and electrical problems. There were also reports of a strange being called Indrid Cold, the “Grinning Man.”

On November 2, 1966, two weeks before the first big Mothman sighting, an appliance salesman named Woody Derenberger was driving on Interstate 77 near Parkersburg, West Virginia. A strange vehicle pulled up beside his car. It was dark and unusual in design.

Its driver stepped out. He was a tall man with dark skin and a wide, fixed grin. He said his name was Indrid Cold. He spoke to Derenberger without words, mind to mind, and warned him about a coming disaster.

Derenberger’s story caught the attention of John A. Keel. Keel was a journalist and paranormal investigator from New York. He had been studying UFO sightings and strange events since the early 1960s. He became the main recorder of the Point Pleasant events. He visited the town again and again between 1966 and 1967 and did over one hundred interviews with witnesses.

Keel wrote down more than the Mothman sightings. He recorded a whole world of weirdness. There were lights in the sky. There were phone calls from unknown numbers with strange electronic sounds. Some visitors said they were government agents and told witnesses to stop talking. And a heavy feeling of dread settled over the town like fog.

Several witnesses said they were visited by people who claimed to be government agents. Their behavior was, to put it mildly, unusual. They had olive-colored skin and badly fitting suits. They spoke in stiff, robot-like language. They also knew details that witnesses had told no one.

These visits were called “Men in Black” visits. They matched similar reports from UFO cases of the 1940s to the 1960s. So Keel argued that the Mothman, the UFOs, and the Men in Black were all signs of the same thing.

Keel’s 1975 book was called The Mothman Prophecies. It argued that the events in Point Pleasant were not random. He saw them as part of a bigger pattern of paranormal activity, which means events that science cannot explain. He called Point Pleasant a “window area,” a place where the wall between dimensions had grown thin.

Many people at the time thought UFOs were spaceships from other planets. Keel did not. He came up with the “ultraterrestrial hypothesis.” This is the idea that the beings behind UFOs, strange creatures, and other odd events are not visitors from other planets. He thought they were beings from other dimensions who had always lived alongside humans. They showed up in different forms at different times. They were fairies in medieval Europe, airships in the 1890s, flying saucers in the 1940s, and Mothman in the 1960s.

You may or may not accept Keel’s theories. But his record of the Point Pleasant events is still the most thorough record of the Mothman story.

On December 15, 1967, at about 5:00 p.m., it was the evening rush hour. The Silver Bridge carried U.S. Route 35 over the Ohio River. It linked Point Pleasant, West Virginia, with Kanauga, Ohio. Without warning, the bridge collapsed.

The bridge was full of cars and trucks. In seconds the whole structure fell into the freezing river. Forty-six people died, and nine were injured. Thirty-seven vehicles were on the bridge, and thirty-one of them fell. Twenty-four fell into the water, and seven landed on the Ohio shore. Many of the victims were never recovered.

The National Transportation Safety Board, or NTSB, investigated. It found that the collapse began with a crack in a single part. This was the lower part of the eye of eyebar 330. An eyebar is a flat metal bar with a round hole, or “eye,” at each end.

The Silver Bridge was built in 1928. Its suspension chains were made of eyebars joined by pins through the eyes. The design was strong. But it had no backup. If one eyebar broke, the whole chain would come apart.

The NTSB found that the crack was caused by stress corrosion cracking and corrosion fatigue. This is the slow, hidden growth of a crack in metal that is stressed again and again over many years. It could not be found with the inspection tools of 1928. When it finally gave way, the result was a catastrophe.

The Mothman sightings stopped after the bridge fell. Some people believed the creature was a harbinger of disaster, a warning of death. For them, the timing proved it. Keel himself had written down witness reports that the creature seemed to appear near the bridge in the weeks before the collapse, as if on patrol.

Skeptics, who ask for proof before they believe, saw a flaw in this logic. People had been seeing the Mothman for thirteen months. They lived in a small town where almost every landmark was within walking distance of the bridge. So any pattern could be found afterward. But the link between the Mothman and the Silver Bridge had already become folklore. It was now part of the legend.

The most common skeptical explanation is that witnesses saw sandhill cranes. These are large birds. They stand up to four feet tall and have seven-foot wingspans. They also have distinctive red markings on the face, which could seem to “glow” at night.

Other possibilities include barred owls or great horned owls. The shine from their eyes in car headlights can look red. Witnesses might also have mistaken herons for the creature. Or it might have been a mix of mistakes and mass suggestion. The heavy news coverage and the isolation of the area probably made the effect stronger. Witnesses saw unclear shapes and understood them in light of the growing legend.

The idea of a strange creature that appears before a disaster is not unique to Point Pleasant. In Japanese folklore, the mujina, a shape-shifting creature, was said to appear before earthquakes. In Irish mythology, the bean-sidhe, or banshee, wails before a death in the family.

Other cases are much like the Mothman. The Flatwoods Monster of West Virginia (1952) and the Owlman of Cornwall, England (1976) have much in common with it. They had human-like shapes and glowing eyes, and they appeared near small communities. Do these likenesses point to a real phenomenon found in many cultures? Or do they just show that people everywhere link the unknown with bad omens? That is a matter of opinion.

Today the Mothman is no longer just a mystery. It is a business. Point Pleasant, with about 4,000 people, has built a whole tourist trade around a creature that may or may not have existed. The Mothman Museum draws thousands of visitors each year. A twelve-foot stainless steel statue of the creature stands in the town square. The Mothman Festival, held every September, brings 10,000 visitors to a town that has little else to draw tourists. The 2002 film The Mothman Prophecies, starring Richard Gere, introduced the legend to the world.

But behind the souvenirs and the movies lies a real tragedy. Forty-six people died when the Silver Bridge collapsed. Their deaths have been overshadowed by the myth of a monster.

The people of Point Pleasant lived through thirteen months of real fear and then a real disaster. They deserve to be remembered as more than characters in a ghost story. Their fear was real. Their loss was real.

And what flew through the West Virginia night in 1966? Was it a crane, an owl, or something else entirely? Nobody knows. The shadow it cast over that small river town has never fully lifted.
