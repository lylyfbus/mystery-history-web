The Crystal Skulls: Ancient Mayan Artifacts or Elaborate Modern Forgeries?

The girl at Lubaantun

Imagine a skull as big as a human head, carved from one block of clear quartz crystal. It glitters in the light. It even has a loose lower jaw. This is the Mitchell-Hedges Crystal Skull, the most famous of all the crystal skulls.

Anna Mitchell-Hedges said that she found it in 1924, at the ancient Maya ruins of Lubaantun. The ruins are in British Honduras, which is now Belize. Anna was the adopted daughter of Frederick Albert (“Mike”) Mitchell-Hedges, a British adventurer and writer. He lived from 1882 to 1959.

The story says that the skull lay hidden under a fallen altar. Some people called it the “Skull of Doom.” They said that no ancient tool could have made it. It was a perfect tale for a public that loved ancient mysteries. But is it true?

A treasure with powers?

Crystal skulls are life-sized carvings of a human skull, each made from one block of clear or milky quartz. Museums have shown them, and films have used them. In 2008, one was at the center of the film Indiana Jones and the Kingdom of the Crystal Skull.

Many New Age believers say the skulls have special powers. New Age believers follow modern spiritual ideas about healing and energy. They say the skulls can heal, bring visions, and send thoughts from mind to mind. Some say the skulls store the knowledge of ancient peoples.

So what do the skulls really say about the past? To find out, we must ask where they came from.

No skull in the ground

Quartz is a very hard stone. On the Mohs scale, which ranks how hard a mineral is, it scores 7. That makes it very difficult to carve.

The peoples of ancient Mesoamerica did carve stone with great skill. The Maya worked jade and obsidian. The Aztec and Mixtec made skull images in stone and bone, as symbols of death, rebirth, and respect for ancestors. They also worked rock crystal, but only in small pieces such as beads and cups.

And here is the key fact. Nobody has found a crystal skull in a documented dig in the Americas. So where do the skulls first appear? In the market of the 19th century.

The story changes

The skulls became known in the late 19th century. Museums in Europe and America were racing to collect objects from ancient civilizations. That created a booming market for antiquities, which are very old objects. It also created a market for fakes.

The Mitchell-Hedges skull first shows up in London in 1933. An art dealer named Sidney Burney owned it before 1934. In 1943, it was sold at Sotheby’s, an auction house in London, and Mitchell-Hedges bought it. His 1954 book, Danger My Ally, then gave the skull a Maya story.

Researchers say that Anna’s tales about Lubaantun were her own inventions, and that they began in 1964. Jane MacLaren Walsh, an anthropologist at the Smithsonian, searched through old records. She found that Mitchell-Hedges bought the skull at an auction in London. She says that the Lubaantun stories were made up and helped to make the skull famous.

So the skull did not come out of a Maya ruin. But where did it come from? One name leads us further back.

The dealer from Paris

Eugene Boban was a French antiquarian and dealer. From about 1860 to about 1880, he worked in Mexico City. He was called the “archaeologist” of the court of Emperor Maximilian, an Austrian archduke whom Napoleon III made ruler of Mexico in 1864.

Walsh calls Boban perhaps the most mysterious figure in the history of the crystal skulls. She says that he sold at least five of them. The truth about them, she wrote, may have gone to the grave with him.

In 1881, his Paris shop offered a life-sized skull as a “masterpiece.” In 1886, he held an auction in New York, where Tiffany & Co. bought a crystal skull for $950. In 1897, Tiffany’s sold it to the British Museum, as a pre-Columbian object. Pre-Columbian means from before Columbus arrived in the Americas. Mexico’s National Museum had judged that skull a fake in 1885.

Researchers traced the British Museum skull back to Boban. The skull in Paris, now in the Musée du Quai Branly, also came from him. If the dealer was in Paris, where was the carver?

Tool marks tell the truth

One likely answer is Idar-Oberstein, a small town in western Germany. In the 19th century, it was a great center for cutting gems. Its craftsmen worked agate, jasper, and quartz with spinning wheels and hard grit. Nobody in the pre-Columbian Americas used such tools. The link between the skulls and this town is only a guess. Still, the quartz of the British Museum skull may come from Brazil, Madagascar, or the Alps. None of these lay on Aztec or Mixtec trade routes.

The strongest study came in 2008. Walsh and Margaret Sax, with the British Museum and the Smithsonian, used scanning electron microscopy. That method makes huge, detailed pictures of a surface. Both skulls showed deep, regular cuts from spinning wheels. The Smithsonian skull showed a grit called silicon carbide, first made in the 1890s. Real Aztec and Mixtec crystal objects have shallow, uneven marks, made with stone and wood tools.

Walsh also studied the Mitchell-Hedges skull. She found signs of diamond-coated tools, and decided that it was probably made in Europe in modern times. In the 1970s, tests at Hewlett-Packard laboratories in California found that it was carved from a single crystal. Believers often cite those tests as proof of an ancient origin. But they do not prove that.

Legends that kept growing

None of this stopped the legends. The best known says that 13 ancient crystal skulls exist. When they are brought together, they will unlock wisdom and save humanity from disaster. Experts say that this idea is modern. It has no basis in any known Mesoamerican myth.

The 2008 film made the legend stronger. In it, the skulls come from beings from another dimension. The film was fiction, but millions of people saw it.

Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica saw skulls differently. For them, skulls were symbols of rebirth, ancestors, and the continuing of life. The “Skull of Doom” is a Western idea placed on their cultures.

What Anna’s skull teaches us

So what do we know? Walsh says that every crystal skull her team has studied is modern. One archaeologist, Michael Smith, agrees that the large skulls are fakes. He thinks that some small ones may be real Aztec objects. The skulls tested so far were made in the mid-19th century or later, almost certainly in Europe. The trail of ownership leads to dealers and auction houses, not to a Maya temple.

What is open? The British Museum’s own page says that the question remains open for its skull. Nobody can say who carved each one. Walsh wrote that Boban’s secrets may have gone to the grave with him.

Back at Lubaantun, the tale of a girl and an altar seems to be false. The story says more about us than about the Maya. Why do we so badly want these skulls to be ancient?
