Unknown Phenomena

The Bennington Triangle: Vermont’s Bermuda Triangle

Picture the dark woods of southwestern Vermont. The Green Mountains rise in old, folded ridges. The trees close in overhead like the walls of a cathedral. Here lies a stretch of wilderness with a reputation as a mysterious place.

It is centered on Glastenbury Mountain, a remote peak 3,748 feet high in the Green Mountain National Forest. People say it also takes in parts of Bennington, Woodford, Shaftsbury, and Somerset. People call it the “Bennington Triangle.” The name was made up by the Vermont author Joseph A. Citro, who first used it in 1992.

Between 1945 and 1950, five people vanished in or near this area. That is a short span of five years, and it is still hard to explain. Some were experienced outdoorsmen who knew the land. Others were ordinary people on ordinary days.

Two vanished on the same date, December 1, three years apart. One disappeared from a moving bus. The legend says his bus schedule was left behind, but not the man. Another was an eight-year-old boy. He vanished while his mother was busy nearby. The only body ever found was that of a woman who disappeared in 1950.

The land itself is part of the story. Glastenbury Mountain is the center of the mystery. It is usually given as 3,748 feet high. It lies in the Green Mountain National Forest, a large area of rough, wooded land in Vermont.

The Long Trail runs along the crest of the Green Mountains. It is Vermont’s famous hiking route, about 272 miles long. It crosses Glastenbury Mountain. Paula Welden was hiking on it when she vanished.

The area once had a logging town called Glastenbury. In the 19th century it did fairly well, because it lived off timber. In the late 1800s, the logging towns of Glastenbury and Somerset began to decline. In 1937, the Vermont General Assembly ended Glastenbury as a town.

All that was left were old foundations and cellar holes. The place felt like it had been given up by the living. These ghost-town ruins still dot the land. Cellar holes, stone walls, and rusting tools are slowly being eaten by the returning forest.

The Long Trail is still one of the most popular hiking routes in Vermont. But some hikers say the part near Glastenbury Mountain has an eerie quiet. Some people find this unsettling.

The first disappearance happened in November 1945, just months after World War II ended. Most accounts give the date as November 12. Middie Rivers was a 74-year-old outdoorsman. He was hunting in rough country near Bickford Hollow, in the hills near Bennington.

Rivers is said to have known this land very well. He walked away from his hunting camp. He never came back.

Searchers came from nearby military and state guard units. They found only a single rifle cartridge and a handkerchief. Nobody knows if these belonged to him. There was no body and no sign of a struggle. A man who had spent his life in these woods was gone.

Just over a year later, on December 1, 1946, the disappearance that made the Bennington Triangle famous took place. Paula Jean Welden was an 18-year-old second-year student at Bennington College. The college sits near the edge of the wilderness.

On the afternoon of December 1, Welden told her roommates she was going for a hike on the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain. Several witnesses saw her that afternoon. A local contractor, Louis Knapp, drove her part of the way. Hikers on the trail also said they saw her.

She wore a red jacket, jeans, and light sneakers. These were not warm enough for the cold night to come. But they were not unusual for an afternoon walk. She never came back.

What followed was a very large search. Hundreds of volunteers joined in, along with firefighters and National Guard troops. Bennington College closed for several days so that students and teachers could help. The FBI also helped.

Despite all of this, Paula Welden was never found. Her case is one of Vermont’s most puzzling unsolved cases. The search showed that Vermont had no statewide police force. Her father and others criticized how it was handled. The Vermont State Police was created in 1947, and her case was one reason.

Three years to the day after Paula Welden walked into the woods, the Bennington Triangle took its third victim. It may be the most unexplained case of all.

On December 1, 1949, James E. Tedford was returning home to St. Albans, Vermont. He was a 68-year-old veteran of World War I. He lived at the Vermont Soldiers’ Home in Bennington. He boarded a bus that was headed for Bennington.

This case is often told as a legend. In the popular story, Tedford sat on the bus as it went through the dark December evening. Then, at some point, he was no longer there. His bus schedule was said to be left on his seat. In this story, nobody saw him get off.

The records are thinner than the legend. One account says he was last seen at the bus depot in Burlington. The driver said a man like him may have got off in Brandon. His disappearance was not reported for about a week. He was never found.

The date was December 1, exactly three years after Paula Welden’s disappearance. This made many people feel that something strange was at work in the mountains of southwestern Vermont.

Less than a year later, the Bennington Triangle took its youngest victim. On October 12, 1950, an eight-year-old boy named Paul Jepson went with his mother to the Bennington town dump. Friends called him Buddy. His mother tended pigs there. This was an ordinary job in rural Vermont. Some sources spell his name Jephson.

She left Paul nearby while she went about her work. When she looked for him, her son was gone.

The search was immediate and big. A bloodhound followed his scent along a road to an intersection. There the trail ended. Paul was never found.

The place where he vanished was not deep wilderness. It was the edge of a populated area, near a road. This makes his case hard to explain.

Sixteen days after Paul Jepson vanished, on October 28, 1950, the Bennington Triangle claimed its fifth and last confirmed victim. Frieda Langer was a 53-year-old woman from North Adams, Massachusetts. She was camping near Somerset with her husband and her cousin Herbert Elsner.

On October 28, Langer and her cousin went for a hike in the woods near their campsite. At some point she was lost. She never came back. The search for her went on for days.

Then, in May 1951, about seven months later, Langer’s body was found. Some accounts give the date as May 12. It lay about three and a half miles southeast of the campsite. This was far from where she had been looking for her way.

The cause of death could not be found. One investigator thought she may have drowned in a deep pond. He thought a spring flood later carried her body out. She was the only one of the five whose remains were ever found. Her death gave few answers.

The five disappearances have led to many theories over the years. Some are likely, and some are paranormal, which means beyond normal science.

The simplest explanation is natural causes. The Green Mountains are rugged. The weather can turn deadly without warning. A person caught by hypothermia, a fall, or a sudden illness in remote country can die quickly and be hard to find. Vermont’s wilderness is not as big as the western mountains. But it is thick enough to hide a body for a long time.

Middie Rivers, at 74, could have had a heart attack or stroke. Paula Welden, in clothes too thin for December, could have died from the cold. Frieda Langer may have drowned, as the investigator thought.

But the natural-causes theory has trouble with some unsettling details. In the legend, James Tedford did not vanish in the wilderness. He vanished from a moving bus with other passengers on it. Paul Jepson did not wander into remote backcountry. He vanished from the edge of a populated area while his mother was close by.

Some people find the number of disappearances strange. Skeptics note that people do go missing in wild country. A Vermont State Police officer has said the cases were likely a coincidence.

Some people think foul play may explain at least some of the cases. In Welden’s case, a local man named Fred Gaudette was a person of interest. He later said his remarks were idle talk, and no evidence of a crime was found. The victims were very different: an older hunter, a young college woman, a veteran, a child, and a middle-aged camper. This does not fit one simple pattern.

The paranormal theories are where the legend truly takes off. Some blame a large, unknown creature living in the deep woods around Glastenbury Mountain. Others suggest UFO abductions. They point to how sudden and complete the vanishings were as a sign of non-human action.

Other ideas include time slips and portals to other dimensions. Some say the land is cursed.

Citro’s later retellings added some of these ideas. They included alien abductions, a man-eating rock, and a Bennington Monster, a creature like Bigfoot. Citro has said that he added speculation that he did not believe himself.

No physical evidence has ever backed up any of these theories. But the lack of clear answers keeps them alive in people’s imaginations.

The name “Bennington Triangle” did not exist when the people vanished. It was coined decades later by Joseph A. Citro, a Vermont author. He first used it in a 1992 radio commentary on Vermont Public Radio. He has said he copied the pattern of the Bermuda Triangle and just pulled the name out of the air.

The name stuck. Over the following decades, the legend grew. Books, documentaries, podcasts, and internet forums turned a real set of unsolved cases into a full American myth.

Today the Bennington Triangle draws hikers, folklore fans, and true crime fans to the Green Mountain National Forest. Glastenbury Mountain is still a remote and moody peak on the Long Trail. The old logging towns of Glastenbury and Somerset still have their cellar holes and stone walls. They still give the feeling of a place the world has passed by.

The families of the missing have had a hard road. This is especially true of the Welden family. Paula’s father hired his own investigators. They have had to live between real grief and public fascination.

The Vermont State Police was created in 1947, and the Paula Welden case was one reason. It is still one of the most lasting results of the story. Sometimes the longest-lasting result of a mystery is not an answer. It is an institution built because there was no answer. And the question remains. What happened to those five people?
