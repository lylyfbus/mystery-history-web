Unknown Phenomena

The Bennington Triangle: Vermont’s Bermuda Triangle

Picture the dark woods of southwestern Vermont. The Green Mountains rise in old, folded ridges. The trees close in overhead like the walls of a cathedral. Here lies a stretch of wilderness with a reputation as one of the most mysteriously dangerous places in America.

It is centered on Glastenbury Mountain, a remote peak 3,748 feet high in the Green Mountain National Forest. It also takes in the towns of Bennington, Woodford, and Shaftsbury, and the ghost town of Somerset. People call it the “Bennington Triangle.” The name was made up by the Vermont author Joseph A. Citro in his 1992 book Passing Strange: True Tales of New England Hauntings and Horrors.

Between 1945 and 1950, five people vanished in or near this area. That is a short span of five years, and it is still hard to explain. Some were experienced outdoorsmen who knew the land. Others were ordinary people on ordinary days.

Two vanished on the same date, December 1, three years apart. One disappeared from a moving bus. Luggage was left behind, but no person. Another was an eight-year-old boy in a bright red jacket. He vanished while his mother’s back was turned. The only body ever found was in a place that had already been searched.

The land itself is part of the story. Glastenbury Mountain is the center of the mystery and the highest point in the area, at 3,748 feet. It lies in the Green Mountain National Forest, a huge area of rough, wooded land that covers much of Vermont.

The Long Trail runs along the crest of the Green Mountains. It is Vermont’s famous 272-mile hiking route. It crosses Glastenbury Mountain, and several of the disappearances happened on or near it.

The area once had a logging town called Glastenbury. In the 19th century it did fairly well, because it lived off timber. By the late 1800s, the forests around it had been cut down and the timber was gone. The town’s economy collapsed. In 1937, an act of the Vermont General Assembly ended Glastenbury and Somerset as towns.

All that was left were old foundations and cellar holes. The place felt like it had been given up by the living. These ghost-town ruins still dot the land. Cellar holes, stone walls, and rusting tools are slowly being eaten by the returning forest.

The Long Trail is still one of the most popular hiking routes in Vermont. But hikers say the part near Glastenbury Mountain has an eerie quiet. There is no birdsong and little animal life. Some people find this deeply unsettling.

The first disappearance happened on November 12, 1945, just months after World War II ended. Middie Rivers was a 74-year-old hunting guide and a lifelong outdoorsman. He was leading four hunters through rough country near Bickford Hollow. This is about four miles west of Bennington and north of Vermont Route 9 near Woodford.

Rivers knew this land very well. He had hunted and guided in these mountains for decades. During the hunt, he went ahead of the group to scout the way. The hunters waited. And waited. Rivers never came back.

A search lasted eight days. Dozens of volunteers, game wardens, and skilled trackers searched the area. They found one rifle cartridge in a stream bed. It was the only trace Middie Rivers ever left. There was no body, no clothing, and no sign of a struggle. A man who had spent his life in these woods had stepped off the trail and was gone.

Just over a year later, on December 1, 1946, the disappearance that made the Bennington Triangle famous took place. Paula Jean Welden was an 18-year-old second-year student at Bennington College. It is a well-known college at the edge of the wilderness.

On the afternoon of December 1, Welden told her roommates she was going for a hike on the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain. Several witnesses saw her that afternoon. A local man gave her directions to the trailhead. A hiker saw her about two miles up the trail at around 2:30 PM.

She wore a red windbreaker, blue jeans, and tennis shoes. These were not warm enough for December cold. But they were not unusual for a young woman on an afternoon walk. She never came back.

What followed was one of the largest searches in Vermont history. Hundreds of volunteers joined in. The Fire Department, the State Police, the National Guard, and even the FBI searched the mountain. A $5,000 reward was offered, which was a lot of money in 1946. Bloodhounds were brought in. Aircraft were used.

Despite all of this, not a single trace of Paula Welden was ever found. There was no scrap of clothing, no footprint, and no button. Her case is one of Vermont’s oldest and most puzzling unsolved cases. Her disappearance led directly to the creation of the Vermont State Police in 1947. The police forces at the time had not been able to handle such a large search.

Three years to the day after Paula Welden walked into the woods, the Bennington Triangle took its third victim. It may be the most unexplained case of all.

On December 1, 1949, James E. Tedford was returning home to St. Albans, Vermont. He was a 68-year-old veteran of World War II. He had been visiting relatives in Bennington. He boarded a commercial bus at the Bennington station and settled into his seat. The bus began its trip north through the Green Mountains. Its road ran beside the very wilderness where Middie Rivers and Paula Welden had vanished.

Other passengers later confirmed that they had seen Tedford on the bus. He was sitting and awake as the bus went through the dark December evening. At some point on the trip, Tedford was no longer on the bus. Most accounts place this between Bennington and the town of Wilmington. That road runs through the heart of the Bennington Triangle.

When the bus reached its next scheduled stop, his luggage was still in the cargo hold. His bus schedule was still on his seat. His hat was still on the luggage rack above his seat. But James Tedford was gone.

He had not got off at any stop. No passenger had seen him stand up or walk to the door. The bus had made no unscheduled stops by the side of the road. There was no sign of a struggle. There was no broken window and no emergency exit opened. One moment a man sat in his seat. The next moment he was not there, as though the air itself had swallowed him.

A long search along the bus route found nothing. No body was ever found. The date was December 1, exactly three years after Paula Welden’s disappearance. This made many people feel that something unnatural was at work in the mountains of southwestern Vermont.

Less than a year later, the Bennington Triangle took its youngest victim. On October 12, 1950, an eight-year-old boy named Paul Jephson went with his mother to a dump area near Woodford. It lies just east of Bennington, on the edge of the triangle. His mother went to throw away garbage. This was an ordinary job in rural Vermont.

She left Paul playing near the vehicle while she went about her business. She turned her back for only a few minutes. When she turned around again, her son was gone.

Paul wore a bright red jacket. It would have stood out against the autumn leaves. This made his sudden, total disappearance even harder to understand.

The search was immediate and huge. Hundreds of volunteers took part. They included the Bennington Fire Department, the Vermont State Police, and local hunters who knew the land. Bloodhounds were brought in to follow the boy’s scent. The dogs reportedly picked up a trail that led into the woods toward Glastenbury Mountain. But the scent faded, and the handlers lost it.

After days of hard searching, not a single trace of Paul Jephson was found. There was no red jacket, no shoe, no scrap of clothing, and no footprint in the soft autumn ground. The place where he vanished was not deep wilderness. It was the edge of a populated area, near a road and within sight of where his mother had stood.

Sixteen days after Paul Jephson vanished, on October 28, 1950, the Bennington Triangle claimed its fifth and last confirmed victim. Frieda Langer was a 53-year-old woman from North Adams, Massachusetts. She was camping with her cousin Herbert Elsner near East Shaftsbury, on the western edge of the Bennington Triangle.

On the morning of October 28, Langer and Elsner went for a hike in the wooded hills above their campsite. At some point Langer decided to head back to camp ahead of her cousin. She was last seen walking alone through the woods toward the campsite. She never arrived.

When Elsner got back to camp and found her missing, he raised the alarm at once. The search for Frieda Langer was one of the biggest in Vermont history up to that time. Search parties combed the woods for days. Aircraft were used. Every trail, ravine, and hollow within five miles was checked. Nothing was found, at least not at first.

Then, in May 1951, seven months after she vanished, Langer’s body was found. It lay in a swampy area near the Long Trail, about three miles from where she was last seen. This was deeply troubling. Search teams had already combed that area carefully during the first search.

How could her body have been there all along without being found? Or was it placed there later? This has never been explained.

There was another problem. The body was in such poor condition that the cause of death could not be found. The medical examiner found no sign of gunshot wounds, stab wounds, or blows. But the state of the remains made a firm ruling impossible. She was the only one of the five whose remains were ever found. Her death gave no answers.

The five disappearances have led to many theories over the past seven decades. Some are likely, and some are paranormal, which means beyond normal science.

The simplest explanation is natural causes. The Green Mountains are rugged. The weather can turn deadly without warning. A person caught by hypothermia, a fall, or a sudden illness in remote country can die quickly and be hard to find. Vermont’s wilderness is not as big as the western mountains. But it is thick enough to hide a body forever, especially in the deep ravines, sinkholes, and old mine shafts that dot the region.

Middie Rivers, at 74, could have had a heart attack or stroke. Paula Welden, in clothes too thin for December, could have died from the cold. Frieda Langer could have fallen and been hurt in the swamp where her body was found.

But the natural-causes theory has trouble with some unsettling details. James Tedford did not vanish in the wilderness. He vanished from a moving bus with other passengers on it. Paul Jephson did not wander into remote backcountry. He vanished from the edge of a populated area while his mother’s back was turned.

The number of disappearances is also unusual. Five people in five years, in a thinly populated rural area, is statistically strange. Skeptics note that people do go missing in wild country. They say the cluster may be partly chance.

Some researchers say foul play is the most likely explanation for at least some of the cases. A serial killer in the area in the late 1940s is not impossible. But no suspect was ever named. The victims were very different: an elderly hunter, a young college woman, a veteran on a bus, a child, and a middle-aged camper. This does not fit the usual pattern of a serial predator.

The paranormal theories are where the legend truly takes off. Some blame a large, unknown creature living in the deep woods around Glastenbury Mountain. Others suggest UFO abductions. They point to how sudden and complete the vanishings were as a sign of non-human action.

Other ideas include time slips and portals to other dimensions. Some are curse theories. These say that the land carries an evil force. It was once home to the Mahican people. Later it held the abandoned logging towns.

Joseph A. Citro himself wrote down local folklore about a “Bennington Monster.” This was a large, hairy, man-like creature said to have been seen in the area. There were also stories of strange lights, sounds, and odd weather on Glastenbury Mountain. These stories were much older than the 1940s disappearances.

No physical evidence has ever backed up any of these theories. But the lack of normal explanations keeps them alive in people’s imaginations.

The name “Bennington Triangle” did not exist when the people vanished. It was coined decades later by Joseph A. Citro, a Vermont author and folklorist. The Boston Globe has called him the “Bard of the Bizarre.” He used the name in his 1992 book Passing Strange.

Citro compared the Vermont disappearances to the better-known Bermuda Triangle. Both had clusters of unexplained vanishings in a roughly triangular area. The name stuck. Over the following decades, the legend grew. Books, documentaries, podcasts, and internet forums turned a real set of unsolved cases into a full American myth.

Today the Bennington Triangle draws hikers, folklore fans, and true crime fans to the Green Mountain National Forest. Glastenbury Mountain is still a remote and moody peak on the Long Trail. The ghost towns of Glastenbury and Somerset still have their cellar holes, stone walls, and forest-covered clearings. They still give the feeling of a place the world has passed by.

The families of the missing have had a hard road. This is especially true of the Welden family, who never stopped searching for answers. They have had to live between real grief and public fascination.

The Vermont State Police was created in direct answer to the Paula Welden case. It is still one of the most lasting results of the Bennington Triangle. Sometimes the longest-lasting result of a mystery is not an answer. It is an institution built because there was no answer. And the question remains. What happened to those five people?
