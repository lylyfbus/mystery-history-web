The Bennington Triangle: Vermont’s Bermuda Triangle

In the dense, dark woodlands of southwestern Vermont, where the Green Mountains rise in ancient, folded ridges and the trees close in overhead like the walls of a cathedral, there is a stretch of wilderness that has earned a reputation as one of the most mysteriously dangerous places in America. Centered on Glastenbury Mountain — a remote, 3,748-foot peak in the Green Mountain National Forest — and encompassing the towns of Bennington, Woodford, Shaftsbury, and the ghost town of Somerset, this area has been called the "Bennington Triangle", a name coined by Vermont author Joseph A. Citro in his 1992 book Passing Strange: True Tales of New England Hauntings and Horrors. Between 1945 and 1950, in a concentrated five-year window that still defies easy explanation, five people vanished in or near this area. Some were experienced outdoorsmen who knew the terrain. Others were ordinary people going about ordinary days. Two vanished on the exact same calendar date — December 1 — three years apart. One disappeared from a moving bus, leaving behind luggage but no body. Another, an eight-year-old boy in a bright red jacket, vanished while his mother's back was turned. The only body ever recovered was found in an area that had already been searched. It is a mystery that resonates with the same eerie sense of an invisible boundary between safety and oblivion as the Bermuda Triangle, the same haunting absence of answers as the Mary Celeste ghost ship, and the same suggestion of something unknown lurking in the wilderness that pervades the legend of Bigfoot.

The landscape of the Bennington Triangle is itself a character in the story. Glastenbury Mountain, the geographic center of the mystery, is the highest point in the area at 3,748 feet (1,143 meters). The mountain is located in the Green Mountain National Forest, a vast expanse of rugged, forested terrain that stretches across much of Vermont. The Long Trail, Vermont's famous 272-mile hiking route that runs the length of the state along the crest of the Green Mountains, passes directly over Glastenbury Mountain — and it was on or near this trail that several of the disappearances occurred. The area was once home to the logging town of Glastenbury, a moderately thriving community in the 19th century that depended on the timber industry. By the late 1800s, the surrounding forests had been clear-cut, the timber exhausted, and the town's economy collapsed. Glastenbury and Somerset were both unincorporated by an act of the Vermont General Assembly in 1937, leaving behind only the remnants of foundations, cellar holes, and the eerie atmosphere of a place that had been abandoned by the living. It is against this backdrop of industrial ruin and reclaiming wilderness that the disappearances occurred.

The Five Who Vanished: A Chronicle of Disappearance

The first disappearance occurred on November 12, 1945, just months after the end of World War II. Middie Rivers, a 74-year-old experienced hunting guide and lifelong outdoorsman, was leading a party of four hunters through the rugged terrain near Bickford Hollow, approximately four miles west of Bennington and north of Vermont Route 9 near Woodford. Rivers knew this territory intimately — he had hunted and guided in these mountains for decades. At some point during the hunt, Rivers went ahead of the group to scout the terrain. The hunters waited. And waited. Rivers never came back. An eight-day search involving dozens of volunteers, game wardens, and experienced trackers combed the area. They found a single rifle cartridge in a stream bed — the only trace Middie Rivers ever left behind. No body, no clothing, no signs of a struggle. A man who had spent his life in these woods had stepped off the trail and simply ceased to exist.

Just over a year later, on December 1, 1946, the disappearance that would make the Bennington Triangle famous occurred. Paula Jean Welden was an 18-year-old sophomore at Bennington College, a prestigious liberal arts school located at the edge of the wilderness. On the afternoon of December 1, Welden told her roommates she was going for a hike on the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain. She was seen by multiple witnesses that afternoon: a local man gave her directions to the trailhead, and a hiker observed her approximately two miles up the trail at around 2:30 PM. She was wearing a red windbreaker, blue jeans, and tennis shoes — inadequate clothing for the December cold but not unusual for a young woman going for an afternoon walk. She never came back. What followed was one of the largest search operations in Vermont history. Hundreds of volunteers, including the Fire Department, State Police, National Guard, and even the FBI, combed the mountain. A $5,000 reward was offered — a substantial sum in 1946. Bloodhounds were brought in. Aircraft were deployed. Despite all of this, not a single trace of Paula Welden was ever found — not a scrap of clothing, not a footprint, not a button. Her case remains one of Vermont's oldest and most baffling cold cases, and her disappearance directly led to the creation of the Vermont State Police in 1947, because existing law enforcement had proven inadequate to handle such a large-scale investigation.

📑 The Disappearances by the Numbers

Between 1945 and 1950, five people vanished in or near the Bennington Triangle. Middie Rivers (74, hunting guide) vanished November 12, 1945 near Bickford Hollow. Paula Jean Welden (18, college student) vanished December 1, 1946 on the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain. James E. Tedford (68, veteran) vanished December 1, 1949 from a bus on Route 7 between St. Albans and Bennington. Paul Jephson (8, child) vanished October 12, 1950 near a dump area close to Glastenbury. Frieda Langer (53, hiker) vanished October 28, 1950 near Glastenbury Mountain; her body was found May 1951. Note the eerie calendar pattern: two disappearances occurred on the exact same date (December 1) three years apart, and three of the five occurred in December. The victims ranged in age from 8 to 74. Three were men, two were women. Only one body was ever recovered. The other four people left behind no physical trace whatsoever.

The Man Who Vanished from a Moving Bus

Of all the Bennington Triangle disappearances, none is more bizarre than the case of James E. Tedford. On December 1, 1949 — exactly three years to the day after Paula Welden's disappearance — the 68-year-old World War II veteran and resident of the Bennington Soldiers' Home was returning from a visit to relatives in St. Albans, Vermont. He boarded a local bus for the journey south to Bennington, a route that took him along Route 7 through the heart of the area that would become known as the Bennington Triangle. According to witness statements, Tedford was seen on the bus during the journey. Other passengers remembered him. Some accounts state that at one point along the route, the bus made a brief stop — and when it resumed its journey, Tedford was still in his seat. But when the bus arrived at the terminal in Bennington, Tedford's seat was empty. His luggage remained on the bus. His personal belongings were still in his seat. He had not gotten off at any stop. He had simply... vanished. The driver had not seen him exit. No passenger had noticed him leave. It was as though James Tedford had dissolved into the air between one bus stop and the next.

The Tedford case is the most controversial of the Bennington Triangle disappearances, and some researchers have questioned whether it occurred as described. Skeptics point out that the details come primarily from secondary accounts and that no contemporaneous newspaper reports have been found confirming the exact circumstances. Nevertheless, the story has been repeated in numerous books and articles about the Bennington Triangle, including those by Joseph Citro himself, and it remains one of the most frequently cited elements of the legend. If the account is accurate, it is deeply unsettling: a man who vanished from a public vehicle on a public road, witnessed by multiple passengers, leaving behind all his possessions, in the geographic heart of a region where people had been disappearing for years. Even if the details have been embellished over time, the persistence of the story speaks to the power of the Bennington Triangle legend and the genuine fear it has inspired in the communities of southwestern Vermont.

The Boy in the Red Jacket and the Body That Was Found

The final two disappearances occurred within sixteen days of each other in October 1950. On October 12, 1950, Paul Jephson, an eight-year-old boy, vanished in an area near Glastenbury Mountain. His mother, who was delivering a pig to a local dump area, had left Paul in the truck or playing nearby. When she returned, the boy was gone. Paul had been wearing a bright red jacket that should have been highly visible against the autumn landscape. Search teams with bloodhounds were dispatched. Aircraft were used to scan the area from above. Despite the visibility of the red jacket, despite the dogs, despite the aerial search, no trace of Paul Jephson was ever found. The search was hampered by the area's dense undergrowth and rugged terrain, but the complete absence of any physical evidence — in an area that was not particularly remote by Vermont standards — troubled investigators.

Then, on October 28, 1950, just sixteen days after Paul Jephson's disappearance, Frieda Langer vanished. The 53-year-old woman was hiking with her cousin, Herbert Elsner, near Glastenbury Mountain when she slipped and fell into a stream. She told her cousin she would return to their camp to change into dry clothes. She was last seen walking in the direction of the camp. She never arrived. Unlike the other Bennington Triangle victims, Frieda Langer's fate was eventually known: in May 1951, approximately seven months after her disappearance, her body was discovered in an area near the Somerset Reservoir. The location was less than two miles from where she had last been seen. What made the discovery deeply unsettling was that the area where she was found had been thoroughly searched during the initial investigation. How her body could have been missed — or whether it had been placed there after the search — remains unclear. The condition of the remains made it impossible to determine a precise cause of death.

🎯 The Bennington Monster and Other Local Legends

The area around Glastenbury Mountain was already steeped in folklore long before Joseph Citro gave it a name. Local traditions spoke of a creature known as the "Bennington Monster" or "Old Scratch" — a large, hairy, bipedal creature not unlike Bigfoot/Sasquatch — that had been sighted in the Green Mountains for generations. Some residents of the Bennington area reported seeing a strange, apelike creature in the woods near Glastenbury in the years surrounding the disappearances. The area is also associated with reports of UFO sightings, strange lights in the sky, and an oppressive, uneasy feeling that many hikers and hunters have reported experiencing in the woods around the mountain. The ghost towns of Glastenbury and Somerset — with their abandoned foundations, cellar holes, and the remnants of 19th-century industry slowly being swallowed by the forest — add to the area's haunted atmosphere. Citro himself documented numerous stories of the supernatural in the area, including tales of a "cursed" Native American burial ground on Glastenbury Mountain and legends of an interdimensional portal that swallowed those who wandered too close. While these stories belong firmly to the realm of folklore rather than fact, they illustrate the deep cultural sense that there is something wrong about this stretch of Vermont wilderness — a feeling that predates the disappearances and persists to this day.

Theories and Explanations: What Happened to the Missing?

The disappearances of the Bennington Triangle have generated a wide range of theories, from the mundane to the fantastic. The most prosaic explanation is wilderness accidents. The Green Mountains are rugged, densely forested, and subject to harsh weather. A person who steps off the trail in dense undergrowth could fall into a ravine, be injured, and succumb to exposure before anyone finds them. The terrain is full of hidden dangers — crevices, streams, steep drop-offs — that could conceal a body indefinitely. The area is also home to black bears, and while fatal bear attacks are exceedingly rare, a predatory encounter cannot be entirely ruled out. Exposure to the elements — particularly in the cases of Rivers (November) and Welden (December) — could have killed quickly, especially for someone inadequately dressed. Under this theory, the cluster of disappearances is a statistical coincidence: five tragic but explicable accidents in an area of rugged terrain, occurring over a five-year period.

A darker theory proposes foul play — possibly a serial killer operating in the area. The concentrated geographic and temporal pattern of the disappearances (all within a five-year window, all in the same general area) is suggestive, and some researchers have pointed to the possibility that one or more of the victims were deliberately targeted. A man named Joseph Goodey, a convicted murderer who was active in New England in the mid-20th century, has been mentioned as a possible suspect in some accounts, though evidence linking him to the Bennington disappearances is thin to nonexistent. The theory of a serial killer is difficult to evaluate: the victims' profiles (ranging from an eight-year-old boy to a 74-year-old guide, both men and women) do not fit the typical pattern of a sexually motivated serial killer, and the methods and locations varied widely. Nevertheless, the possibility of foul play — particularly in the cases of Welden and Langer, who were women hiking alone — cannot be entirely dismissed. The Vermont State Police investigated the disappearances extensively and suspected foul play in several cases but were never able to make an arrest or identify a suspect.

Joseph Citro and the Birth of a Legend

The name "Bennington Triangle" did not exist during the actual disappearances. It was coined by Joseph A. Citro, a Vermont-born author and folklorist, in his 1992 book Passing Strange: True Tales of New England Hauntings and Horrors. Citro had been collecting and writing about New England folklore, ghost stories, and strange events for years, and he recognized that the cluster of disappearances around Glastenbury Mountain formed a compelling narrative. He drew explicit parallels to the Bermuda Triangle and to the Bridgewater Triangle in southeastern Massachusetts, another area associated with strange occurrences. Citro expanded on the Bennington Triangle legend in his subsequent books, including Shadow Child and The Vermont Ghost Guide, adding layers of folklore, ghost stories, and local legends to the historical facts. Critics have argued that Citro's naming and framing of the "Bennington Triangle" imposed a narrative of supernatural menace on what may have been a series of unrelated tragedies. Supporters counter that the geographic and temporal concentration of the disappearances is genuinely unusual and that the folklore surrounding the area — including stories of Bigfoot-like creatures, UFOs, and cursed ground — long predates Citro's books. In 2025, Vermont Public's Brave Little State podcast produced a detailed episode on the Bennington Triangle, tracing the rise of the legend in the popular imagination and noting that the story has developed a "cult following online" with a global audience, even as many Bennington residents remain unfamiliar with or skeptical of the legend.

🔍 The Long Trail: America's Oldest Long-Distance Hiking Trail

The Long Trail, which passes directly over Glastenbury Mountain, is the oldest long-distance hiking trail in the United States. Conceived in 1910 by the Green Mountain Club and completed in 1930, the trail runs approximately 272 miles (438 km) along the crest of the Green Mountains, from the Massachusetts-Vermont border to the Canadian border. It inspired the creation of the Appalachian Trail, which shares the same route through much of Vermont. The Long Trail is known for its rugged terrain, steep elevation changes, and remote sections that pass through dense, unpopulated wilderness — including the stretch over Glastenbury Mountain where several of the Bennington Triangle disappearances occurred. In 2008, a 27-year-old Bennington College student named Robert Singley got lost in the Glastenbury area while hiking the Long Trail but was found safe by the Vermont State Police — demonstrating that even experienced hikers can get into trouble in this remote, challenging terrain. The trail remains one of the most popular hiking destinations in New England, and the section over Glastenbury Mountain continues to attract both outdoor enthusiasts and those drawn by the legend of the Bennington Triangle.

🏔 A Wilderness That Keeps Its Secrets

The Bennington Triangle remains one of the most compelling clusters of unexplained disappearances in American history. Five people vanished in or near the same stretch of Vermont wilderness within a five-year period, and four of them left behind absolutely no physical trace. The area's rugged terrain, dense forests, and harsh weather provide plausible explanations for individual disappearances, but the concentration of cases — including the calendar coincidence of two vanishings on the same date three years apart, and the extraordinary case of James Tedford's disappearance from a moving bus — resists easy categorization. Joseph Citro's naming of the "Bennington Triangle" gave the legend a framework that has captivated readers and researchers for over three decades, and the area's existing folklore — ghost towns, Bigfoot sightings, UFO reports, and tales of cursed ground — adds layers of cultural meaning that elevate the story beyond a simple catalogue of missing persons. The most likely explanation is that a combination of factors — wilderness accidents, harsh weather, possible foul play, and statistical coincidence — accounts for the disappearances. But the complete absence of physical evidence in four of the five cases, despite extensive searches, leaves a gap that logic struggles to fill. The Green Mountains are beautiful, but they are also vast and indifferent. Those who enter them do so at their own risk. And sometimes, they do not come back.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Bennington Triangle?

The Bennington Triangle is a term coined by Vermont author Joseph A. Citro in his 1992 book Passing Strange to describe an area of southwestern Vermont centered on Glastenbury Mountain (elevation 3,748 feet / 1,143 meters) in the Green Mountain National Forest. The area roughly encompasses the towns of Bennington, Woodford, Shaftsbury, and the ghost town of Somerset. Between 1945 and 1950, five people disappeared in or near this area under mysterious circumstances: Middie Rivers (74, hunting guide, November 1945), Paula Jean Welden (18, college student, December 1946), James E. Tedford (68, veteran, December 1949), Paul Jephson (8, child, October 1950), and Frieda Langer (53, hiker, October 1950). Only Langer's body was ever recovered. The area is also associated with folklore including Bigfoot-like creature sightings and reports of paranormal activity.

Was there a serial killer in the Bennington Triangle?

Some researchers have proposed that a serial killer may have been responsible for one or more of the Bennington Triangle disappearances. The concentrated geographic and temporal pattern (five disappearances in five years in the same area) is suggestive, and the Vermont State Police suspected foul play in several cases. A man named Joseph Goodey, a convicted murderer active in New England, has been mentioned as a possible suspect, though evidence linking him to the Bennington cases is extremely thin. Critics of the serial killer theory note that the victims' profiles vary widely in age (8 to 74), gender, and circumstance, which does not fit typical patterns. No suspect was ever identified, and no arrests were made. The theory remains speculative.

What happened to Paula Welden?

Paula Jean Welden was an 18-year-old Bennington College student who set out to hike the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain on December 1, 1946. She was seen by multiple witnesses that afternoon, including a hiker who observed her approximately two miles up the trail. She never returned. A massive search involving hundreds of volunteers, the FBI, the National Guard, and a $5,000 reward failed to find any trace of her — not a single item of clothing, not a footprint. Her case remains one of Vermont's oldest cold cases. Her disappearance directly led to the creation of the Vermont State Police in 1947, because existing local law enforcement proved inadequate to manage such a large investigation. Theories about her fate include wilderness accident, foul play, and voluntary disappearance, but no evidence supports any single explanation.

Is the Bennington Triangle dangerous to visit today?

The area around Glastenbury Mountain is part of the Green Mountain National Forest and is a popular destination for hikers on the Long Trail. There is no evidence that the area is inherently more dangerous than any other stretch of rugged wilderness in New England. The disappearances that gave the area its reputation occurred between 1945 and 1950, and no similar cluster has been reported since. However, the terrain is rugged, remote, and subject to harsh weather, and hikers should take standard wilderness precautions: carry proper equipment, tell someone your planned route, and be prepared for rapidly changing conditions. In 2008, a Bennington College student got lost in the area but was found safely by Vermont State Police. The legend of the Bennington Triangle adds an atmosphere of mystery to the landscape, but the real dangers are the same as those found in any mountain wilderness.
