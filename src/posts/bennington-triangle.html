Unknown Phenomena

The Bennington Triangle: Vermont’s Bermuda Triangle

A red jacket on the Long Trail

On the afternoon of December 1, 1946, an 18-year-old student named Paula Jean Welden told her roommates she was going for a hike. She studied at Bennington College in Vermont. Her plan was to walk the Long Trail near Glastenbury Mountain.

Several witnesses saw her that afternoon. A local contractor, Louis Knapp, drove her part of the way. Hikers on the trail also said they saw her. She wore a red jacket, jeans, and light sneakers. These were not warm enough for the cold night to come. But they were not unusual for an afternoon walk.

Paula never came back. Hers is the best-known case of five disappearances between 1945 and 1950. Today many people call the place the “Bennington Triangle.” Let us walk into those woods and see why.

A mountain and a ghost town

Imagine the dark woods of southwestern Vermont. The Green Mountains rise in old, folded ridges. Glastenbury Mountain, a remote peak usually given as 3,748 feet high, stands in the Green Mountain National Forest. People say the Triangle also takes in parts of Bennington, Woodford, Shaftsbury, and Somerset.

The Long Trail runs along the crest of the Green Mountains. It is Vermont’s famous hiking route, about 272 miles long. It crosses Glastenbury Mountain.

The area once had a logging town called Glastenbury. In the late 1800s, the logging towns of Glastenbury and Somerset began to decline. In 1937, the Vermont General Assembly ended Glastenbury as a town. Now only old foundations and cellar holes are left. Stone walls and rusting tools are slowly being eaten by the returning forest.

Some hikers say this part of the trail has an eerie quiet. Some people find this unsettling. But the story begins a year before Paula, with a man who knew these hills very well.

The first to vanish

In November 1945, just months after World War II ended, a 74-year-old outdoorsman named Middie Rivers went hunting. Most accounts give the date as November 12. He was in rough country near Bickford Hollow, in the hills near Bennington.

Rivers is said to have known this land very well. He walked away from his hunting camp. He never came back.

Searchers came from nearby military and state guard units. They found only a single rifle cartridge and a handkerchief. Nobody knows if these belonged to him. There was no body and no sign of a struggle.

The search for Paula

After Paula Welden vanished, a very large search began. Hundreds of volunteers joined in, along with firefighters and National Guard troops. Bennington College closed for several days so that students and teachers could help. The FBI also helped.

Despite all of this, Paula was never found. Her case is one of Vermont’s most puzzling unsolved cases. The search showed that Vermont had no statewide police force. Her father and others criticized how it was handled. The Vermont State Police was created in 1947, and her case was one reason.

Three years to the day later, another man vanished. Let us look at his story and at the two that followed.

A bus, a boy, and a camper

On December 1, 1949, James E. Tedford was traveling by bus to Bennington. He was a 68-year-old veteran of World War I. He lived at the Vermont Soldiers’ Home in Bennington. The legend says his bus schedule was left on his seat, and nobody saw him get off.

The records are thinner than the legend. One account says he was last seen at the bus depot in Burlington. The driver said a man like him may have got off in Brandon. His disappearance was not reported for about a week. He was never found.

On October 12, 1950, an eight-year-old boy named Paul Jepson went with his mother to the Bennington town dump. Friends called him Buddy. His mother tended pigs there. Some sources spell his name Jephson. She left Paul nearby while she worked. When she looked for him, her son was gone.

A bloodhound followed his scent along a road to an intersection. There the trail ended. Paul was never found. This was not deep wilderness. It was the edge of a populated area, near a road.

Sixteen days later, on October 28, 1950, a 53-year-old woman named Frieda Langer went for a hike near her campsite at Somerset. She was camping with her husband and her cousin Herbert Elsner. She never came back. The search went on for days.

In May 1951, about seven months later, her body was found about three and a half miles southeast of the campsite. She was the only one of the five whose remains were ever found. The cause of death could not be found. One investigator thought she may have drowned in a deep pond, and that a spring flood later carried her body out.

Could it be nature?

The simplest explanation is natural causes. The Green Mountains are rugged. The weather can turn deadly without warning. A person caught by hypothermia, a fall, or a sudden illness in remote country can die quickly and be hard to find.

Middie Rivers, at 74, could have had a heart attack or stroke. Paula Welden, in clothes too thin for December, could have died from the cold. Frieda Langer may have drowned, as the investigator thought.

But some details are hard to fit. In the legend, James Tedford vanished from a moving bus with other passengers on it. Paul Jepson vanished from the edge of a populated area while his mother was close by.

Skeptics note that people do go missing in wild country. A Vermont State Police officer has said the cases were likely a coincidence. Some people find the number of disappearances strange anyway.

Crime, monsters, and a borrowed name

Some people think foul play may explain at least some of the cases. In Welden’s case, a local man named Fred Gaudette was a person of interest. He later said his remarks were idle talk, and no evidence of a crime was found. The victims were very different: an older hunter, a young college woman, a veteran, a child, and a middle-aged camper. That does not fit one simple pattern.

Other ideas are paranormal, which means beyond normal science. Some blame a large, unknown creature in the woods. Others suggest UFO abductions, time slips, or portals to other dimensions. Some say the land is cursed. No physical evidence has ever backed up any of these ideas.

Here is a surprise. The name “Bennington Triangle” did not exist when these people vanished. The Vermont author Joseph A. Citro coined it, and he first used it in 1992 in a commentary on Vermont Public Radio. He has said he copied the pattern of the Bermuda Triangle. His later retellings added aliens, a man-eating rock, and a Bennington Monster, a creature like Bigfoot. Citro has said that he added speculation that he did not believe himself.

Five people, no answers

Here is what we know. Between 1945 and 1950, five people vanished in or near this area. Only one body was ever found. Real people lost their lives, and their families lived between grief and public fascination. Paula’s father even hired his own investigators.

Here is what we do not know. Nobody can say what happened to Middie Rivers, Paula Welden, James Tedford, or Paul Jepson. The natural explanations are the simplest, but they do not fit every detail.

Think again of that red jacket on the Long Trail. The Vermont State Police exists today partly because of Paula’s case. Sometimes the longest-lasting result of a mystery is not an answer. It is something built because there was no answer.
