Skinwalker Ranch: The 480-Acre Property in Utah Where Paranormal Activity Became a Government Secret

In 1994, a cattle rancher named Terry Sherman and his wife Gwen bought a 480-acre ranch near Ballard, Utah. It lay southeast of the town, in a remote area called the Uintah Basin. The land was lovely. It was high desert with wide views of the Uinta Mountains, and the Shermans found the quiet peaceful. They moved their family there, expecting a calm country life.

Within months, the peace was gone. The family began to see things they could not explain. Their cattle were found dead, with organs removed by cuts so clean that veterinarians could not name the tool. Strange craft appeared in the sky. They were glowing orbs, vehicles with clear shapes, and orange doorways that opened in midair. Odd creatures that walked on two legs stood at the edge of the trees and watched the house. Machines failed for no reason.

Then came the night that made the ranch famous. Terry Sherman met a wolf-like animal three times the size of a normal wolf. It had glowing red eyes. He shot it three times from close range, and it did not fall. It simply turned and walked away into the dark.

The Shermans lasted eighteen months. In 1996 they sold the ranch and left. They told their story in the Deseret News and later in articles by a reporter named George Knapp. A billionaire named Robert Bigelow read about them. He owned hotels and an aerospace company, and he bought the property. He began an investigation that became one of the most unusual, and most argued over, in American history. People called the place Skinwalker Ranch, after shape-shifting witches in Navajo legend.

The name comes from the Navajo (Diné) idea of the skin-walker. The Navajo word is yee naaldlooshii, which means “with it, he goes on all fours.” In Navajo tradition, a skin-walker is a medicine man or witch who has misused healing knowledge to gain power. To get it, he does terrible things, including killing a close family member. Then he can change between human and animal shapes. The animal is usually a coyote, wolf, fox, owl, or crow.

Skin-walkers are among the most feared beings in Navajo belief. They are said to run very fast and to read minds. They are said to slip into homes through tiny cracks and to bring sickness, madness, and death. Many traditional Navajo people will not speak of them directly. They fear that even saying the word may draw their attention.

The Ute people have their own traditions about this land. Their reservation borders the Uintah Basin. Ute elders have long called the area cursed or spiritually dangerous. Some tribal members have described a long-held rule against visiting the property. Strange sightings have been reported in the region since at least the 1970s, when UFO reports from the Uintah Basin appeared in local and national news.

In 1996, Bigelow bought the ranch and set up the National Institute for Discovery Science, or NIDS. It was a privately funded group. Its staff included physicists with Ph.D. degrees, former police officers, veterinarians, and biochemists. The NIDS study ran for about eight years, until the group closed in 2004.

NIDS researchers wrote down many strange events. They reported more cattle deaths that had no ordinary explanation. They reported glowing orbs and craft in the sky. Several witnesses described “windows,” orange round openings in the sky that seemed to hold a whole scene behind them. Instruments that measure electric and magnetic fields gave odd readings. GPS devices went wrong. Cameras and other electronics failed. In some cases, researchers said the events seemed to react to being watched, as if something knew it was being observed.

The main written record is the book Hunt for the Skinwalker (2005). Its authors are Dr. Colm A. Kelleher, a NIDS biochemist, and George Knapp. The book tells of hundreds of reported events, including the Sherman family’s first experiences and the team’s own.

It describes noises and movements inside the ranch house like those of a poltergeist, a ghost that moves things. It describes creatures that scientists call cryptids, which are animals not known to science. It also tells of NIDS researchers who watched a yellowish, see-through opening in the sky. A large, dark object with a clear shape seemed to come out of it before it closed.

In 2008, the story took a big turn. The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA), a US government agency, gave a secret $22 million contract to Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS). BAASS belonged to Bigelow’s space company. Its job was to run a program called the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP).

The program could study more than unknown flying objects. It could look at odd events in space, under water, and on the ground. That included the kind of events reported at Skinwalker Ranch.

AAWSAP ran from 2008 to 2012 and produced dozens of secret reports. Some were later made public. A news investigation in 2017 revealed the program. It showed that the Pentagon had been involved in UFO research, with links to Bigelow and the ranch. It was surprising to learn that the US Department of Defense had secretly spent public money for years on a supposedly haunted ranch in Utah.

In 2016, Bigelow sold the ranch to Brandon Fugal. Fugal is a well-known Utah real estate businessman and the chairman of Colliers International in Utah. He bought it through his company, Adamantium Real Estate LLC. Since then he has spent millions of dollars on a long scientific study.

Fugal built a research team. It includes Dr. Travis Taylor, an aerospace engineer and light physicist who has worked on Department of Defense programs. It also includes Erik Bard, a physicist and data scientist.

The team uses many tools. They have sensors for electric and magnetic fields, radiation detectors, heat cameras, LIDAR (which measures distance with laser light), and ground-penetrating radar. They report odd readings, including sudden jumps in radiation, GPS failures, and local electromagnetic interference. In 2020, the work became the History Channel series The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch. It has run for several seasons and reached millions of viewers.

Skinwalker Ranch holds a special place in the study of the unexplained. It is one of the most carefully studied sites of its kind. It is also one of the most strongly criticized. Skeptics point out that there are no papers checked by other scientists. They note that the evidence is mostly stories told by people, and that the entertainment business is involved. Defenders reply that these events are hard to study with usual methods. They say the events come without warning, avoid instruments, and may react to watchers, which makes controlled tests nearly impossible.

The link to the wider government study of UAP, unidentified aerial phenomena, gives the ranch more credit than most such places have. The DIA was willing to spend $22 million. That suggests someone in national security took the reports seriously enough to commit real money. Does that show the events are real? Or does it only show that the government will look into odd claims? People still read this fact in different ways.

Some say Skinwalker Ranch is the most important unexplained place on Earth. Others say it shows how money, media, and belief can turn a bare piece of Utah desert into a world-famous story. The evidence is unclear. It holds many stories and much instrument data, but few repeatable results checked by other scientists, which science requires. The Pentagon’s $22 million and the skilled researchers give the ranch more weight than most claims of this kind.

The Navajo and Ute traditions also remind us of something. Native peoples spoke of strange events in the Uintah Basin long before the Sherman family arrived. Maybe the ranch is a doorway to another world. Maybe it draws the imagination of those who visit. Maybe it is just a very unusual piece of land. Whatever it is, the mystery there is still active and still unfolding.
