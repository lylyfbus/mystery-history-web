Skinwalker Ranch: The 480-Acre Property in Utah Where Paranormal Activity Became a Government Secret

A wolf that would not fall

A cattle rancher named Terry Sherman told a strange story. He said that he met a wolf three times the size of a normal wolf. He said that he shot it several times from close range, and nothing happened to it.

That is his story. Nobody has proved it. But it made a quiet ranch in Utah famous around the world. Today people call the place Skinwalker Ranch. Let us find out how a piece of high desert became a legend.

A quiet ranch that did not stay quiet

In the mid-1990s, Terry Sherman and his wife Gwen bought a ranch of about 512 acres near Ballard, Utah. It lay southeast of the town, in a remote area called the Uintah Basin. The land was lovely. It was high desert with wide views of the Uinta Mountains, and the Shermans found the quiet peaceful. They moved their family there, expecting a calm country life.

Within months, the peace was gone, the Shermans later said. They told of things that they could not explain. They said that some cattle were found dead with neat, bloodless cuts. They described strange lights and craft in the sky. They also spoke of animals with red eyes and of machines that failed for no reason. These are their claims, and nobody has proved them.

The Shermans stayed about eighteen months. In 1996, they told their story to the Deseret News, and a reporter named George Knapp wrote more about it. Then they sold the ranch. But where did the strange name come from?

The name and the old stories

The name comes from the Navajo (Diné) idea of the skin-walker. One Navajo term is yee naaldlooshii, which means “by means of it, it goes on all fours.” In Navajo belief, a skin-walker is an evil witch. The stories say that such a witch can change into an animal. The animal is often a coyote. Real Navajo healers are the opposite of these witches.

In the stories, skin-walkers bring fear and harm. Many traditional Navajo people do not like to talk about them with outsiders. For them, it is a private and serious subject.

The ranch is next to land of the Ute people, who have their own traditions. One historian says that the Utes saw some springs and waters in the area as sources of bad power. She also says that the Utes and Navajo were often enemies, so some people ask whether a Navajo name fits this place. UFO reports from the Uintah Basin were in the news in the 1970s.

So the name is much older than the ranch. A Las Vegas businessman soon made the ranch more famous still.

A businessman starts a hunt

Robert Bigelow bought the ranch in 1996. He studied it with the National Institute for Discovery Science, or NIDS. It was a privately funded group, and it watched the ranch for several years.

NIDS researchers said that they wrote down many strange events. They told of cattle deaths, glowing orbs, red-eyed animals, and odd magnetic readings. Some said that the events seemed to react to being watched. Other writers looked at the same work and found no strong physical proof for the stories of the Shermans.

The main written record is the book Hunt for the Skinwalker, published in 2005. Its authors are Dr. Colm A. Kelleher, a NIDS biochemist, and George Knapp. They say that they looked into close to 100 incidents. They also admit that it was hard to get evidence good enough for a science journal.

In one story from 1997, Kelleher said that he saw a large, human-shaped figure in a tree. He said that it vanished after he fired at it. He found a large oval track on the ground. Then the story took a big turn.

The government joins in

The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA), a US government agency, began a program called AAWSAP. The long name is the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program. Reports differ on the start year, but it ran from about 2007 until 2012. It cost about $22 million. A company of Bigelow, called Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies, held a contract in it. One account says that a DIA official had visited the ranch first.

The Department of Defense says that the program ended in 2012. It paid for dozens of studies, and some were made public. In December 2017, news reports revealed the program. People were surprised that the Pentagon had paid for UFO research.

In 2016, Bigelow sold the ranch to Brandon Fugal. He is a Utah real estate businessman and the chairman of Colliers International in Utah. He bought it through a company called Adamantium Real Estate LLC. Since then, he has paid for a new study of the ranch.

Fugal built a research team that includes Dr. Travis Taylor and Erik Bard. The team says that it uses many kinds of sensors and cameras and reports odd readings. In 2020, the work became the History Channel series The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch. It has run for several seasons. So what do people think is going on?

Idea one: the stories are not real

The ranch is strongly criticized. One skeptic, Robert Sheaffer, thinks that the events are almost certainly not real. He suggests that the Sherman family made up the stories.

Skeptics also point out that the evidence is mostly stories told by people, and that the entertainment business is involved. The book admits that good evidence was hard to get. And other writers found no strong physical proof.

The government link does not settle the matter. The program cost about $22 million. Does that show that the events are real? Or does it only show that the government will look into odd claims? People still read this fact in different ways.

Idea two: events that hide from tests

Defenders reply that these events are hard to study with usual methods. They say that the events come without warning, avoid instruments, and may react to watchers. That, they say, makes controlled tests nearly impossible.

Some people say that Skinwalker Ranch is an important unexplained place. The link to the government study of UAP, unidentified aerial phenomena, gives the ranch more attention than most such places have. But the evidence is unclear. The ranch holds many stories, but few results that other scientists have checked.

Others say that the ranch shows how money, media, and belief can turn a bare piece of Utah desert into a world-famous story.

Back to the wolf

So let us return to Terry Sherman and his wolf. Here is what we know. A family said that strange things happened on their ranch. A businessman, a government program, and a new owner have all studied it. Here is what is open. The evidence is unclear, and few results have been checked by other scientists.

The Ute traditions also remind us of something. People told stories about strange lights in the Uintah Basin long before the Shermans arrived. One writer tells of fireballs seen there in 1776.

Maybe the ranch is a doorway to another world. Maybe it draws the imagination of those who visit. Maybe it is just a very unusual piece of land. Whatever it is, the mystery there is still active and still unfolding.
