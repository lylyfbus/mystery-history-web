Skinwalker Ranch: The 480-Acre Property in Utah Where Paranormal Activity Became a Government Secret

In the mid-1990s, a cattle rancher named Terry Sherman and his wife Gwen bought a ranch of about 512 acres near Ballard, Utah. It lay southeast of the town, in a remote area called the Uintah Basin. The land was lovely. It was high desert with wide views of the Uinta Mountains, and the Shermans found the quiet peaceful. They moved their family there, expecting a calm country life.

Within months, the peace was gone, the Shermans later said. They told of things they could not explain. They said some cattle were found dead with neat, bloodless cuts. They described strange lights and craft in the sky. They also spoke of animals with red eyes and of machines that failed for no reason. These are their claims. Nobody has proved them.

The story that made the ranch famous is about a wolf. Terry Sherman said he met a wolf three times the size of a normal wolf. He said he shot it several times from close range, with no effect.

The Shermans stayed about eighteen months. In 1996 they told their story to the Deseret News, and a reporter named George Knapp wrote more about it. Then they sold the ranch. A Las Vegas businessman named Robert Bigelow bought it. He began a private investigation that became very unusual and much argued over. People called the place Skinwalker Ranch, after shape-shifting witches in Navajo legend.

The name comes from the Navajo (Diné) idea of the skin-walker. One Navajo term is yee naaldlooshii, which means “by means of it, it goes on all fours.” In Navajo belief, a skin-walker is an evil witch. The stories say such a witch can change into an animal. Many retellings say a witch must do terrible things to gain this power. The animal is often a coyote. Other animals linked to death or bad luck are named too. Real Navajo healers are the opposite of these witches.

In the stories, skin-walkers bring fear and harm. Many traditional Navajo people do not like to talk about them with outsiders. For them it is a private and serious subject.

The ranch is next to land of the Ute people, who have their own traditions. One historian says the Utes saw some springs and waters in the area as sources of bad power. She also says the Utes and Navajo were often enemies, so some people ask whether a Navajo name fits this place. UFO reports from the Uintah Basin were in the news in the 1970s.

Bigelow bought the ranch in 1996 and studied it with the National Institute for Discovery Science, or NIDS. It was a privately funded group. It watched the ranch for several years.

NIDS researchers said they wrote down many strange events. They told of cattle deaths, glowing orbs, red-eyed animals, and odd magnetic readings. Some said the events seemed to react to being watched. Other writers looked at the same work and found no strong physical proof for the Shermans’ stories.

The main written record is the book Hunt for the Skinwalker (2005). Its authors are Dr. Colm A. Kelleher, a NIDS biochemist, and George Knapp. They say they looked into close to 100 incidents. They also admit it was hard to get evidence good enough for a science journal.

In one story from 1997, Kelleher said he saw a large, human-shaped figure in a tree. He said it vanished after he fired at it. He found a large oval track on the ground.

Then the story took a big turn. The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA), a US government agency, began a program called the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP). Reports differ on the start year, but it ran from about 2007 until 2012. It cost about $22 million. A company of Bigelow’s, Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS), held a contract in it. One account says a DIA official had visited the ranch first.

The Department of Defense says the program ended in 2012. It paid for dozens of studies, and some were made public. In December 2017, news reports revealed the program. People were surprised that the Pentagon had paid for UFO research.

In 2016, Bigelow sold the ranch to Brandon Fugal. Fugal is a Utah real estate businessman and the chairman of Colliers International in Utah. He bought it through a company called Adamantium Real Estate LLC. Since then he has paid for a new study of the ranch.

Fugal built a research team. It includes Dr. Travis Taylor and Erik Bard. The team says it uses many kinds of sensors and cameras and reports odd readings. In 2020, the work became the History Channel series The Secret of Skinwalker Ranch. It has run for several seasons.

Skinwalker Ranch holds a special place in the study of the unexplained. It is also strongly criticized. One skeptic, Robert Sheaffer, thinks the events are almost certainly not real. He suggests the Sherman family made up the stories. Skeptics also point out that the evidence is mostly stories told by people, and that the entertainment business is involved. Defenders reply that these events are hard to study with usual methods. They say the events come without warning, avoid instruments, and may react to watchers, which makes controlled tests nearly impossible.

The link to the government study of UAP, unidentified aerial phenomena, gives the ranch more attention than most such places have. The program cost about $22 million. Does that show the events are real? Or does it only show that the government will look into odd claims? People still read this fact in different ways.

Some say Skinwalker Ranch is an important unexplained place. Others say it shows how money, media, and belief can turn a bare piece of Utah desert into a world-famous story. The evidence is unclear. It holds many stories, but few results that other scientists have checked.

The Ute traditions also remind us of something. People told stories about strange lights in the Uintah Basin long before the Shermans arrived. One writer tells of fireballs seen there in 1776. Maybe the ranch is a doorway to another world. Maybe it draws the imagination of those who visit. Maybe it is just a very unusual piece of land. Whatever it is, the mystery there is still active and still unfolding.
