The Curse of Tutankhamun: Myth, Media, and the Science Behind Egypt's Most Famous Legend

On November 4, 1922, a team of Egyptian workers was digging in the Valley of the Kings. Their leader was the British archaeologist Howard Carter. That day they uncovered a step cut into the solid rock.

The step led down to a sealed door. On the door were cartouches, the oval signs that hold a king’s name in Egyptian writing. They named a pharaoh the world had nearly forgotten. Behind the door lay Tutankhamun.

Tutankhamun was a boy-king. He died around 1323 BCE, when he was about nineteen years old. His tomb was packed with more than five thousand objects. There were golden chariots and jewelry covered with precious stones. There was a beautiful death mask of solid gold.

The king’s mummy lay inside a nest of coffins, one inside the next. The innermost coffin was made of 110 kilograms of solid gold. It was the most dazzling find of the twentieth century in archaeology. It is the only royal burial from ancient Egypt ever found almost whole.

Then, within weeks, the story turned darker. Lord Carnarvon had paid for Carter’s work. He died in Cairo on April 5, 1923, just weeks after the grand opening of the tomb.

People said that all the lights of Cairo went out at the moment he died. Back in England, his dog was said to have howled and died at the very same time. The newspapers went wild. “The Curse of Tutankhamun” was born.

The curse has lasted for more than a century and inspired films and novels. But the truth is more interesting than the myth. The real story tells us as much about ourselves as about the ancient Egyptians. It is about newspapers that stretch the truth. It is about why people believe in bad luck. It is about real germs that may hide in old tombs. And it is about how people look for patterns and meaning in events that happen by chance.

Howard Carter had been digging in Egypt since he was seventeen years old. By 1922, he was forty-eight. He had searched the Valley of the Kings for fifteen years. George Herbert, the 5th Earl of Carnarvon, paid for the search. He was a rich English nobleman, and he had spent a fortune on the hunt for an unknown royal tomb.

Carnarvon was losing patience. Year after year, Carter had found little of importance. The earl was ready to stop paying. Carter talked him into one last season.

On November 4, 1922, the workers found the step. By the next day, they had cleared a staircase. It went down to a plastered door that carried the seals of the royal burial ground.

Carter sent a telegram to Carnarvon at once. It said: “At last have made wonderful discovery in Valley a magnificent tomb with seals intact re-covered same for your arrival congratulations.”

Carnarvon reached Egypt on November 23. On November 26, Carter made a small hole in the second door. He pushed a candle through and looked inside. Carnarvon asked if he could see anything. Carter is said to have answered: “Yes, wonderful things.”

He was looking into the first room of the tomb. It was stuffed with couches covered in gold, jars of pale alabaster stone, chariots, and golden shrines. This was the treasure of a pharaoh. Nobody had touched it for more than three thousand years.

The find made Tutankhamun the most famous pharaoh in the world. That is a strange fate for a king who ruled for only about ten years and died in his teens. Historians remember him mostly for one act. Before him, the pharaoh Akhenaten had turned Egypt upside down by ordering the worship of a single god. Tutankhamun brought the old Egyptian religion back.

Lord Carnarvon died on April 5, 1923, in the Continental-Savoy Hotel in Cairo. It was less than six months after the grand opening of the tomb. Doctors said the cause was blood poisoning. A mosquito had bitten his cheek, and the bite had become infected. Then he caught pneumonia, a serious illness of the lungs.

He was fifty-six years old, and he had been in poor health for years. He had a long-lasting breathing problem. In fact, that was why his doctors had sent him to the dry air of Egypt in the first place.

So a sickly older man died from an infected insect bite. By any sensible measure, that was not strange. But the press turned it into something amazing.

The story said that all the lights in Cairo went out when he died. In truth, Cairo’s electricity was famous for failing, and blackouts were common.

The story also said that his much-loved dog howled and dropped dead at the exact moment her master died. She was at Highclere Castle, his home in Hampshire, England. Nobody can check whether this story is true.

The newspapers grabbed these details. The London Daily Mail and its pushy reporter Arthur Merton led the way. They wove the details into a tale of revenge from the spirit world.

The idea of a pharaoh’s curse was hard to resist. It had the glamour of ancient Egypt and the thrill of magic. It also gave readers a feeling of justice. Proud modern men had broken into the resting place of the dead, and now they were being punished.

The person who did the most to spread the story was Sir Arthur Conan Doyle. He was the creator of Sherlock Holmes. He also believed strongly in spirits and in contact with the dead.

Conan Doyle said in public that “elementals” might have caused Carnarvon’s death. By this he meant evil spirits that Egyptian priests had set to guard the tomb. His words were printed around the world. Now the curse had the support of one of the most famous writers in the English language.

Other newspapers made things up or stretched them. Many reported that carved warnings in the tomb promised doom to anyone who entered. No such writing existed in Tutankhamun’s tomb. Reporters also collected the death of anyone with even a small link to the dig. Each one was blamed on the curse.

In 2002, a study appeared in the British Medical Journal. It looked at 44 Westerners who were in Egypt during the dig. Records showed which of them had been in contact with the tomb. The result was clear. Contact with the tomb had no link with an early death.

Of the 44 people, 25 had been in contact with the tomb. Their average age at death was 70 years. Lord Carnarvon was the only person close to the dig who died within the first year.

What about the others? George Jay Gould was an American money man who visited the tomb. He died of pneumonia in May 1923, after catching a cold. Prince Ali Kamel Fahmy Bey had also visited the tomb. His wife shot and killed him in July 1923.

Arthur Mace was a member of Carter’s team. He died of pneumonia in 1928, after years of failing health. Richard Bethell was Carter’s secretary. He was found dead in his bed in 1929, and the cause was given as a failure of his blood circulation.

And what about Howard Carter himself? He spent more time in the tomb than anyone. He lived until 1939. He died at the age of sixty-four of lymphoma, a kind of cancer. That was seventeen years after he opened the tomb.

If any person on Earth had been “cursed,” it would have been Carter. He was not. The numbers are plain. Opening Tutankhamun’s tomb was not dangerous.

The curse was made by the newspapers. It lasted because people remembered the deaths and forgot the many who lived. It also lasted because a story of revenge from the grave is so exciting to tell.

Still, this does not mean that old tombs are harmless. Since the 1960s, scientists have suggested natural reasons why some people fell ill after entering Egyptian tombs.

The best-known idea is about harmful fungi. A fungus is a living thing like a mold. Scientists point to a group called Aspergillus, with types such as Aspergillus niger and Aspergillus flavus. These can cause serious lung infections in people whose bodies are already weak.

Such molds grow well in warm, dark, closed places. A sealed tomb is exactly that. When a tomb is opened after thousands of years, tiny mold seeds called spores can float into the air. People may breathe them in. This can lead to aspergillosis, a dangerous infection of the lungs.

Scientists have suggested other dangers too. Some bacteria form tough spores that can live for centuries. Rotting materials can give off ammonia gas. And the ancient Egyptians used poisonous chemicals when they made mummies.

But nobody has tied any of these to a real death in the Tutankhamun story. No one has shown that his tomb held unusual amounts of germs. And many of the “curse deaths” clearly had nothing to do with germs at all. So the scientific idea is possible, but in this case it is not proven.

The curse lives on because it speaks to the fear of death and to the old rule against disturbing the dead. That fear is very deep. Almost every human culture has its own customs and beliefs about how to treat the bodies of the dead.

It also answers a wish for justice. It is comforting to think that people who break into holy places will be punished. And it uses the Western world’s old love of ancient Egypt as a land of magic, mystery, and hidden power.

Last of all, it makes money. Curse stories sell newspapers, books, movie tickets, and museum passes. So the curse of Tutankhamun is not really a story about ancient Egypt. It is a story about the modern world and how it treats the past. Wanting, guilt, and profit all play a part.

Carter’s find was a victory of patience, skill, and hard work. Yet many people do not think first of beauty, art, or history when they hear the name Tutankhamun. They think of a curse that never existed. Reporters invented it. A famous writer of detective stories made it bigger. And our love of a good ghost story kept it alive.

The real lesson is not about ancient magic. It is about how easily people today believe things. A strong story can beat the evidence. We see patterns in events that happen by chance. And we very much want to believe that the dead can reach across the centuries and touch the living.

The pharaohs had no need of curses. The living were able to invent their own. So one question is left for us. Why do we still want the curse to be true?
