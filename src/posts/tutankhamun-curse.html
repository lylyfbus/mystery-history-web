The Curse of Tutankhamun: Myth, Media, and the Science Behind Egypt's Most Famous Legend

A step in the rock

It is November 4, 1922, in the Valley of the Kings in Egypt. A team of Egyptian workers is digging. Their leader is the British archaeologist Howard Carter. That day, they uncover a step cut into the solid rock.

In the next days, they clear a whole staircase. It leads down to a sealed door. Seals of the royal burial ground are pressed into the plaster. Later, the team found that the seals bore the name of a pharaoh the world had nearly forgotten.

His name was Tutankhamun. And the man who paid for this dig was almost ready to give up.

One last season

Carter had been digging in Egypt since he was seventeen years old. By 1922, he was forty-eight. He worked for George Herbert, the 5th Earl of Carnarvon, a rich English nobleman. Carnarvon had spent a fortune on the hunt for an unknown royal tomb.

But year after year, Carter had found little of importance. The earl was losing patience and was ready to stop paying. Carter offered to pay for the last stretch of digging himself. Carnarvon agreed to one more season.

Carter sent a telegram to Carnarvon in England at once. It told him that Carter had made a wonderful discovery and that the seals were still in place. Carnarvon reached Egypt on November 23. What happened next made history.

Wonderful things

On November 26, Carter made a small hole in the second door. He pushed a candle through and looked inside. Carnarvon asked if he could see anything. Carter is said to have answered: “Yes, wonderful things.”

He was looking into the first room of the tomb. It was stuffed with couches covered in gold, jars of pale alabaster stone, chariots, and golden shrines. It had stayed mostly undisturbed for more than three thousand years.

Tutankhamun was a boy-king. He died around 1323 BCE, in his late teens or early twenties. His tomb held more than five thousand numbered objects. There were golden chariots, jewelry covered with precious stones, and a beautiful death mask of solid gold.

The king’s mummy lay inside a nest of coffins, one inside the next. The innermost coffin was made mostly of solid gold and weighed about 110 kilograms. Few royal burials from ancient Egypt have been found almost untouched. But the joy did not last long.

Death in a Cairo hotel

Lord Carnarvon died in a hotel in Cairo on April 5, 1923. That was less than two months after the inner burial chamber was opened. He was fifty-six years old.

Doctors said the cause was blood poisoning. A mosquito had bitten his cheek, and the bite had become infected. Then he caught pneumonia, a serious illness of the lungs. He had been in poor health for years, and his doctors had sent him to the dry air of Egypt for his lungs.

So an older, sickly man died from an infected insect bite. That is sad, but it is not strange. The newspapers, however, saw something else. How did they turn a sad death into a tale of magic?

How the story grew

People said that all the lights of Cairo went out at the moment he died. Some accounts do describe a power cut in the city that night. But power cuts were common in Cairo at that time, so it need not mean anything.

People also said that his dog howled and dropped dead at the very same time. She was at Highclere Castle, his home in Hampshire, England. Nobody can check whether this story is true.

Reporters in London and elsewhere wove these details into a tale of revenge from the spirit world. “The Curse of Tutankhamun” was born.

A famous voice helped. Sir Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes, believed strongly in spirits. He said in public that it was possible an evil spirit had killed Carnarvon. His words were printed around the world.

Other newspapers made things up. Some reported carved warnings in the tomb that promised doom to anyone who entered. No such curse has been found in the tomb. Reporters also counted the death of anyone with even a small link to the dig. Each one was blamed on the curse.

Was there any truth in it? A scientist decided to count.

What the numbers say

In 2002, a study appeared in the British Medical Journal. A researcher named Mark Nelson used Carter’s diaries and old records. He compared Westerners who entered the tomb with others on the trip who did not.

The result was clear. The people who entered the tomb died at an average age of about 70. The others lived to about 75. That difference was too small to prove anything.

What about the people named in the stories? George Jay Gould was an American railway man who visited the tomb. He died of illness in May 1923, about a month after Carnarvon. Richard Bethell was Carter’s secretary. He was found dead in 1929. Reports first named a heart problem, and later reports disagree.

And what about Howard Carter himself? He worked in and around the tomb for years. If any person had been “cursed,” it would have been Carter. He lived until 1939. He died at the age of sixty-four of lymphoma, a kind of cancer. That was seventeen years after he opened the tomb.

The numbers are plain. Opening the tomb was not shown to be dangerous. But some scientists asked a different question. Could something real in an old tomb make people ill?

Could tiny germs be to blame?

The best-known idea is about harmful fungi. A fungus is a living thing like a mold. A group called Aspergillus is often named. These molds can cause serious lung infections in people whose bodies are already weak.

Such molds grow well in warm, dark, closed places. A sealed tomb is exactly that. When a tomb is opened after thousands of years, tiny mold seeds called spores can float into the air. People may breathe them in. This can lead to aspergillosis, a dangerous infection of the lungs.

Some researchers did find Aspergillus flavus in a tomb in Poland that was opened in 1973. But it was not found in Tutankhamun’s tomb. Other dangers have been suggested too, but none has been shown for this tomb.

Nobody has tied any of these to a real death in the Tutankhamun story. And many of the “curse deaths” had nothing to do with germs at all. So the idea is possible, but in this case it is not proven.

Why we still tell the story

If the curse was never shown to be real, why does it live on? Think about what the story gives us.

It speaks to our fear of death and to the old rule against disturbing the dead. Almost every human culture has its own customs about how to treat the bodies of the dead. It also feels fair. It is comforting to think that people who break into holy places will be punished.

It lasted because people remembered the deaths and forgot the many who lived. And it makes money. Curse stories sell newspapers, books, movie tickets, and museum passes.

So here is what we know. A boy-king’s tomb was found in 1922. Lord Carnarvon died a few months later, of an infected bite and a lung illness. No curse has been found in the tomb, and the numbers show no curse at work. Fungi are an idea, but not a proven one.

Think again of that first step in the rock. Carter’s find was a victory of patience, skill, and hard work. Yet many people hear the name Tutankhamun and think first of a curse. The pharaohs had no need of curses. The living were able to invent their own. So one question is left for us. Why do we still want the curse to be true?
