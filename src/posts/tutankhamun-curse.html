The Curse of Tutankhamun: Myth, Media, and the Science Behind Egypt's Most Famous Legend

On November 4, 1922, a team of Egyptian workers was digging in the Valley of the Kings. Their leader was the British archaeologist Howard Carter. That day they uncovered a step cut into the solid rock.

The step led down to a sealed door. Seals of the royal burial ground were pressed into the plaster. Later, the team found that the seals bore the name of a pharaoh the world had nearly forgotten. Behind the doors lay Tutankhamun.

Tutankhamun was a boy-king. He died around 1323 BCE, when he was in his late teens or early twenties. His tomb held more than five thousand numbered objects. There were golden chariots and jewelry covered with precious stones. There was a beautiful death mask of solid gold.

The king’s mummy lay inside a nest of coffins, one inside the next. The innermost coffin was made mostly of solid gold and weighed about 110 kilograms. It was one of the most famous finds of the twentieth century in archaeology. It is also one of the few royal burials from ancient Egypt found almost untouched.

Then, within weeks, the story turned darker. Lord Carnarvon had paid for Carter’s work. He died in Cairo on April 5, 1923, less than two months after the inner burial chamber was opened.

People said that all the lights of Cairo went out at the moment he died. Back in England, his dog was said to have howled and died at the very same time. The newspapers went wild. “The Curse of Tutankhamun” was born.

The curse has lasted for more than a century and inspired films and novels. But the truth is more interesting than the myth. The real story tells us as much about ourselves as about the ancient Egyptians. It is about newspapers that stretch the truth. It is about why people believe in bad luck. It is about real germs that may hide in old tombs. And it is about how people look for patterns and meaning in events that happen by chance.

Howard Carter had been digging in Egypt since he was seventeen years old. By 1922, he was forty-eight. He had searched the Valley of the Kings for years. George Herbert, the 5th Earl of Carnarvon, paid for the search. He was a rich English nobleman, and he had spent a fortune on the hunt for an unknown royal tomb.

Carnarvon was losing patience. Year after year, Carter had found little of importance. The earl was ready to stop paying. Carter offered to pay for the last stretch of digging himself, and Carnarvon agreed to one more season.

On November 4, 1922, the workers found the step. In the next days, they cleared a staircase. It went down to a plastered door that carried the seals of the royal burial ground.

Carter sent a telegram to Carnarvon in England at once. It told him that Carter had made a wonderful discovery and that the seals were still in place.

Carnarvon reached Egypt on November 23. On November 26, Carter made a small hole in the second door. He pushed a candle through and looked inside. Carnarvon asked if he could see anything. Carter is said to have answered: “Yes, wonderful things.”

He was looking into the first room of the tomb. It was stuffed with couches covered in gold, jars of pale alabaster stone, chariots, and golden shrines. This was the treasure of a pharaoh. It had stayed mostly undisturbed for more than three thousand years.

The find made Tutankhamun the most famous pharaoh in the world. That is a strange fate for a king who ruled for only about ten years and died young. Historians remember him mostly for one act. Before him, the pharaoh Akhenaten had turned Egypt upside down by ordering the worship of a single god. Tutankhamun is credited with bringing the old Egyptian religion back.

Lord Carnarvon died on April 5, 1923, in a hotel in Cairo. It was less than six months after the tomb was found. Doctors said the cause was blood poisoning. A mosquito had bitten his cheek, and the bite had become infected. Then he caught pneumonia, a serious illness of the lungs.

He was fifty-six years old, and he had been in poor health for years. His doctors had sent him to the dry air of Egypt for his lungs.

So a sickly older man died from an infected insect bite. By any sensible measure, that was not strange. But the press turned it into something amazing.

The story said that all the lights in Cairo went out when he died. Some accounts do describe a power cut in the city that night. But power cuts were common in Cairo at that time, so it need not mean anything.

The story also said that his much-loved dog howled and dropped dead at the exact moment her master died. She was at Highclere Castle, his home in Hampshire, England. Nobody can check whether this story is true.

The newspapers grabbed these details. Reporters in London and elsewhere wove them into a tale of revenge from the spirit world.

The idea of a pharaoh’s curse was hard to resist. It had the glamour of ancient Egypt and the thrill of magic. It also gave readers a feeling of justice. Proud modern men had broken into the resting place of the dead, and now they were being punished.

A well-known voice helped spread the story. Sir Arthur Conan Doyle was the creator of Sherlock Holmes. He also believed strongly in spirits and in contact with the dead.

Conan Doyle said in public that it was possible an evil spirit had killed Carnarvon. His words were printed around the world. Now the curse had the support of one of the most famous writers in the English language.

Other newspapers made things up or stretched them. Some reported that carved warnings in the tomb promised doom to anyone who entered. No such curse has been found in Tutankhamun’s tomb. Reporters also counted the death of anyone with even a small link to the dig. Each one was blamed on the curse.

In 2002, a study appeared in the British Medical Journal. A researcher named Mark Nelson used Carter’s diaries and old records. He compared Westerners who entered the tomb with others on the trip who did not. The result was clear. Entering the tomb had no clear link with an early death.

The people who entered the tomb died at an average age of about 70. The others lived to about 75. That difference was too small to prove anything.

What about the others? George Jay Gould was an American railway man who visited the tomb. He died of illness in May 1923, about a month after Carnarvon. Richard Bethell was Carter’s secretary. He was found dead in 1929. Reports first named a heart problem, and later reports disagree.

And what about Howard Carter himself? He worked in and around the tomb for years. He lived until 1939. He died at the age of sixty-four of lymphoma, a kind of cancer. That was seventeen years after he opened the tomb.

If any person on Earth had been “cursed,” it would have been Carter. He was not. The numbers are plain. Opening Tutankhamun’s tomb was not shown to be dangerous.

The curse was helped along by the newspapers. It lasted because people remembered the deaths and forgot the many who lived. It also lasted because a story of revenge from the grave is so exciting to tell.

Still, this does not mean that old tombs are harmless. Scientists have suggested natural reasons why some people might fall ill after entering old tombs.

The best-known idea is about harmful fungi. A fungus is a living thing like a mold. A group called Aspergillus is often named. These molds can cause serious lung infections in people whose bodies are already weak.

Such molds grow well in warm, dark, closed places. A sealed tomb is exactly that. When a tomb is opened after thousands of years, tiny mold seeds called spores can float into the air. People may breathe them in. This can lead to aspergillosis, a dangerous infection of the lungs.

Some researchers did find Aspergillus flavus in a tomb in Poland that was opened in 1973. But it was not found in Tutankhamun’s tomb. Other dangers have been suggested too, but none has been shown for this tomb.

Nobody has tied any of these to a real death in the Tutankhamun story. And many of the “curse deaths” clearly had nothing to do with germs at all. So the scientific idea is possible, but in this case it is not proven.

The curse lives on because it speaks to the fear of death and to the old rule against disturbing the dead. That fear is very deep. Almost every human culture has its own customs and beliefs about how to treat the bodies of the dead.

It also answers a wish for justice. It is comforting to think that people who break into holy places will be punished. And it uses the Western world’s old love of ancient Egypt as a land of magic, mystery, and hidden power.

Last of all, it makes money. Curse stories sell newspapers, books, movie tickets, and museum passes. So the curse of Tutankhamun is not really a story about ancient Egypt. It is a story about the modern world and how it treats the past. Wanting, guilt, and profit all play a part.

Carter’s find was a victory of patience, skill, and hard work. Yet many people do not think first of beauty, art, or history when they hear the name Tutankhamun. They think of a curse that has never been shown to exist. Newspapers helped spread it. A famous writer of detective stories made it bigger. And our love of a good ghost story kept it alive.

The real lesson is not about ancient magic. It is about how easily people today believe things. A strong story can beat the evidence. We see patterns in events that happen by chance. And we very much want to believe that the dead can reach across the centuries and touch the living.

The pharaohs had no need of curses. The living were able to invent their own. So one question is left for us. Why do we still want the curse to be true?
