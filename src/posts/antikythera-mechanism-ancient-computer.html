The Antikythera Mechanism: An Ancient Greek Computer 1,500 Years Ahead of Its Time

The Antikythera Mechanism, recovered from a Roman shipwreck in 1901, is the world’s oldest known analog computer — built over 2,000 years ago

In the spring of 1901, a team of Greek sponge divers was working at a Roman-era shipwreck. It lay off the tiny island of Antikythera, a rocky island between Crete and the Peloponnese. From the seabed, the divers lifted a shapeless, rusty lump of bronze about the size of a large book.

The lump was almost overlooked. The wreck dates to about 70–60 BCE. It had already given up great treasures. There were marble and bronze statues, glassware, jewelry, and coins. One of the statues was a marble Hercules. The lump was covered with two thousand years of sea crust. It looked like nothing important.

It was listed and put in a storage crate at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Then it was mostly forgotten. It sat in the museum for months. Then, in 1902, someone noticed a gear in the crust. Inside were the outlines of tiny, exact bronze gears, with ancient Greek writing on them.

The divers had not found a statue or a tool. They had found a machine. It is one of the most advanced technical objects to survive from the ancient world.

The Antikythera Mechanism, as it came to be called, was an analog computer. That means a machine that works with gears and dials instead of electronics. Experts think it was built sometime between about 205 and 87 BCE. It could predict eclipses of the Sun and Moon. It showed the Sun and Moon moving through the zodiac, the band of sky where the planets travel. Researchers think it showed the five known planets too. It tracked the cycle of the ancient Olympic Games. It even copied the uneven movement of the Moon across the sky.

Nothing like it is known in Europe for more than a thousand years afterward. Researchers such as Tony Freeth of University College London have spent years trying to understand it.

The mechanism challenges what we thought we knew about the ancient Greeks. We are often told that they were brilliant thinkers and mathematicians, but poor engineers. They gave us geometry and drama, not clockwork. The mechanism shows that idea was wrong, at least in part.

About 30 gears that fit into each other survive. The teeth were cut by hand. The gears sat in a wooden case about 34 centimeters tall.

It also holds clever mathematics. One example is the Metonic cycle of 235 lunar months. Another is the Saros eclipse cycle of 223 lunar months. A third is the Callippic cycle of 940 lunar months. Machines like this did not appear in Europe until the great astronomical clocks of the fourteenth century.

So the mechanism seems impossible. But it exists. And we still cannot fully explain how it was made, or who made it.

The story of its discovery begins with sponge divers, not scientists. In 1900, a crew of Greek sponge fishermen was sailing home to the island of Symi. Their captain was Dimitrios Kondos. A storm forced them to take shelter near Antikythera.

While they waited for the weather to clear, a diver named Elias Stadiatis went down to the seabed. He found the wreck of an ancient ship. It lay about 45 meters (150 feet) deep. Statues, amphorae, and other objects lay scattered all around.

Kondos told the Greek authorities. From late 1900 into 1901, Greek Navy ships helped the divers to bring up the finds.

The work was very dangerous. The divers wore early diving suits and went down to a hard depth with poor sight. They collected objects by hand. Many divers got decompression sickness, also called the bends, which comes from coming up too fast from deep water. One diver died and two others were paralyzed.

The haul was spectacular. It had bronze and marble statues, including the famous Antikythera Ephebe and the Philosopher. It had pottery, glassware, coins, and jewelry. It also had the rusty bronze lump that would become the most important find of all.

At first the mechanism was just a lump among many finds. Few people paid attention. In 1902, the archaeologist Valerios Stais looked at it closely. He saw that it held a gear. That was when people began to see how important it was.

For decades, scholars could see the mechanism but could not read it. The remains came in 82 pieces. About 30 gear wheels survive. They were too rusty and fragile to take apart or clean.

A big step came with Derek J. de Solla Price, a British science historian. He began studying the mechanism in 1951 and later used X-rays. He showed how complex the gear system was. He argued that it was an astronomical calculator. His 1959 paper in Scientific American was called “An Ancient Greek Computer.” It made the mechanism famous.

The next big step came in 2005. The Antikythera Mechanism Research Project was an international team of scientists, astronomers, and historians. They used strong X-ray scans called tomography. These let them look inside the rusty pieces more clearly than ever before.

The results were stunning. The team could read writing that had been hidden for two thousand years. It was like a user’s manual written on the machine itself, in ancient Greek. They found new gears. They worked out much of the layout of the back dials.

They learned more about what the device could do. On the front dial it showed the Egyptian civil calendar and the zodiac. Pointers marked the positions of the Sun and the Moon. The planets are less certain, because the front is badly damaged.

On the upper back dial was the Metonic cycle. It says that 235 lunar months equal 19 solar years. It helped match the Moon’s calendar to the Sun’s.

On the lower back dial was the Saros cycle of 223 lunar months. It was used to predict eclipses of the Sun and Moon. A smaller dial showed the Callippic cycle of 940 lunar months, which equals 76 solar years. It made the calendar more accurate.

There was one more surprise. A dial followed the four-year cycle of the ancient athletic games. These included the Olympic, Nemean, Isthmian, and Pythian Games. This linked the stars to the festival calendar of the Greek world.

Who made the mechanism is one of the biggest questions in the history of technology. It is too advanced to be the first try of one craftsman. It probably comes from a tradition of making such instruments. Several people have been suggested, but nobody knows.

Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190–120 BCE) was a great astronomer of ancient times. Some experts think his ideas about the Moon’s motion may be built into the mechanism. Hipparchus worked on Rhodes, and some experts think the mechanism came from there. Others suggest Corinth, Syracuse, or Pergamon.

Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BCE) was a legendary mathematician and engineer. He built models of the heavens and other machines. He died in 212 BCE, probably before the mechanism was made. Still, his ideas may have inspired it. The Roman statesman Cicero wrote that Archimedes had built two sphere-shaped astronomical devices. They were taken to Rome after Syracuse was captured.

The school of Posidonius on Rhodes is another idea. Posidonius (c. 135–51 BCE) was a Stoic philosopher and astronomer. Cicero wrote about a device made by Posidonius that showed the motions of the Sun, the Moon, and the five wandering stars. That sounds like the Antikythera Mechanism, but nobody can be sure.

In 2021, a team led by Tony Freeth of University College London published an important paper. It described a new reconstruction of the front dial, the part that showed the Sun, Moon, and planets. The team used the writing on the mechanism as a guide. They built a model of the display of the heavens. It showed the Sun, the Moon, and the five known planets.

This may solve one of the last big puzzles. Seen from Earth, planets such as Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn seem to move backward for a while. This is called retrograde motion. The team says its model fits the writing and the pieces that survive. It is the first model to do so, they say. But they say they still must prove that it could be built with ancient tools.

The back of the mechanism has a spiral scale on the Saros dial. It was used to predict eclipses years ahead. Greek craftsmen made it with bronze and hand tools about two thousand years ago. Experts think it did not work perfectly, because hand-made gears were never exact.

The mechanism matters for more than its skill. It makes us rethink what ancient Greek engineers could do.

Before the mechanism was understood, some scholars thought the Greeks were great thinkers who did not care much about machines. The mechanism shows that this idea was too simple.

Some Greek craftsmen had skills like those of later clockmakers. They could cut small gear teeth by hand. They could design complex gear trains. And they could build astronomy into a machine.

The mechanism also raises a deep question about lost knowledge. If the Greeks could build analog computers in the second century BCE, why was the skill lost? Why did Europe wait until the fourteenth century CE for similar machines?

Nobody knows the answer. Perhaps such machines were rare and costly, and few people knew how to make them. Perhaps the skill was lost as the Greek world changed under Roman rule. Other machines may have been made but did not survive, because bronze was often melted down.

The Antikythera shipwreck is still giving up new finds. In recent years, marine archaeologists have returned to the wreck. They were led by the Greek Ephorate of Underwater Antiquities, with international partners.

They use modern diving tools. These include robot submarines and closed-circuit rebreathers, which let divers breathe without making bubbles. With them, they can work in deeper water than the sponge divers could.

New finds include more pieces of statues, human remains, and parts of the ship itself. Nobody has yet reported new pieces of the mechanism. Some people hope more pieces still lie on the seabed. This hope keeps the search going. The sea has kept its secrets for two thousand years. It may not be finished revealing them.

The Antikythera Mechanism is one of the most important technical objects to survive from the ancient world. It is also humbling. This small, rusty box of bronze gears was lifted from a Roman shipwreck by sponge divers in 1901.

It shows that the ancient Greeks reached a level of machine skill that Europe is not known to have matched until the late Middle Ages. That is a gap of more than a thousand years. It was built to predict eclipses. It followed the Sun, the Moon, and probably the planets. It tracked the cycle of the Olympic Games. It holds Hellenistic astronomy in a machine that was about the size of a shoebox.

Yet we do not know who built it. We do not know for sure where it was built. And we do not know how the knowledge to build it was lost. It is a message from a people who were more skilled than many once thought.

This early analog computer is kept in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Its gears have been silent for two thousand years. Its makers are unknown. Its secrets are still unfolding. What else could it teach us?
