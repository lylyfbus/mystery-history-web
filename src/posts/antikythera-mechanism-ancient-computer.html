The Antikythera Mechanism: An Ancient Greek Computer 1,500 Years Ahead of Its Time

The Antikythera Mechanism, recovered from a Roman shipwreck in 1901, is the world’s oldest known analog computer — built over 2,000 years ago

Divers and a storm

In 1900, a crew of Greek sponge fishermen was sailing home to the island of Symi. Their captain was Dimitrios Kondos. A storm forced them to take shelter near Antikythera, a rocky island between Crete and the Peloponnese.

While they waited for the weather to clear, a diver named Elias Stadiatis went down to the seabed. He found the wreck of an ancient ship. It lay about 45 meters (150 feet) deep. Statues, amphorae, and other objects lay scattered all around.

Kondos told the Greek authorities. From late 1900 into 1901, Greek Navy ships helped the divers to bring up the finds. The wreck dates to about 70–60 BCE. It was a Roman-era ship, and it carried great treasures. Among its cargo was something that nobody understood. It would turn out to be one of the most advanced technical objects to survive from the ancient world.

What the sea gave up

The work was very dangerous. The divers wore early diving suits and went down to a hard depth with poor sight. They collected objects by hand. Many divers got decompression sickness, also called the bends, which comes from coming up too fast from deep water. One diver died and two others were paralyzed.

The haul was spectacular. It had bronze and marble statues, including the famous Antikythera Ephebe and the Philosopher. One of the statues was a marble Hercules. There was also pottery, glassware, coins, and jewelry.

And there was a shapeless, rusty lump of bronze, about the size of a large book. It was covered with two thousand years of sea crust. It looked like nothing important. It was listed and put in a storage crate at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Then it was mostly forgotten.

The gear in the crust

The lump sat in the museum for months. Then, in 1902, an archaeologist named Valerios Stais looked at it closely. He saw that it held a gear. Inside the crust were the outlines of tiny, exact bronze gears, with ancient Greek writing on them.

The divers had not found a statue or a tool. They had found a machine. For decades, though, scholars could see the mechanism but could not read it. The remains came in 82 pieces. About 30 gear wheels survive. They were too rusty and fragile to take apart or clean.

A big step came with Derek J. de Solla Price, a British science historian. He began studying the mechanism in 1951 and later used X-rays. He argued that it was an astronomical calculator. His 1959 paper in Scientific American was called “An Ancient Greek Computer.” It made the mechanism famous.

The next big step came in 2005. The Antikythera Mechanism Research Project was an international team of scientists, astronomers, and historians. They used strong X-ray scans called tomography. These let them look inside the rusty pieces more clearly than ever before. They could read writing that had been hidden for two thousand years. So what did the machine do?

A computer made of gears

The Antikythera Mechanism, as it came to be called, is an analog computer. That means a machine that works with gears and dials instead of electronics. Experts think it was built sometime between about 205 and 87 BCE. The gears sat in a wooden case about 34 centimeters tall. The teeth were cut by hand.

On the front dial it showed the Egyptian civil calendar and the zodiac, the band of sky where the planets travel. Pointers marked the positions of the Sun and the Moon. It even copied the uneven movement of the Moon across the sky.

On the upper back dial was the Metonic cycle. It says that 235 lunar months equal 19 solar years. It helped match the Moon’s calendar to the Sun’s.

On the lower back dial was the Saros cycle of 223 lunar months. It was used to predict eclipses of the Sun and Moon. A smaller dial showed the Callippic cycle of 940 lunar months, which equals 76 solar years.

There was one more surprise. A dial followed the four-year cycle of the ancient athletic games. These included the Olympic, Nemean, Isthmian, and Pythian Games. This linked the stars to the festival calendar of the Greek world. Nothing like it is known in Europe for more than a thousand years afterward. So who could have built it?

Who made it?

Who made the mechanism is one of the biggest questions in the history of technology. It is too advanced to be the first try of one craftsman. It probably comes from a tradition of making such instruments. Several people have been suggested, but nobody knows.

Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190–120 BCE) was a great astronomer of ancient times. Some experts think his ideas about the Moon’s motion may be built into the mechanism. Hipparchus worked on Rhodes, and some experts think the mechanism came from there. Others suggest Corinth, Syracuse, or Pergamon.

Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212 BCE) was a legendary mathematician and engineer. He died in 212 BCE, probably before the mechanism was made. Still, his ideas may have inspired it. The Roman statesman Cicero wrote that Archimedes had built two sphere-shaped astronomical devices. They were taken to Rome after Syracuse was captured.

The school of Posidonius on Rhodes is another idea. Posidonius (c. 135–51 BCE) was a Stoic philosopher and astronomer. Cicero wrote about a device made by Posidonius that showed the motions of the Sun, the Moon, and the five wandering stars. That sounds like the Antikythera Mechanism, but nobody can be sure.

Did it show the planets?

Researchers think the mechanism showed the five known planets too. The front is badly damaged, so this is less certain. In 2021, a team led by Tony Freeth of University College London published an important paper. It described a new reconstruction of the front dial. The team used the writing on the mechanism as a guide. They built a model of the display of the heavens. It showed the Sun, the Moon, and the five known planets.

This may solve one of the last big puzzles. Seen from Earth, planets such as Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn seem to move backward for a while. This is called retrograde motion. The team says its model fits the writing and the pieces that survive. It is the first model to do so, they say. But they say they still must prove that it could be built with ancient tools.

Experts think the mechanism did not work perfectly, because hand-made gears were never exact. Even so, it could be used to predict eclipses years ahead.

A skill that vanished

The mechanism matters for more than its skill. It makes us rethink what ancient Greek engineers could do. We are often told that the Greeks were brilliant thinkers and mathematicians, but poor engineers. They gave us geometry and drama, not clockwork. The mechanism shows that this idea was too simple.

Some Greek craftsmen had skills like those of later clockmakers. They could cut small gear teeth by hand. They could design complex gear trains. And they could build astronomy into a machine. Machines like this did not appear in Europe until the great astronomical clocks of the fourteenth century.

So why was the skill lost? Nobody knows the answer. Perhaps such machines were rare and costly, and few people knew how to make them. Perhaps the skill was lost as the Greek world changed under Roman rule. Other machines may have been made but did not survive, because bronze was often melted down.

Still diving

The Antikythera shipwreck is still giving up new finds. In recent years, marine archaeologists have returned to the wreck. They were led by the Greek Ephorate of Underwater Antiquities, with international partners. They use modern diving tools, such as closed-circuit rebreathers, which let divers breathe without making bubbles. With them, they can work in deeper water than the sponge divers could.

New finds include more pieces of statues, human remains, and parts of the ship itself. Nobody has yet reported new pieces of the mechanism. Some people hope more pieces still lie on the seabed.

So here is what we know. A small box of bronze gears was lifted from a Roman shipwreck by sponge divers. It predicted eclipses, followed the Sun and Moon, and tracked the cycle of the Olympic Games. Yet we do not know who built it, where it was built, or how the knowledge to build it was lost. It is kept in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Its gears have been silent for two thousand years. Somewhere on the seabed near Antikythera, where Stadiatis once stood, more of the story may still be waiting.
