Ball Lightning: The Ghost Orbs of Storms

Ball lightning — a glowing sphere of electricity that appears during thunderstorms and defies scientific explanation

A fireball in the church

It was the afternoon of October 21, 1638. In the tiny village of Widecombe-in-the-Moor, on Dartmoor in Devon, England, about 300 people sat in St. Pancras Church for Sunday service. A storm was raging outside. Accounts from the time say a strange darkness fell over the building.

Then came a thunderous roar. A blinding flash of light followed. Witnesses said a great ball of fire came through a window. It bounced around inside the church and caused terrible damage.

Four people were killed. About sixty were hurt. The minister’s wife was badly burned. A dog ran out the door and fell dead. Some victims had burns on their bodies, yet their clothes were not burned.

It was one of the most frightening meetings between people and one of nature’s strangest events. That event is called ball lightning. Let us find out what people say it looks like.

A glowing ball in the storm

Witnesses describe a glowing ball that appears almost only during or just after thunderstorms. Reported sizes range from about 1 to 100 centimeters. Most are 10 to 20 centimeters across. They last from one second to over a minute before they vanish. Sometimes they vanish silently. Sometimes they end with a loud bang or an explosion.

They are usually red, orange, or yellow. Some witnesses report white, blue, and green ones. They can appear indoors or outdoors. Some hover without moving. Some seem to move with a purpose. Some witnesses say they pass through windows without breaking them. Others say they move against the wind.

Some surveys suggest that about 5 percent of people say they have seen ball lightning. A 1960 study of staff at the Oak Ridge laboratory in the United States found that between 3 and 6 percent had seen it. If such numbers are right, hundreds of millions of people have seen it. Yet nobody can explain it. But who first wrote it down?

Reports from eight centuries

One of the earliest known written reports comes from the twelfth century. In 1195, the English monk Gervase of Canterbury wrote about a dark cloud. A white shape came out of it and formed a ball. The ball then fell toward the river as a fiery globe. Some historians say this may describe ball lightning. Some sources name Gervase of Tilbury and the year 1211 instead.

In 1749, the crew of HMS Montague reported a ball of fire during a storm at sea.

Then there is the story of Georg Wilhelm Richmann, a physicist working in St. Petersburg, Russia. In 1753, he was doing experiments on electricity in the air. This field was famous at the time because of Benjamin Franklin’s experiments. During a thunderstorm on August 6, 1753, a ball of fire is said to have jumped from his iron rod and struck him in the head. He died. An engraver who was with him was knocked out but lived.

Richmann may have been a victim of ball lightning. Some historians say it may have been an ordinary lightning strike. Other details of the story are uncertain.

Tsar Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia, is said to have described a fiery ball that he saw as a child, inside a church, with his grandfather Alexander II. Such stories are hard to check. Even so, for centuries many scientists were doubtful. What changed their minds?

A ball on a laboratory screen

Many physicists once said ball lightning was an optical illusion, an afterimage, or a hallucination caused by the bright flash of normal lightning. This doubt began to fade in the late twentieth century. There were simply too many reports to ignore.

The turning point came in 2012. A team from Northwest Normal University in Lanzhou, China, was watching ordinary lightning during a thunderstorm on the Qinghai Plateau. The team included Jianyong Cen, Ping Yuan, and Simin Xue. They had instruments that record the colors in light. This is called spectroscopy.

A lightning bolt hit the ground about 900 meters away. Then a glowing ball appeared. It lasted about 1.6 seconds. Their instruments recorded its light. The light showed silicon, iron, and calcium. These are common in soil. The team published their results in 2014 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

It was a rare and important result. But it was a single event. Other scientists said it does not show which theory is right. So let us look at the ideas.

Idea one: burning soil

Two chemists from the University of Canterbury in New Zealand, John Abrahamson and James Dinniss, first suggested the soil idea in 2000. A lightning bolt hitting the ground has huge energy. It turns silicon dioxide in the soil into vapor. This makes a cloud of tiny silicon particles called nanoparticles. They mix with oxygen in the air. They then burn, making a ball that keeps itself glowing.

This idea tries to explain several key features of ball lightning. These are its link to thunderstorms, its round shape, and its glow. The 2012 recording is consistent with it. But it is not a full proof.

There is also a problem. The idea does not easily explain balls seen indoors, inside aircraft, or high above the ground. There is no soil there to turn into vapor.

Idea two: a glowing gas

Other ideas say that ball lightning is plasma. Plasma is a very hot, electrically charged gas. The Russian physicist Pyotr Kapitsa suggested that ball lightning is a glow kept alive by microwaves. These waves would travel down channels of charged air from the storm cloud. Other ideas call it a wave packet that holds its shape, or a pocket of charged gas that is light enough to float. Not all scientists think these ideas explain enough.

Scientists have tried to make ball lightning in the laboratory. In 1991, two Japanese researchers made glowing balls of plasma with microwaves. Other experiments have made small glowing balls in microwave ovens. But these fade within a short time. Many natural sightings are said to last much longer. None of these tests has made a convincing, lasting, large ball under controlled conditions.

All plasma ideas face one problem. How can a plasma ball last tens of seconds in open air without fading? In the laboratory, plasmas usually fade very fast once the energy source is removed.

Idea three: a picture in the brain

One surprising idea comes from the physicists Josef Peer and Alexander Kendl at the University of Innsbruck. Their 2010 study suggests that some ball lightning reports may be visions in the brain. The cause would be the strong magnetic field from a nearby lightning strike. The fast-changing field could act on the part of the brain that sees. It is like a method doctors use, called transcranial magnetic stimulation. People would then see a glowing spot of light.

The authors say that only rare kinds of lightning, with repeated flashes lasting a few seconds, could do this. And the person would need to be close.

This idea cannot explain reports of ball lightning that caused real damage, such as burns or broken windows. It also cannot explain the 2012 recording, which captured real light from outside. Most researchers think ball lightning is a real physical event. The vision idea may explain some of the reports.

Still a puzzle

What do we know? Ball lightning is reported by many people in many countries, and the stories are alike. A typical report is a glowing ball about 10 to 20 centimeters wide, lasting from a second to over a minute. That is one argument that it is a real event that can happen again and again.

What is open? We cannot predict when or where it will appear. We cannot reliably make it in a controlled test. And we cannot fully explain the wide range of behavior that witnesses have reported over eight centuries. Scientists from many countries have met at symposiums on ball lightning since 1988, when the first one was held in Tokyo.

Think again of the church at Widecombe in 1638, with its fireball and its dark storm. The next time you are caught in a thunderstorm, look carefully. You might see something that science still cannot explain. What is it?
