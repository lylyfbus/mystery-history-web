Ball Lightning: The Ghost Orbs of Storms

Ball lightning — a glowing sphere of electricity that appears during thunderstorms and defies scientific explanation

It was the afternoon of October 21, 1638. In the tiny village of Widecombe-in-the-Moor, on Dartmoor in Devon, England, about 300 people sat in St. Pancras Church for Sunday service. A storm was raging outside. Accounts from the time say a strange darkness fell over the building.

Then came a thunderous roar, like the sound of cannons. A blinding flash of light followed. Witnesses said a great ball of fire tore through a window. It bounced around inside the church and caused terrible damage.

Four people were killed. About sixty were hurt. The minister’s wife was badly burned. A dog ran out the door, was thrown around by an unseen force, and fell dead.

Some victims had burns on their bodies, yet their clothes were not burned. The building was badly damaged. It was one of the most frightening meetings between people and one of nature’s strangest events. That event is called ball lightning.

Ball lightning is one of the most puzzling mysteries in the science of the air and weather. Witnesses describe a glowing ball that appears almost only during or just after thunderstorms. Many people have reported it over at least eight centuries. They include scientists, soldiers, pilots, and ordinary people around the world.

Some surveys suggest that about 5 percent of people say they have seen ball lightning. That would be hundreds of millions of people. Yet after centuries of reports and decades of research, ball lightning is still unexplained. No laboratory test has fully copied it as it happens in nature. And no single scientific theory has won general agreement.

The objects that witnesses describe are very strange. They are glowing spheres. Reported sizes range from about 1 to 100 centimeters. Most are 10 to 20 centimeters across. They last from one second to over a minute before they vanish. Sometimes they vanish silently. Sometimes they end with a loud bang or an explosion.

They are usually red, orange, or yellow. Some witnesses report white, blue, and green ones. They can appear indoors or outdoors. Some hover without moving. Some seem to move with a purpose. Some witnesses say they pass through windows without breaking them. Others say they move against the wind.

One of the earliest known written reports comes from the twelfth century. In 1195, the English monk Gervase of Canterbury wrote about a dark cloud. A white shape came out of it and formed a ball. The ball then fell toward the river as a fiery globe. Some historians say this may describe ball lightning. Some sources name Gervase of Tilbury and the year 1211 instead.

Modern reports sound much the same. There is a thunderstorm. The shape is round. It glows. In the end it fades away or bursts.

The Great Thunderstorm of Widecombe-in-the-Moor in 1638 is one of the most dramatic and best-recorded cases from the past. But it is far from the only one. In 1749, the crew of HMS Montague reported a ball of fire during a storm at sea.

One of the most famous scientific victims of ball lightning may have been Georg Wilhelm Richmann. He was a physicist working in St. Petersburg, Russia. In 1753, he was doing experiments on electricity in the air. This field of study was famous at the time because of Benjamin Franklin’s experiments. Richmann was using an iron rod to study how it reacted to a storm.

During a thunderstorm on August 6, 1753, a ball of fire is said to have jumped from the device and struck Richmann in the head. He died. An engraver who was with him was knocked out but lived.

Richmann’s death is sometimes called the first documented case of death by ball lightning. Some historians say it may have been an ordinary lightning strike. Other details of the story are uncertain.

Other famous people are said to have seen ball lightning too. One story is about Tsar Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia. He is said to have described a fiery ball that he saw as a child, inside a church, with his grandfather Alexander II. Such stories are hard to check. They show how many people, even in palaces, have told of seeing something like it.

For centuries, many scientists were doubtful about ball lightning. The idea was so strange. Glowing balls that pass through windows? Many physicists said it was an optical illusion, an afterimage, or a hallucination caused by the bright flash of normal lightning.

This doubt began to fade in the late twentieth century. There were simply too many reports to ignore.

The turning point came in 2012. A team from Northwest Normal University in Lanzhou, China, was watching ordinary lightning during a thunderstorm on the Qinghai Plateau. The team included Jianyong Cen, Ping Yuan, and Simin Xue. They had instruments that record the colors in light. This is called spectroscopy. It helps to learn what a glowing thing is made of.

A lightning bolt hit the ground about 900 meters away. Then a glowing ball appeared. It lasted about 1.6 seconds. Their instruments recorded its light. The light showed silicon, iron, and calcium. These are common in soil. The team published their results in 2014 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

These results fit one idea. It says that a lightning strike turns the soil on the ground into vapor. The vapor then glows. The silicon dioxide in sand or soil is a main part of this idea.

The Chinese recording was a rare and important result. It gave real data about the light of a natural ball. But it was a single event. Other scientists said it does not show which theory is right. Two chemists from the University of Canterbury in New Zealand, John Abrahamson and James Dinniss, first suggested the soil idea in 2000.

They proposed that a lightning bolt hitting soil has huge energy. It turns silicon dioxide in the ground into vapor. This makes a cloud of tiny silicon particles called nanoparticles. They mix with oxygen in the air. They then burn, making a ball that keeps itself glowing.

This idea tries to explain several key features of ball lightning. These are its link to thunderstorms, its round shape, and its glow. The 2012 recording is consistent with it. But it is not a full proof.

Scientists have also tried to make ball lightning in the laboratory. In 1991, two Japanese researchers made glowing balls of plasma with microwaves. Plasma is a very hot, electrically charged gas. Other experiments have made small glowing balls in microwave ovens. But these fade within a short time. Many natural sightings are said to last much longer.

None of these tests has made a convincing, lasting, large ball of lightning under controlled conditions.

Scientists have suggested a huge range of explanations. This shows how hard it is to explain something with so many puzzling features. The vaporized silicon idea is one of the best known. But it does not easily explain balls seen indoors, inside aircraft, or high above the ground. There is no soil there to turn into vapor.

The microwave idea comes from the Russian physicist Pyotr Kapitsa. It says that ball lightning is a glow kept alive by microwaves. These waves would travel down channels of charged air from the storm cloud. The ball would act like a small hollow for them.

The soliton idea says that ball lightning is a kind of wave packet that holds its shape as it travels. The buoyant plasma idea says that ball lightning is a pocket of charged gas that is light enough to float. Not all scientists think these ideas explain enough.

All plasma ideas face one problem. How can a plasma ball last tens of seconds in open air without fading? In the laboratory, plasmas usually fade very fast once the energy source is removed.

One surprising idea comes from the physicists Josef Peer and Alexander Kendl at the University of Innsbruck. Their 2010 study suggests that some ball lightning reports may be visions in the brain. The cause would be the strong magnetic field from a nearby lightning strike. The fast-changing field could act on the part of the brain that sees. It is like a method doctors use, called transcranial magnetic stimulation. People would then see a glowing spot of light.

The authors say that only rare kinds of lightning, with repeated flashes lasting a few seconds, could do this. And the person would need to be close.

This idea cannot explain reports of ball lightning that caused real damage, such as burns or broken windows. It also cannot explain the 2012 recording, which captured real light from outside. Most researchers think ball lightning is a real physical event. The vision idea may explain some of the reports.

A 1960 study of staff at the Oak Ridge laboratory in the United States found that between 3 and 6 percent had seen ball lightning. If the estimate is right, hundreds of millions of people alive today have seen it.

A typical report is a glowing ball about 10 to 20 centimeters wide. It lasts from a second to over a minute. It appears during or just after a thunderstorm. The most common colors are red, orange, and yellow.

Reports from many countries tell similar stories. This is one of the arguments that ball lightning is a real event that can happen again and again, and not just a set of mistakes.

Ball lightning is still one of the most stubborn puzzles in nature. There was the 2012 recording. There is the vaporized soil idea. And there have been decades of laboratory tests. Even so, we cannot predict when or where ball lightning will appear. We cannot reliably make it in a controlled test. And we cannot fully explain the wide range of behavior that witnesses have reported over the last eight centuries.

The puzzle sits where the physics of the air, plasma science, electromagnetism, and even brain science meet. It uses almost every branch of physical science, and it still refuses to be solved.

Scientists from many countries have met at symposiums on ball lightning since 1988, when the first one was held in Tokyo. These meetings help a small group of researchers share their work and debates.

Yet the mystery goes on. Next time you are caught in a thunderstorm, and the lightning flashes, and the thunder rolls, look carefully. You might see something that science still cannot explain. It might be a glowing sphere, drifting through the storm like a ghost. What is it?
