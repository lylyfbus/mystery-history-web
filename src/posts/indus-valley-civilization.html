Indus Valley Civilization: Cities Before Their Time

A city under the mud

Picture a city beside the Indus River, more than 4,000 years ago. The streets are straight. The houses are built of baked brick. Some homes have a room for washing, and covered drains carry the dirty water away.

Then, over a few hundred years, the people leave. Sand and mud bury the city, and the world forgets it. Nobody dug up these cities again until the 1920s.

This was the Indus Valley Civilization. It grew up between about 3300 and 1300 BCE, on the rich flat land beside the Indus River and its side rivers. Today that land is in Pakistan and western India. It is also called the Harappan Civilization, after Harappa, one of its cities.

It was one of the earliest places where city life began. Others were ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and China. Experts do not fully understand how it rose, and they do not fully understand how it ended. Let us start by walking through one of its streets.

Cities drawn on a grid

The best years of the civilization were the Mature period, from about 2600 to 1900 BCE. At that time it covered more land than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia together. One estimate says that between one and five million people lived there.

More than 1,000 sites from those years have been reported. Nearly 100 have been dug up, and new ones turn up from time to time. The cities included Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi.

The first thing that a visitor notices is how well the cities were planned. Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were built on a grid, like a sheet of squared paper. Covered drains lined the major streets. Most buildings used baked bricks held together with mortar.

Mohenjo-daro is the site that has been dug up the most. Sources give its size as 240 to 300 hectares. Estimates for the number of people range from at least 40,000 to about 60,000. The city had a raised mound called the citadel, which held large public buildings. Below it was the lower city, with homes, workshops, and markets.

But the most surprising part of a Harappan city was hidden under the streets.

Drains, tanks, and a great bath

Some houses had rooms for bathing. Waste water ran through covered drains under the streets and was sent out of the city. Experts call this the oldest known system of city sanitation in the world, and a sign of careful planning.

The Harappans were also clever with water in other ways. Dholavira stood on an island in western Gujarat. It had stepwells and large reservoirs. That was important in a dry land where the monsoon, the yearly season of rain, could not be trusted.

At Lothal, near the sea in Gujarat, archaeologists found a large basin lined with brick. The man who dug it up thought that it was a dock for ships. Other experts think that it was a tank for fresh water. Nobody has proved which is right.

On the citadel of Mohenjo-daro lies the Great Bath. It is a large rectangular tank, about 12 meters long, 7 meters wide, and 2.4 meters deep. The builders put a lining of bitumen, a kind of natural tar, on the brick pool to make it watertight. Experts think that it was probably used for ritual washing, a way of making oneself pure. But the Harappans left no writing that we can read. So that is only a careful guess.

Why can we not read it?

Signs that nobody can read

Of all the puzzles of the Indus Valley, the writing is the most tempting. The Harappans left thousands of short texts on seals, tablets, pottery, and metal objects. Experts count roughly 400 to 600 different signs.

Most texts are very short. The usual one has only about five signs. The longest known, on a copper plate, has 34. With no long texts, language experts have few patterns to study. Scholars have worked on the script since the 1920s, and nobody has succeeded.

The script appears mostly on seals. These are small square tablets of steatite, a soft stone, usually 2 to 4 centimeters across. Many have a hole for a cord, and they were probably used for trade. The animals on the seals include bulls and elephants. There is also a strange animal that looks like a unicorn. Some seals show a man with the horns of a bull.

One famous seal, the Pashupati seal, shows a figure sitting with animals all around. In the 1900s, the scholar John Marshall linked it to the Hindu god Shiva. Later scholars doubt this. Nobody can read the words beside it, so these ideas are guesses.

Computer scientists have tried, too. A much-discussed study came out in 2009 in the journal Science. Its authors, led by Rajesh Rao, argued that the order of the signs looks like a spoken language. Other scholars say that the comparison was flawed.

The basic problem stays the same. The texts are too short, and no long text has been found to act as a key. There is no “Rosetta Stone” for the Indus script. So we do not know what the people called themselves or what language they spoke. We do not know how they were ruled, either.

A land without palaces

Egypt had its pharaohs, and Mesopotamia had its kings. In the Indus Valley, there is no clear proof of palaces or temples. No large monuments were built. So some scholars have a bold idea. Perhaps the Harappans did not have a small group of rulers far above the rest. Other scholars point out that we cannot read the script, so we cannot be sure.

Two small works of art tell us a little about these people. The first is the “priest-king,” a famous figure of soft stone found at Mohenjo-daro. It is about 17.5 centimeters tall and shows a bearded man. The name is only a guess. Nothing shows that he was a king or a priest.

The second is the “dancing girl,” a small bronze figure about 10.8 centimeters tall. It was made by the lost wax process, a way of casting metal in a mold shaped with wax. Experts date both figures to between about 2400 and 1900 BCE.

There is another surprise. Archaeologists found little proof of local defense or war between regions. There are no royal tombs full of weapons. The cities show no clear signs of being burned by attackers. But new studies of skeletons from Harappa suggest that life was less peaceful than people used to think. Studies of late skeletons show more violence near the end. So the Harappan world may have been calmer than many others, but probably not free of violence.

That leads to the biggest question of all.

Where did the people go?

Around 1900 BCE, the Mature Harappan period began to give way to the Late Harappan period. People slowly left the cities. Drains and other city comforts were no longer kept up. Trade with Mesopotamia grew smaller. By about 1700 BCE, most cities were empty.

Other ancient societies fell suddenly. The Indus Valley seems to have faded slowly, over several centuries. But slow does not mean well understood.

There was once a leading theory that people called Aryans invaded. In 1953, the archaeologist Mortimer Wheeler pointed to 37 skeletons at Mohenjo-daro. But later scholars showed that those skeletons came from after the city was empty. Most experts have now given up this theory.

In 2019, scientists studied the DNA of a woman buried at Rakhigarhi, one of the largest Harappan sites. DNA is the code inside every living cell. She lived about 4,500 years ago. Her ancestry came partly from early hunter-gatherers linked to Iran, and partly from the early people of South Asia. It had no sign of ancestry from the herders of the steppe, who came later. So her DNA does not show that invaders took the place of the old people.

When the rains grew weak

Many scholars now point to a change in climate. A key study appeared in 2014 in the journal Geology. Its authors looked at snail shells in the mud of an ancient lake called Kotla Dahar, in Haryana, India. The mud showed that the summer monsoon grew weak about 4,100 years ago. The weak rains lasted roughly 200 years.

Less rain could have ruined the farms. It could also have dried up the rivers that kept the cities alive. The Ghaggar-Hakra River was once fed by the monsoon all year. Around 4,000 years ago, it became a seasonal river that flowed only part of the year.

Some scholars think that people who lost water moved east. When a river dried up, many people had to move. The authors of the study say that their results fit this idea but do not prove it. One of them said that there was probably not a single cause.

Found again, still silent

So what do we know? The Indus Valley Civilization was larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia together. Its cities were carefully planned, and their drains are the oldest of their kind that we know. Its people traded far from home. Jewelry made in the Indus region has been found as far away as Mesopotamia.

There was no single great battle at the end. It seems to have been a slow retreat as the rains weakened, the rivers dried, and the people scattered. The Harappans did not truly disappear. But their cities, their script, and their name were lost for a very long time.

Think again of that straight street under the mud. Perhaps the greatest mystery is not that the people left. It is that they did so much, so long ago, and were then forgotten. Who were they, and what did they write?
