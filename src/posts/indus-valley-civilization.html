Indus Valley Civilization: Cities Before Their Time

Picture a city beside the Indus River, more than 4,000 years ago. The streets are straight. The houses are built of baked brick. Some homes have a room for washing, and covered drains carry the dirty water away. Then, over a few hundred years, the people leave. Sand and mud bury the city, and the world forgets it.

This was the Indus Valley Civilization. It grew up between about 3300 and 1300 BCE, on the rich flat land beside the Indus River and its side rivers. Today that land is in Pakistan and western India. Experts do not fully understand how it rose. They do not fully understand how it ended.

It is also called the Harappan Civilization, after Harappa, one of its cities. It was one of the earliest places where city life began. Others were ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and China.

Its best years were from about 2600 to 1900 BCE. At that time it covered more land than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia together. One estimate says that between one and five million people lived there.

Its cities included Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi. They had planned streets in a grid, buildings of baked brick, and covered drains. Experts call this the oldest known system of city sanitation in the world.

The Harappans had a writing system, and nobody can read it to this day. They traded with faraway lands. Jewelry made in the Indus region has been found as far away as Mesopotamia. There is no sign of kings in palaces, and the Harappans left few pictures of war.

Then, around 1900 BCE, the civilization began to break apart. Most of the cities were empty by about 1700 BCE. Trade fell. The great cities of the Indus fell silent under mud and sand. Nobody dug them up again until the 1920s.

The civilization spread over a huge area of Pakistan and western India. More than 1,000 sites from its best years have been reported. Nearly 100 have been dug up. New ones turn up from time to time.

Experts divide its history into three parts. The Early Harappan period lasted from about 3300 to 2600 BCE. The Mature Harappan period lasted from about 2600 to 1900 BCE. The Late Harappan period lasted from about 1900 to 1300 BCE.

The civilization was at its height in the Mature period. Its cities show many shared habits, such as the grid plan and the use of baked brick.

Egypt had its pharaohs, and Mesopotamia had its kings. In the Indus Valley, there is no clear proof of palaces or temples. No large monuments were built. Records give no quick answer about who ruled.

So some scholars have a bold idea. Perhaps the Harappans did not have a small group of rulers far above the rest. Other scholars point out that we cannot read the script. So we cannot be sure.

The first thing a visitor notices is how well the cities were planned. Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were built on a grid, like a sheet of squared paper. Covered drains lined the major streets.

Most buildings used baked bricks held together with mortar. Some used bricks made of dried mud.

Mohenjo-daro is the site that has been dug up the most. Sources give its size as 240 to 300 hectares. Estimates for the number of people range from at least 40,000 to about 60,000.

The city had two parts. One was a raised mound called the citadel. It held large public buildings. The other was the lower city, with homes, workshops, and markets.

The most surprising part of a Harappan city was its system for keeping clean. Some houses had rooms for bathing. Waste water ran through covered drains under the streets and was sent out of the city.

This is the oldest known system of this kind. Experts call it a sign of careful city planning.

The Harappans were also clever with water. The city of Dholavira stood on an island in western Gujarat. It had stepwells and large reservoirs that held water. That was important in a dry land where the monsoon, the yearly season of rain, could not be trusted.

At Lothal, near the sea in Gujarat, archaeologists found a large basin lined with brick. The man who dug it up thought it was a dock for ships. Other experts think it was a tank for fresh water. Nobody has proved which is right.

One of the best-known Harappan buildings is the Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro. It is a large tank in the shape of a rectangle, on the citadel mound. It is about 12 meters long, 7 meters wide, and 2.4 meters deep.

The builders made the tank watertight. They put a lining of bitumen, a kind of natural tar, on the brick pool. The method worked well, and the pool is still seen today.

Experts think the Great Bath was probably used for ritual washing, a way of making oneself pure. But the Harappans left no writing that we can read. So the purpose of the Great Bath is a careful guess.

The Great Bath was found in the 1920s, in the early digs at Mohenjo-daro. It is still one of the most famous buildings of the ancient world.

Of all the puzzles of the Indus Valley, the writing is the most tempting. The Harappans left thousands of short texts on seals, tablets, pottery, and metal objects. Experts count roughly 400 to 600 different signs.

Most texts are very short. The usual one has only about five signs. The longest known, on a copper plate, has 34. This is a major obstacle for anyone who wants to read the script. With no long texts, language experts have few patterns to study.

Scholars have worked on the script since the 1920s. Many have tried to read it. Nobody has succeeded, and the script is still undeciphered.

Because we cannot read the script, we know far less than we would like. We do not know what the people called themselves or what language they spoke. We do not know how they were ruled or what they believed.

The script appears mostly on seals. These are small square tablets of steatite, a soft stone. They are usually 2 to 4 centimeters across. Many have a hole for a cord, and they were probably used for trade.

The animals on the seals include bulls and elephants. There is also a strange animal that looks like a unicorn. Some seals show a man with the horns of a bull.

One famous seal, the Pashupati seal, shows a figure sitting with animals all around. In the 1900s the scholar John Marshall linked it to the Hindu god Shiva. Later scholars doubt this. Nobody can read the words beside it, so these ideas are guesses.

In recent years, computer scientists have tried their luck. They use statistics and programs that look for patterns. They hope to do what human scholars could not.

A much-discussed study came out in 2009 in the journal Science. Its authors, led by Rajesh Rao, argued that the order of the signs looks like a spoken language. Other scholars say that the comparison was flawed.

So the basic problem is the same. The texts are too short. And no long text has been found to act as a key, no “Rosetta Stone” for the Indus script.

Until someone finds such a text, the script will probably stay a mystery. The history, the stories, and the very name of this people are locked behind a wall of symbols.

One surprising thing about the Indus Valley is how few signs of war have been found. Archaeologists found little proof of local defense or war between regions in the Indus lands.

There are no royal tombs full of weapons. The cities show no clear signs of being burned or destroyed by attackers.

Two small works of art tell us a little about these people. The first is the “priest-king,” a famous figure of soft stone found at Mohenjo-daro. It is about 17.5 centimeters tall and shows a bearded man. The name is only a guess. Nothing shows that he was a king or a priest.

The second is the “dancing girl,” a small bronze figure about 10.8 centimeters tall. It was made by the lost wax process, a way of casting metal in a mold shaped with wax. Experts date both figures to between about 2400 and 1900 BCE.

Scholars argue about the peaceful picture. Some say the missing evidence means exactly what it seems to mean. The Harappans ran their land through trade and shared customs, and perhaps through some kind of council.

Others give a warning. New studies of skeletons from Harappa suggest that life was less peaceful than people used to think. One man’s skull shows a broken nose and a hard blow. Studies of late skeletons also show more violence near the end of the civilization.

So the Harappan world may have been calmer than many others. But it was probably not free of violence. This was still a vast and well-ordered society of cities that did well for centuries.

The most haunting question is also the simplest. What happened to them? Around 1900 BCE, the Mature Harappan period began to give way to the Late Harappan period. People slowly left the cities. Drains and other city comforts were no longer kept up.

Trade with Mesopotamia grew smaller. By about 1700 BCE most cities were empty. The Late Harappan period lasted until about 1300 BCE.

Other ancient societies fell suddenly. The Indus Valley seems to have faded slowly, over several centuries. But slow does not mean well understood.

Many scholars now point to a change in climate. A key study appeared in 2014 in the journal Geology. Its authors looked at snail shells in the mud of an ancient lake called Kotla Dahar, in Haryana, India. The mud showed that the summer monsoon grew weak about 4,100 years ago. The weak rains lasted roughly 200 years.

Less rain could have ruined the farms. It could have dried up the rivers that kept the cities alive. The authors say that the results fit this idea but do not prove it. One of them said that there was probably not a single cause.

Evidence from the land fits this idea. The Ghaggar-Hakra River was once fed by the monsoon all year. Around 4,000 years ago it became a seasonal river that flowed only part of the year. Some people link it to the Sarasvati, a river in the old Vedic texts of India.

Some scholars think that people who lost water moved east. When a river dried up, many people had to move.

There was once another leading theory, that people called Aryans invaded. In 1953 the archaeologist Mortimer Wheeler pointed to 37 skeletons at Mohenjo-daro. But later scholars showed that those skeletons came from after the city was empty. This theory has now been given up by most experts.

In 2019, scientists studied the DNA of a woman buried at Rakhigarhi, one of the largest Harappan sites. She lived about 4,500 years ago. DNA is the code inside every living cell.

Her ancestry came partly from early hunter-gatherers linked to Iran, and partly from the early people of South Asia. It had no sign of ancestry from the herders of the steppe, who came later. So her DNA does not show that invaders took the place of the old people.

The Indus Valley Civilization is one of the great ancient city societies. It is also one of the hardest to understand. It was larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia together. Its cities were carefully planned, and their drains are the oldest of their kind that we know.

Its people traded far from home. Many scholars think they were mostly at peace. They made a script that we still cannot read. Then, over several centuries, it all faded away. There was no single great battle. It seems to have been a slow retreat as the rains weakened, the rivers dried, and the people scattered.

The Harappans did not truly disappear, but their cities, their script, and their name were lost for a very long time. They lay buried under the mud of the Indus until the 1920s.

Perhaps the greatest mystery is not that they vanished. It is that they did so much, so long ago, and were then forgotten. Who were they, and what did they write?
