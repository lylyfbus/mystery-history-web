Göbekli Tepe: The World's Oldest Temple and the Mystery of Who Built It

Image: Teomancimit, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

A note about stones on a hill

In 1994, a German archaeologist named Klaus Schmidt was reading old survey records from the University of Chicago. He worked with the German Archaeological Institute, and he was studying prehistoric sites in southeastern Turkey. He had just finished work at a nearby Neolithic site called Nevalı Çori. There, similar T-shaped limestone pillars had been found.

One short note caught his eye. In 1963, a team from the University of Chicago and Istanbul University had noted a low mound near the city of Şanlıurfa. They saw pieces of limestone on the surface, and they took them for grave markers. They wrote the mound down and moved on. It sat quiet for about thirty more years.

Schmidt guessed that there might be more at the place. He went to the mound and looked at what lay on the ground. To a visitor who does not know better, it looks like an ordinary bump in the land. But Schmidt saw that the flint tools and limestone pieces did not come from the Middle Ages. They came from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. That is a time when people had not yet made pottery. It was thousands of years earlier than anyone had expected for such big buildings.

Circles of stone, 11,000 years old

Schmidt began digging in 1995, with the German Archaeological Institute and Turkish archaeologists. Under the mound lay a set of large round enclosures. The place is called Göbekli Tepe. It was built more than 11,000 years ago, from about 9500 BCE.

Each enclosure is marked by a ring of huge T-shaped limestone pillars set into stone walls. The biggest pillars stand in pairs at the center. Some are about 5.5 meters tall and weigh up to 16 tons. Nearly two hundred pillars have been found through digging and surveys.

Göbekli Tepe is more than six thousand years older than Stonehenge. Most experts think that its builders were hunter-gatherers, with no pottery and no metal tools. Somehow they organized themselves to cut, move, carve, and raise these stones. Many people call it the world’s first temple. Experts say its pillars are among the oldest big stone monuments known.

So who were the stone figures in the circles?

Pillars with arms and hands

Many experts think that the pillars show stylized humans. Many have carved arms and hands down their sides, and the fingers are clear. Some have what look like belts, loincloths, and animal-skin clothes. The top of the T may be a head, drawn in a simple way.

Enclosure D is one of the most impressive. It has two central pillars, each more than five meters tall, covered with detailed carvings. If the human idea is right, each enclosure can be seen as a meeting of stone ancestors or spirit beings. The central pair would be the most important, and the ring would be the lesser ones.

The floors were a surprise too. Some were made of burnt lime that looks like terrazzo, a smooth stone floor. That is a surprisingly advanced floor for people without pottery. Experts are not sure whether the enclosures had roofs.

The pillars carry another kind of picture as well.

What do the animals mean?

The pillars are carved with animals: foxes, boars, snakes, vultures, lions, and gazelles. The carvings are among the earliest large pictures of animals known. The choice of animals, and where they were placed, seems deliberate and meaningful.

Some scholars think that the animals were clan totems, or spirit animals. Each group may have given a pillar with its own symbolic creature.

Others see a shaman’s view of the world. A shaman is a spiritual healer or guide. Dangerous animals such as snakes and scorpions may stand for the underworld. Soaring birds such as vultures may stand for the sky. Strong hunters such as lions may stand for the earth.

The vultures have drawn special attention. On one pillar in Enclosure D, a vulture spreads its wings. Beside it is what looks like a human figure with no head. Some researchers link this to excarnation. That means leaving a dead body in the open so that scavenging birds clean the bones. Several ancient cultures did this. If the idea is right, Göbekli Tepe may have had a link to rituals for the dead. But nobody can be sure.

Did a temple start farming?

The big question is not only what the carvings mean. It is when the place was built. For a long time, experts believed that organized religion and great buildings came from farming societies. The Neolithic Revolution was the change from hunting and gathering to farming and living in one place. Only farming villages with extra food, people thought, could support workers who did not grow food themselves.

Göbekli Tepe challenged that story. Here was a great monument built before farming was common. Schmidt had a bold idea. Perhaps farming did not make religion possible. Perhaps religion pushed people to start farming.

Many people may have gathered to build the site and hold ceremonies. They needed to eat. That need may have led them to grow wild grains on purpose. The area is home to the wild ancestors of modern wheat. One is einkorn wheat, which genetic studies suggest was first farmed near the Karacadağ mountains, about thirty kilometers away. In this view, Göbekli Tepe may have helped cause civilization. Not all experts agree.

A holy place, or a village?

Newer digs have complicated the picture. They found houses, more than 7,000 grinding stones, and a water system. The water system had carved channels that fed cisterns cut into the rock. These could hold at least 150 cubic meters of water. That suggests careful planning.

So Göbekli Tepe may have been more of a permanent village than people thought. It may not have been only a holy place visited by wandering pilgrims. Experts still debate whether the grain that was ground was wild or farmed.

Then there is a second puzzle. Over time, the enclosures were filled with soil, stone, and waste.

Why was it buried?

Some experts once thought that the people buried the enclosures on purpose, as a ritual to close an old temple. Other experts now doubt this. Nobody knows for sure why it happened. It may be why the site is so well preserved after thousands of years.

Only a small part of Göbekli Tepe has been dug up. Sources say it is between five and ten percent. The hill is about eight hectares in size, and surveys using special equipment suggest that at least twenty enclosures lie under it. Schmidt’s team dug four, which they named A to D.

Still being written

Schmidt spent the rest of his life on Göbekli Tepe. He led the digs until he died in 2014. The site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018. The T-shape appears at other sites in the area, such as Karahan Tepe and Nevalı Çori. The region is now called the Taş Tepeler, which means “Stone Hills.”

What do we know? Hunter-gatherers could organize big projects, divide up skilled work, and make fine art, before farming was common. What do we not know? Why they built it, and why it was filled in. Go back to that low mound near Şanlıurfa, which looked like an ordinary bump in the land. Under it, most of the story is still waiting.
