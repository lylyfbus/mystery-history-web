Göbekli Tepe: The World's Oldest Temple and the Mystery of Who Built It

Image: Teomancimit, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

In the rolling limestone hills of southeastern Turkey, about 15 kilometers from the city of Şanlıurfa, there is a low, round mound. To a visitor who does not know better, it looks like an ordinary bump in the land. But under that mound lies one of the great discoveries in human history.

It is a huge group of stone enclosures built more than 11,000 years ago, from about 9500 BCE. This was during a time called the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, when people had not yet made pottery. The place is called Göbekli Tepe. Many people call it the world’s first temple. Experts say its pillars are among the oldest big stone monuments known.

Its giant T-shaped limestone pillars are arranged in great circles. Some stand about 5.5 meters tall and weigh up to 16 tons. They are carved with animals: foxes, boars, snakes, vultures, lions, and gazelles.

Göbekli Tepe is more than six thousand years older than Stonehenge. It is also thousands of years older than the Egyptian pyramids. Most experts think that its builders were hunter-gatherers. They had no pottery and no metal tools. Somehow they organized themselves to cut, move, carve, and raise huge stones. Over time, the enclosures were filled in with earth and stones.

The discovery has changed how we see early humans. For a long time, experts believed that organized religion and great buildings came from farming societies. At Göbekli Tepe, some experts think that big building came first. Farming may have come after.

Before this discovery, the main story of human civilization went like this. The Neolithic Revolution was the change from hunting and gathering to farming and living in one place. It was thought to come before everything else: cities, temples, writing, kings, and organized religion. The idea seemed sensible. Only farming villages with extra food could support workers who did not grow food themselves.

Göbekli Tepe challenged that story. Here was a great monument built before farming was common. The German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt (1953–2014) saw its importance. He had a bold idea. Perhaps farming did not make religion possible. Perhaps religion pushed people to start farming.

Many people may have gathered to build the site and hold ceremonies. They needed to eat. That need may have led them to grow wild grains on purpose. Those could have been the first steps toward farming.

The site was first noted in 1963. A team from the University of Chicago and Istanbul University was surveying sites in southeastern Turkey. They noted the mound and wrote it down in their records. But they did not see how important it was. They saw pieces of limestone on the surface, and they took them for grave markers. The mound sat quiet for about thirty more years.

In 1994, Klaus Schmidt was reading the old Chicago survey records. He worked with the German Archaeological Institute (DAI), and he was studying prehistoric sites in the area. He had just finished work at a nearby Neolithic site called Nevalı Çori. There, similar T-shaped limestone pillars had been found.

Schmidt read the note about limestone pieces on the surface of Göbekli Tepe. He guessed that there might be similar buildings there. He went to the mound and looked at what lay on the ground. He saw that the flint tools and limestone pieces did not come from the Middle Ages. They came from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. That was thousands of years earlier than anyone had expected for such big buildings.

Schmidt began digging in 1995. He worked with a team from the German Archaeological Institute and with Turkish archaeologists.

Under the mound lay a set of large round enclosures. Each was marked by a ring of huge T-shaped limestone pillars set into stone walls. The biggest pillars stood in pairs at the center. Smaller ones stood around the edge.

The pillars were carved with pictures of animals, abstract signs, and, in some cases, what seem to be human features. There are arms, hands, belts, and loincloths. The work was excellent, even though the carvers had only stone tools. No metal tools existed then.

Schmidt’s team found four main enclosures. They named them Enclosures A to D. Surveys of the ground using special equipment, called geophysical surveys, suggest that there are at least twenty in all. The hill is about eight hectares in size. It was a large site.

Schmidt spent the rest of his life on Göbekli Tepe. He led the digs until he died in 2014. The site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018.

At the heart of the site are the great circular enclosures. Each has a ring of huge T-shaped pillars around two even larger pillars in the middle. Surveys have found at least twenty enclosures. But only a small part of the site has been dug up.

Enclosure D is one of the most impressive. It has two central pillars, each more than five meters tall. They are covered with detailed carvings. Both have stylized human arms and hands along their sides. It is as if the pillars themselves were simple human figures.

The ring pillars around them show many other animals. The carvings are sharp and detailed.

Experts are not sure whether the enclosures had roofs. Some floors were made of burnt lime that looks like terrazzo, a smooth stone floor. That is a surprisingly advanced floor for a people without pottery.

The carvings on the pillars are among the earliest large pictures of animals known. The animals are all wild. They include foxes, wild boars, lions, snakes, vultures, ducks, spiders, and bulls.

The choice of animals, and where they were placed, seems deliberate and meaningful. Some scholars think the animals were clan totems, or spirit animals. Each group may have given a pillar with its own symbolic creature.

Others see a shaman’s view of the world. A shaman is a spiritual healer or guide. Dangerous animals like snakes and scorpions may stand for the underworld. Soaring birds like vultures may stand for the sky. Strong hunters like lions may stand for the earth.

The vultures have drawn special attention. On one pillar in Enclosure D, a vulture spreads its wings. Beside it is what looks like a human figure with no head. Some researchers link this to excarnation. That means leaving a dead body in the open so that scavenging birds clean the bones. Several ancient cultures did this. If the idea is right, Göbekli Tepe may have had a link to rituals for the dead.

Göbekli Tepe’s greatest gift to archaeology may be what it says about the Neolithic Revolution. The old view was that farming gave people extra food. This let big communities grow. They then built monuments, divided up their work, and formed social ranks. But Göbekli Tepe may turn that order upside down. Here are great buildings made before farming was common.

Schmidt thought that feeding the many workers who built and kept up the site may have led to growing wild grains. The area is home to the wild ancestors of modern wheat. One is einkorn wheat. Genetic studies suggest that it was first farmed near the Karacadağ mountains, about thirty kilometers away.

The huge task of building may have pushed hunter-gatherers to find better ways to get food. In time, this may have led to full farming. In this view, Göbekli Tepe may have helped cause civilization. Not all experts agree.

Newer digs have complicated this picture. They found houses, more than 7,000 grinding stones, and a water system. So Göbekli Tepe may have been more of a permanent village than people thought. It may not have been only a holy place visited by wandering pilgrims. Experts still debate whether the grain that was ground was wild or farmed.

The water system had carved channels that fed cisterns cut into the rock. These could hold at least 150 cubic meters of water. That suggests careful planning.

The enclosures were filled with soil, stone, and waste over time. Some experts once thought that the people buried them on purpose, as a ritual to close an old temple. Other experts now doubt this. Nobody knows for sure why it happened. It may be why the site is so well preserved after thousands of years.

The T-shaped pillars are the main feature of Göbekli Tepe. What they mean is one of the most debated questions. Nearly two hundred pillars have been found through digging and surveys. The tallest are over five meters tall.

Many experts think the pillars show stylized humans. Several clues support this. Many pillars have carved arms and hands down their sides, and the fingers are clear. Some have what look like belts, loincloths, and animal-skin clothes. The top of the T may be a head, drawn in a simple way.

If this is right, each enclosure can be seen as a meeting of stone ancestors or spirit beings. The central pair would be the most important. The ring would be the lesser ones.

The T-shape appears at other sites in the area. They include Karahan Tepe and Nevalı Çori. The region is now called the Taş Tepeler, which means “Stone Hills.”

Only a small part of Göbekli Tepe has been dug up. Sources say it is between five and ten percent. Surveys suggest that most of the site lies buried under the mound. Each digging season brings new questions.

Today Göbekli Tepe is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Visitors can see the enclosures near the city of Şanlıurfa.

What Göbekli Tepe means for human history is still being written. It shows that hunter-gatherers could organize big projects, divide up skilled work, and make fine art. They did so before farming was common. It suggests that the urge to build monuments may have helped start farming, and not the other way around. Why did these people build it, and why did they bury it? Nobody knows yet.
