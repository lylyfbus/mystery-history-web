The Moai of Easter Island: How Did the Rapa Nui Move About 900 Giant Stone Statues?

Easter Sunday, 1722

It was Easter Sunday, April 5, 1722. A Dutch navigator named Jacob Roggeveen and his crew saw a low, treeless island in the Pacific Ocean. It was one of the most remote places on Earth where people lived. It lay more than 3,500 kilometers from the coast of Chile and about 2,000 kilometers from the nearest inhabited island.

The sailors found many huge stone statues. Most of them stood on stone platforms around the edge of the island. They looked inland, with their backs to the sea. The local people, the Rapa Nui, called the statues moai.

The moai were enormous. They had long heads, serious faces, and long fingers resting near the waist. Some wore giant round hats of red stone. The people who met Roggeveen’s ships numbered perhaps 2,000 to 3,000. They lived on land with very few trees. They had no wheels and no work animals.

So how had they made such huge works? Let us start with the island itself.

An island at the end of the world

Easter Island is called Rapa Nui by its people. Some people say an older name is Te Pito o te Henua, which they translate as “the navel of the world.” Scholars disagree about that meaning. It is a small, triangular volcanic island of only 163 square kilometers.

Polynesian sailors reached it long ago. Newer studies say about 1200 CE, but older guesses go back much earlier. They crossed thousands of miles of open ocean in canoes. Over the next centuries, they built a complex society.

Later, the forests were cut down and the statues were toppled. The number of people dropped. By the 1860s, almost every statue on a platform had fallen. Many have been raised again in modern times.

Where did the statues come from? The answer is a volcano.

Giants cut from a volcano

Most moai were cut from volcanic tuff, a stone made of squeezed volcanic ash. The place was Rano Raraku, an extinct volcano on the east side of the island. Archaeologists have counted about 900 moai. Archaeologists are people who study the past by digging up old things.

The average moai is about 4 meters (13 feet) tall and weighs 12.5 tons. But sizes vary a great deal. The tallest one ever raised is called Paro. It stands nearly 10 meters (33 feet) tall and weighs about 82 tons.

Sculptors carved each moai lying on its back, straight out of the rock. They used hand-held stone tools called toki. Experts think the statue was cut free only when the carving was almost done. Then it was moved down the slope and stood upright in a deep hole.

The largest one ever carved is called El Gigante. It would be about 21 meters (69 feet) tall if finished. It may weigh 145 to 165 tons. It was never taken out of the quarry. It is still attached to the rock.

Nearly 400 unfinished moai remain at Rano Raraku. Some are barely roughed out of the rock. Others are finished and ready to move. The marks from the stone chisels can still be seen. Most moai were carved between about 1250 and 1500 CE. Why the work stopped is still argued about.

Hundreds of statues did leave the quarry. How?

Did the statues walk?

The Rapa Nui had no wheels, no horses or cattle, and, by the time Europeans came, almost no trees. This puzzle has caused more argument than almost any other in Polynesian archaeology.

The islanders’ own stories give a clue. They said the statues “walked” to their platforms. Modern science has tested this idea.

Archaeologists Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo proposed the “walking” theory. Teams of people pulled on ropes tied to the head and base of an upright statue. They rocked it from side to side so that it stepped forward. In an experiment, a team of 18 people “walked” a copy of a moai that weighed about 4 tons for 100 meters in 40 minutes.

A study from 2025 in the Journal of Archaeological Science looked at the statues along the old roads. Their bases are wide and D-shaped, and the statues lean forward. Hunt and Lipo say this fits a rocking walk. Other experts say the tests show that walking was possible, but they do not prove it happened.

Other ideas exist too. The sledge and rail theory says moai were laid on wooden sledges and dragged over wooden rails or a bed of small round stones. But that needs a lot of wood, and wood became scarce as the trees disappeared. The Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl tested a lever and pivot method in 1986. His teams used 8 and 16 workers to move copies of 5 and 9 tons. Some think this is too slow to explain how so many statues were moved.

And wood leads us to the next question.

Where did the trees go?

The older account was made famous by Jared Diamond in his 2005 book Collapse. It says the Rapa Nui cut down their own forests. They needed wood to move the moai, to fuel cooking fires, and to make fields.

Studies of pollen suggest that the island was once covered with a thick forest of giant palm trees. That palm is now extinct. By the time Europeans arrived in 1722, almost no trees were left. In this account, the soil washed away and people ran short of firewood.

The account also says the population was perhaps between 6,000 and 15,000 at its peak. But newer studies suggest only about 3,000 people at most.

Later research has made the story more complicated. Some researchers say the Polynesian rat played a big part. It came with the first settlers. It ate palm seeds, so new trees could not grow. Others say the people were strong and clever. They covered the soil with stones to keep it moist, a way of farming called lithic mulching. Some researchers say the real damage came later, from disease and slave raids.

Oral traditions tell of wars between clans, and some say moai were knocked down on purpose. But one expert, Mike Pitts, says nothing shows the statues were pushed over. Most agree on one thing. By the 18th century, the forests were gone, and the island had changed forever.

The Birdman and a script nobody can read

As the old ways changed, a new religion took its place. It centered on a contest called the Tangata Manu, or Birdman. Each year, chiefs and their swimmers gathered at Orongo, a ceremonial village on the rim of the Rano Kau crater, high above the sea.

A swimmer crossed the sea to a small islet called Motu Nui. Many swimmers were hurt or died. He had to bring back the first egg laid by the sooty tern, a seabird, that season. The man whose swimmer won became the Birdman. The contest ended in the 1860s, when Christian missionaries stopped it.

Another puzzle is the rongorongo script. It is a set of signs cut into wooden tablets. The signs show human figures, animals, plants, and shapes. You read one line, then turn the tablet around and read the next. About 26 objects with rongorongo are accepted today. Nobody can read them.

Is it true writing, which records spoken language? Or is it proto-writing, which records ideas without grammar? Most experts now say it is proto-writing. Some think European visits may have inspired it. Others disagree.

When the ships came back

The arrival of Europeans brought disaster. In 1862 and 1863, slave raiders from Peru kidnapped about 1,500 Rapa Nui. They were forced to work on farms and in guano mines in Peru. Guano is bird droppings, used as fertilizer. Many of the captives died.

Under pressure, a few survivors came home. By one count, only about a dozen did. They brought smallpox with them, and it hurt the island even more. By 1877, only 111 Rapa Nui remained.

Much was lost, including the skill of reading rongorongo and the Birdman traditions. Chile took over the island in 1888. Most Rapa Nui were kept in the village of Hanga Roa for decades, until the 1950s or 1960s. About 7,750 people live on the island today, and their culture is being revived.

Still staring inland

Return to that Easter Sunday in 1722. The sailors saw a people who had lived alone on one of the most remote islands on Earth. They had built a remarkable civilization.

What do we know? The Rapa Nui carved about 900 statues. The forests vanished, and in the 19th century disease and slave raids hurt the people badly. What is open? How the statues were moved, why the quarry went quiet, and which cause did the most harm. Cutting trees, rats, too many people, a changing climate, disease and slave raids, or all of these together? Nobody can say for sure.

Many moai have been raised again. They stand on their platforms, staring inland. They are the ancestors of the Rapa Nui, frozen in stone. They watch over an island whose people are reviving their culture.
