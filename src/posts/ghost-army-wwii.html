The Ghost Army: How Fake Tanks and Sound Effects Helped Win WWII

In 1944, in the last year of World War II, Allied armies were fighting their way across Europe. The U.S. Army sent out a very strange weapon. It had no guns, no armor, and no explosives. It could not blow up a bridge or capture a hill. Yet it is credited with saving the lives of an estimated 15,000 to 30,000 American soldiers.

The weapon was the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops. It is better known as the Ghost Army. Its story stayed secret for more than fifty years.

The unit was activated on January 20, 1944. It had about 1,100 men, and it was unlike anything the U.S. military had made before. Its soldiers were not hardened fighters. They were artists, designers, actors, sound engineers, architects, and advertising workers. They came from art schools, advertising firms on Madison Avenue, and Hollywood studios. Their job was not to fight the enemy. It was to fool him.

They had inflatable tanks that could be unpacked and blown up in hours. They had powerful loudspeakers on half-tracks, which were army trucks with tank-like tracks at the back. These could play the sounds of a whole armored division across miles of countryside. Their radio operators sent carefully made fake messages to trick German listeners. Together they put on a traveling show of trickery. It ran from the beaches of Normandy to the banks of the Rhine River.

The Ghost Army was the idea of two men. Ralph Ingersoll was a journalist and publisher who had co-founded PM, a liberal newspaper in New York. Billy Harris was a U.S. Army planner who saw the power of large-scale tricks in battle.

The idea came from the British. They had perfected military deception in the North African desert. It was led by the clever Dudley Clarke and his group, called “A Force.” One of their most famous tricks was Operation Bertram. It came before the Battle of El Alamein in late 1942. They used dummy tanks, fake supply dumps, and false information. They wanted to convince the German Field Marshal Erwin Rommel that the main attack would come from the south, not the north.

Their success convinced American planners that a special U.S. unit could play a key part in the coming invasion of France. So the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops was activated at Camp Forrest, Tennessee. It later finished its training at Camp Pine, New York, which is now Fort Drum. The men were chosen for their creativity, their technical skills, and their ability to think like the enemy. Ordinary fighting soldiers rarely needed that skill.

To find recruits, the Army did not look for sharpshooters or foot soldiers. It sent notices to art schools, design programs, and advertising agencies across the country. It wanted people skilled in visual tricks, sound, and stage work. The search was so secret that many men did not know what they were joining.

One was Bernie Bluestein, a 19-year-old student at the Cleveland Institute of Art. In March 1943, he saw a vague notice on the school bulletin board. It asked for recruits for a “camouflage unit.” He joined. He did not know that the real mission was far more dangerous and unusual than painting camouflage.

The Ghost Army drew some of the most talented creative minds of its day. Bill Blass later became one of America’s most celebrated fashion designers. In the unit, he used his eye for design in the art of deception.

Ellsworth Kelly later became one of the most important abstract painters and sculptors of the twentieth century. As a Ghost Army soldier, he made fake badges and vehicle markings, and learned more about form and color. Art Kane later became a legendary photographer. He is famous for his portrait of 57 jazz musicians on the steps of a brownstone house in Harlem, called “A Great Day in Harlem.” He served in the unit’s visual deception work.

Arthur Singer became one of America’s greatest wildlife illustrators. His bird paintings appeared on U.S. postage stamps. He was another member.

These men, and hundreds like them, brought great skill and care to the work. That made the tricks very effective. They knew that fooling the enemy took more than a few inflatable tanks. It needed a complete illusion for every sense. It needed the look of a camp, the sound of engines, and the radio chatter of a busy headquarters. The Ghost Army was really a theater company, and Europe in wartime was its stage.

The Ghost Army used three main kinds of trickery. Each one aimed at a different way the Germans gathered information. The first, and the most striking to look at, was inflatable tanks, trucks, jeeps, and airplanes. Special companies made them for the Army. Set up correctly, they looked very real.

A typical inflatable M4 Sherman tank weighed only a tiny part of the real 30-ton tank. Filled with air and placed well, it looked just like the real thing from a few hundred yards away. The soldiers got detailed instructions. They painted on unit markings. They added “battle damage” and mud. They placed the decoys the way real armies would. The tanks even came with fake treads that could be pressed into the ground to make it look as if heavy vehicles had passed.

The second kind was sound trickery, and it may have been the most inventive. The unit’s sound engineers had a library of recordings made at Fort Knox, Kentucky. The recordings used real tanks, trucks, artillery, and infantry. They caught the special sound of every major American vehicle and weapon. There was the rumble of M4 Sherman tanks, each with a deep 500-horsepower growl. There was the whine of M3 half-tracks, the clatter of jeeps and trucks, and the crash of artillery. There were even the sounds of soldiers building bridges and forts.

The recordings were pressed onto vinyl records and sorted by vehicle, speed, and distance. In an operation, the engineers picked the right records. They played them through powerful 500-watt amplifiers on half-tracks with huge speakers. They adjusted the volume and tone to match the distance and the land. The sound could carry up to 15 miles. It gave the impression of a whole armored division on the move.

German soldiers later said it was completely convincing. They had no doubt that a large American force was coming.

The third kind was radio trickery, and it was the hardest of all. The radio operators learned to copy how real American units talked on the radio. Each division in the U.S. Army had its own radio “fingerprint.” This was a pattern of frequencies, sending times, code words, and operator habits. Trained German listeners could pick it out.

The Ghost Army’s radio operators studied these fingerprints carefully. They practiced until they could copy them. They sent fake orders, reports, and supply messages. The messages gave German listeners a false picture of where American troops were and what they planned. Together with the inflatable vehicles and the loudspeakers, the radio messages completed the illusion. It was a many-sided fake, and German intelligence found it very hard to see through.

The Ghost Army arrived in Europe in May 1944. The war ended in May 1945. In that time, the unit carried out more than 20 deception operations across France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and Germany.

Operation Elephant was in July 1944. It pretended to be the 6th Armored Division in Brittany. This drew German attention away from the breakout at St. Lô. Operation Bergen was in August 1944. It tricked the Germans about where the 79th Infantry Division was. Operation Bettembourg was in September 1944. It pulled German attention from the U.S. Third Army’s crossing of the Moselle River.

But the largest and most important operation was Operation Viersen. It took place in March 1945, when the Allies crossed the Rhine River. The Rhine was the last great natural barrier between the Allied armies and the heart of Germany.

Operation Viersen was a masterpiece of trickery. The real Rhine crossing was called Operation Plunder. British Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery led it. It was planned for the area around Wesel and Rees. The Ghost Army’s job was to make the Germans think the crossing would be about 20 miles to the north, near the town of Viersen.

To do it, the 1,100 men of the Ghost Army pretended to be the entire 30th and 79th Infantry Divisions. That was about 40,000 men, with all their vehicles, guns, and supplies. The unit put hundreds of inflatable tanks, trucks, and guns across a wide area. It looked like two full divisions getting ready for a big river attack.

The sound teams played tank engines, vehicle convoys, and the building of pontoon bridges for hours. Pontoon bridges are bridges that float on boats. The radio operators sent a flood of fake messages that matched what two divisions would send before an attack.

It worked beautifully. The Germans moved two full divisions to the Viersen area to meet the crossing that did not exist. They were the 6th Parachute Division and the 15th Panzergrenadier Division. This weakened the defenses at the real crossing point. It saved thousands of American and British lives. Many people see Operation Viersen as the Ghost Army’s finest hour. It is one of the most successful battlefield tricks in military history.

For more than fifty years after the war, the men of the Ghost Army kept their secret. They had promised silence during the war. They were told that their mission was classified, so they could not talk about it. They could not tell their wives or children. They could not even talk to other veterans.

The unit’s existence was made public only in 1996. That was when the Freedom of Information Act led to the release of documents. Even then, few people knew the story. Then the filmmaker Rick Beyer made the documentary The Ghost Army in 2013. In 2015 came the book The Ghost Army of World War II, which he wrote with Thomas H. Tillison.

In 2022, Congress passed a law to give the Ghost Army the Congressional Gold Medal. It is Congress’s highest civilian honor. The award ceremony was held on March 21, 2024, at the U.S. Capitol. By then, only seven surviving members were known to be alive. Three of them came in person. They were Seymour Nussenbaum (100), Bernard Bluestein (100), and John Christman (99).

“It was like putting on a big production,” Nussenbaum said. “We have had in some cases people impersonating generals, putting on a general’s uniform and walking around the streets.”

The Ghost Army was one of the most unusual units in the history of war. It was a battalion of artists and actors. They used creativity, technology, and sheer nerve to fool one of the most capable military intelligence systems in the world. Their inflatable tanks never fired a shot. But their sounds and fake radio messages pulled real German divisions away from real American soldiers. They saved an estimated 15,000 to 30,000 lives.

For more than fifty years, the unit worked in almost complete secrecy. Its members could not tell their families what they had done. When the story came out, it showed a side of World War II that was almost unreal in its cleverness. A traveling show of rubber tanks and recorded sound proved that war is not always won by firepower. Sometimes it is won by the willingness to believe. It can also be won by the creative skill to make the enemy believe something that is not there. How many other secret tricks of the war are still unknown?
