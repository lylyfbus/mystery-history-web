The Ghost Army: How Fake Tanks and Sound Effects Helped Win WWII

In 1944, in the last year of World War II, Allied armies were fighting their way across Europe. The U.S. Army sent out a very strange weapon. It had no guns, no armor, and no explosives. It could not blow up a bridge or capture a hill. Yet some estimates say that it saved the lives of 15,000 to 30,000 American soldiers.

The weapon was the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops. It is better known as the Ghost Army. Its story stayed secret for more than fifty years.

The unit was activated on January 20, 1944. It had about 1,100 men, and it was unlike anything the U.S. military had made before. Many of its soldiers were not hardened fighters. They were artists, designers, actors, sound engineers, and architects. Many came from art schools and advertising firms. Their job was not to fight the enemy. It was to fool him.

They had inflatable tanks that could be blown up with air pumps. They had powerful loudspeakers on half-tracks, which were army trucks with tank-like tracks at the back. These could play the sounds of a whole armored division. People could hear them from about 15 miles away. Their radio operators sent carefully made fake messages to trick German listeners. Together they put on a traveling show of trickery. It ran across France and into Germany, as far as the Rhine River.

The Ghost Army was the idea of two men. Ralph Ingersoll was a journalist and publisher who had co-founded PM, a liberal newspaper in New York. Billy Harris was a U.S. Army planner. Together they created the unit.

The idea came from the British. They had perfected military deception in the North African desert. It was led by the clever Dudley Clarke and his group, called “A Force.” One of their most famous tricks was Operation Bertram. It came before the Battle of El Alamein in late 1942. They used dummy tanks, fake supply dumps, and false information. They wanted the German commanders to think that the main attack would come from the south, not the north.

The British success helped to convince American planners that a special U.S. unit could be useful. So the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops was activated at Camp Forrest, Tennessee. It later finished its training at Camp Pine, New York, which is now Fort Drum. Many men were chosen for their creativity and their technical skills.

To find recruits, the Army did not look only for sharpshooters or foot soldiers. It also looked at art schools and advertising agencies. It wanted people skilled in visual tricks, sound, and stage work. The work was secret, so many men did not know what they were joining.

The Ghost Army drew some of the most talented creative minds of its day. Bill Blass later became a famous American fashion designer. He served in the unit.

Ellsworth Kelly later became a famous abstract painter and sculptor. He served in the Ghost Army too. So did Arthur Singer, who later became a well-known painter of birds.

These men, and hundreds like them, brought great skill to the work. They knew that fooling the enemy took more than a few inflatable tanks. It needed a complete illusion for every sense. It needed the look of a camp, the sound of engines, and the radio chatter of a busy headquarters. The Ghost Army was a little like a theater company, and Europe in wartime was its stage.

The Ghost Army used three main kinds of trickery. Each one aimed at a different way the Germans gathered information. The first, and the most striking to look at, was inflatable tanks, trucks, jeeps, and airplanes. Special companies made them for the Army. Set up correctly, they looked very real.

An inflatable tank was far lighter than a real one. Filled with air and placed well, it could look real from a distance. The soldiers placed the decoys the way a real army would.

The second kind was sound trickery, and it may have been the most inventive. The unit’s sound engineers had recordings made at Fort Knox, Kentucky. The recordings used real tanks, trucks, and soldiers. They caught the sounds of American vehicles on the move and of soldiers at work.

In an operation, the engineers played the recordings through heavy speakers on half-tracks. They adjusted the sound to match the distance and the land. The sound could carry up to 15 miles. It gave the impression of a whole armored division on the move.

The third kind was radio trickery, and it was the hardest of all. The radio operators learned to copy how real American units talked on the radio. Some could even copy the sending style of a particular Morse code operator. German listeners could learn to know these habits.

The Ghost Army’s radio operators studied these habits carefully. They sent fake orders, reports, and supply messages. The messages gave German listeners a false picture of where American troops were and what they planned. Together with the inflatable vehicles and the loudspeakers, the radio messages completed the illusion. It was a many-sided fake.

The Ghost Army arrived in Europe in May 1944. It reached Normandy that summer. In the next year, the unit carried out more than 20 deception operations across France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and Germany.

In August 1944, near Brest in France, some men pretended to be tank units of the 6th Armored Division. In September 1944, in Operation Bettembourg, the unit pretended to be the whole 6th Armored Division. This hid thin defenses near Metz. In December 1944, a radio-only trick called Operation Kodak was used during the Battle of the Bulge.

The best-known operation was Operation Viersen. It took place in March 1945, when the Allies crossed the Rhine River. The Rhine was the last great natural barrier between the Allied armies and the heart of Germany.

Operation Viersen was part of a plan by the U.S. Ninth Army. It was to hide where, and when, the Ninth Army would cross the Rhine. The real crossing was on March 23, 1945. The Ghost Army’s job was to make the Germans think that the 30th and 79th Infantry Divisions were gathering in a different place.

To do it, the 1,100 men of the Ghost Army pretended to be two full divisions. Together, the two divisions had about 30,000 to 40,000 men, as different sources say. The unit put hundreds of inflatable vehicles and guns across a wide area. It looked like two divisions getting ready for a big river attack.

The sound teams played tank engines, vehicle convoys, and the building of pontoon bridges for hours. Pontoon bridges are bridges that float on boats. The radio operators sent a flood of fake messages that matched what two divisions would send before an attack.

The unit’s official history says that the operation was a success. An officer of the 30th Division called the real attack a “complete surprise to the enemy.” Another officer found a German map that showed the Americans in the wrong places. Nobody can say how many lives the trick saved.

For more than fifty years after the war, the men of the Ghost Army kept their secret. They had promised silence during the war. They were told that their mission was classified, so they could not talk about it. They could not tell their wives or children.

The unit’s story stayed classified until 1996. Even then, few people knew it. Then the filmmaker Rick Beyer made the documentary The Ghost Army in 2013. In 2015 came the book The Ghost Army of World War II, which he wrote with Elizabeth Sayles.

In 2022, a law was signed to give the Ghost Army the Congressional Gold Medal. It is one of the highest civilian honors in the United States. The award ceremony was held on March 21, 2024, at the U.S. Capitol. By then, only seven surviving members were known to be alive. Three of them came in person. They were Seymour Nussenbaum (100), Bernard Bluestein (100), and John Christman (99).

“It was like putting on a big production,” Nussenbaum said.

The Ghost Army was one of the most unusual units in the history of war. It was a battalion of artists and actors. They used creativity, technology, and sheer nerve to fool German forces. Their inflatable tanks never fired a shot. Some estimates say that the unit saved 15,000 to 30,000 lives.

For more than fifty years, the unit’s story stayed secret. Its members could not tell their families what they had done. When the story came out, it showed a side of World War II that was almost unreal in its cleverness. A traveling show of rubber tanks and recorded sound showed that war is not only about firepower. Sometimes it also depends on making the enemy believe something that is not there. How many other secret tricks of the war are still unknown?
