The Ghost Army: How Fake Tanks and Sound Effects Helped Win WWII

Three old men and a big production

On March 21, 2024, three very old men came to the U.S. Capitol in Washington. They were Seymour Nussenbaum, who was 100, Bernard Bluestein, who was also 100, and John Christman, who was 99. Congress was about to give their old army unit the Congressional Gold Medal. It is one of the highest civilian honors in the United States.

By then, only seven surviving members of the unit were known to be alive. Nussenbaum looked back at the war and said, “It was like putting on a big production.”

He meant it. The unit had no guns and no armor. It could not blow up a bridge or capture a hill. Yet some estimates say that it saved the lives of 15,000 to 30,000 American soldiers. It was called the Ghost Army, and its story stayed secret for more than fifty years. How can an army win without fighting?

Artists in uniform

The official name was the 23rd Headquarters Special Troops. It was activated on January 20, 1944, at Camp Forrest, Tennessee. It had about 1,100 men. It later finished its training at Camp Pine, New York, which is now Fort Drum.

Many of its soldiers were not hardened fighters. They were artists, designers, actors, sound engineers, and architects. Many came from art schools and advertising firms. Their job was not to fight the enemy. It was to fool him.

Some became famous later. Bill Blass became a well-known American fashion designer. Ellsworth Kelly became a famous painter and sculptor. Arthur Singer became a painter of birds. All three served in the unit.

These men knew that a few rubber tanks were not enough. They needed a complete illusion. The unit was a little like a theater company, and Europe in wartime was its stage. But who had the idea?

A trick learned from the British

The Ghost Army was the idea of two men. Ralph Ingersoll was a journalist and publisher who had co-founded PM, a newspaper in New York. Billy Harris was a U.S. Army planner. Together they created the unit.

The idea came from the British. They had perfected military deception in the North African desert. It was led by the clever Dudley Clarke and his group, called “A Force.” One of their most famous tricks was Operation Bertram, before the Battle of El Alamein in late 1942. They used dummy tanks, fake supply dumps, and false information. They wanted the German commanders to think that the main attack would come from the south, not the north.

That success helped to convince American planners that a special U.S. unit could be useful. The American version would use three kinds of trickery. Each one aimed at a different way that the Germans gathered information.

Tanks full of air

The first kind was the one that you could see. The unit had inflatable tanks, trucks, jeeps, and airplanes. Special companies made them for the Army, and the soldiers blew them up with air pumps.

An inflatable tank was far lighter than a real one. But if it was filled with air and placed well, it looked real from a distance. The soldiers placed the decoys the way that a real army would.

The eyes were only one way to be fooled. Next came the ears.

The sound of a whole division

The second kind may have been the most inventive. The unit’s sound engineers made recordings at Fort Knox, Kentucky. They used real tanks, trucks, and soldiers. The recordings caught the sounds of American vehicles on the move and of soldiers at work.

In an operation, the engineers played the recordings through heavy speakers on half-tracks. A half-track is an army truck with tank-like tracks at the back. The engineers adjusted the sound to match the distance and the land. It could be heard from about 15 miles away. It gave the impression of a whole armored division on the move.

A German soldier could see a field of tanks and hear the engines. But the Germans also listened to the radio.

Voices on the radio

The third kind was the hardest of all. The radio operators learned to copy how real American units talked on the radio. Some could even copy the sending style of a particular Morse code operator. German listeners could learn to know these habits.

The Ghost Army’s operators studied these habits carefully. They sent fake orders, reports, and supply messages. The messages gave German listeners a false picture of where American troops were and what they planned.

Together with the rubber vehicles and the loudspeakers, the radio messages completed the show. Now the unit only needed a stage.

Show time on the Rhine

The Ghost Army arrived in Europe in May 1944. It reached Normandy that summer. In the next year, the unit carried out more than 20 deception operations across France, Belgium, Luxembourg, and Germany.

In September 1944, in Operation Bettembourg, it pretended to be the whole 6th Armored Division. This hid thin defenses near Metz. In December 1944, a radio-only trick called Operation Kodak was used during the Battle of the Bulge.

The best-known operation was Operation Viersen, in March 1945. The Allies were about to cross the Rhine River, the last great natural barrier between them and the heart of Germany. The real crossing was on March 23, 1945. The Ghost Army’s job was to make the Germans think that the 30th and 79th Infantry Divisions were gathering in a different place.

So the 1,100 men pretended to be two full divisions. Together, two divisions had about 30,000 to 40,000 men, as different sources say. The unit put hundreds of inflatable vehicles and guns across a wide area. The sound teams played tank engines and vehicle convoys for hours. They also played the building of pontoon bridges, which are bridges that float on boats. The radio operators sent a flood of fake messages that matched what two divisions would send before an attack.

The unit’s official history says that the operation was a success. An officer of the 30th Division called the real attack a “complete surprise to the enemy.” Another officer found a German map that showed the Americans in the wrong places. Nobody can say how many lives the trick saved.

Fifty years of silence

After the war, the men kept their secret. They had promised silence, and they were told that their mission was classified. They could not tell their wives or children.

The unit’s story stayed classified until 1996. Even then, few people knew it. In 2013, the filmmaker Rick Beyer made the documentary The Ghost Army. In 2015 came the book The Ghost Army of World War II, which he wrote with Elizabeth Sayles. In 2022, a law was signed to give the unit its Gold Medal.

So we know the story now. Rubber tanks never fired a shot, and some estimates say that the unit saved 15,000 to 30,000 lives. We do not know the exact number. Think again of three men at the Capitol in 2024, aged 100, 100, and 99. They had kept their secret for a very long time. Sometimes war depends on making the enemy believe something that is not there. How many other secret tricks of the war are still unknown?
