Dyatlov Pass Incident: The 1959 Mystery Where 9 Hikers Died in Ways That Still Defy Explanation

On the night of February 1, 1959, nine young hikers put up their tent on a frozen mountain slope in Russia. The local Mansi people called the mountain Kholat Syakhl, which means “Dead Mountain.” The hikers were students from the Ural Polytechnic Institute in the Soviet Union. By morning, every one of them would be dead.

Their leader was Igor Dyatlov, a 23-year-old engineering student and experienced trekker. Their trip was rated Category III, the hardest level of hiking in the Soviet Union. The group was fit, well prepared, and used to the harsh winters of the northern Ural Mountains.

Search parties found the camp three weeks later. The scene was so strange that the Soviet officials sealed the case files. They said the hikers died from a “compelling natural force.” That phrase answered nothing and raised every question. For more than sixty years, people have argued about what really happened.

The group had nine hikers. Igor Dyatlov was 23. Zinaida Kolmogorova was 22. Lyudmila Dubinina was 20. Alexander Kolevatov was 25, and Rustem Slobodin was 23. Georgy Krivonischenko was 24, Yuri Doroshenko was 24, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles was 24. Alexander Zolotarev was 37. He was the oldest and a veteran of World War II.

They set out on January 23, 1959. They were heading toward Mount Otorten in the northern Urals. It was a remote and punishing route, even for skilled winter climbers. The hikers kept diaries and took photographs. Their trip was hard but quiet, until the last night.

On February 1, Dyatlov’s group left their planned route. Nobody knows why. They made camp on the open eastern slope of Kholat Syakhl. The forest valley nearby would have given them more shelter. The temperature was about -30°C (-22°F). What happened next has fed books, films, and scientific papers ever since.

The first search teams reached the slope on February 26, 1959. The group had been expected back almost a month earlier. The tent was still standing, half-buried in snow. But someone had cut it open from the inside with a knife.

Inside, the hikers’ belongings, shoes, warm clothes, and supplies were still neatly in place. Footprints led away from the tent. They were made by people wearing socks, one boot, or nothing on their feet. The footprints went down the slope toward the edge of a forest about 1.5 kilometers away.

That night the temperature fell to -30°C. Something had driven nine experienced hikers to cut their way out of their only shelter. They ran into the freezing dark half-dressed. Whatever it was, it must have been sudden and terrifying.

Investigators later pieced the night together from the evidence, the autopsies, and the diaries and photographs. They think the hikers ran from the tent in two or three groups. Some reached the edge of the forest and found a large cedar tree. Others seem to have tried to go back. Their bodies were found along the slope between the cedar and the camp, as if they had fallen while trying to crawl home in the dark.

The groups met different ends, but all nine died. The question that has puzzled investigators and amateur detectives for six decades sounds simple. What made them run?

The bodies were found over weeks and months. Each new find made the mystery deeper. The first two were Yuri Doroshenko and Georgy Krivonischenko. They were found on February 27 at the foot of the cedar tree. They had no shoes and wore only underwear.

The branches of the cedar were broken up to nearly five meters high. Someone may have climbed it in desperation. Perhaps they were looking for the tent, or escaping something on the ground. Both men’s hands were burned, and Krivonischenko’s were burned badly. They may have grabbed burning branches to get warm or to signal for help. Both died of hypothermia, which is deadly cold. They had no other injuries.

Three more bodies were found between late February and early March. They lay between the cedar tree and the tent. Igor Dyatlov was found in a pose that suggested he was crawling back to the tent. His fist was closed around a tree branch. Zinaida Kolmogorova lay face-down in the snow, also on the way back to camp.

Rustem Slobodin was close by. The autopsy showed that his skull was cracked. That injury was serious, but it would not have killed him on its own. All three died of hypothermia. It looked as if they had tried to return to shelter in the dark and cold, and had not made it.

Then came a discovery that turned a sad hiking accident into a deep mystery. On May 4, 1959, searchers found the last four bodies. They were in a ravine, about 75 meters from the cedar tree. They were buried under four meters of snow. They were Lyudmila Dubinina, Alexander Kolevatov, Alexander Zolotarev, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles.

Their injuries were not what the searchers or doctors expected. Dubinina, at 20 the youngest woman in the group, had a badly crushed chest. The chief medical examiner said the force was “equal to the effect of a car crash.” Yet her body had no wounds on the outside. She also had injuries to her face.

Thibeaux-Brignolles had a badly broken skull. Zolotarev and Kolevatov had broken ribs, and again there were no marks on the skin. The clothes of some of these four tested positive for unusual levels of radioactivity. Several of the victims’ skin had a strange orange-brown or grey color.

Doctors, physicists, and avalanche experts have studied these reports for decades. The key detail is how the last four were hurt. Dubinina’s chest needed a huge force. The medical examiner compared it to being hit by a fast car. Zolotarev’s ribs were broken on both sides. Thibeaux-Brignolles’ skull was broken in many places.

Yet none of them had the cuts or bruises that such injuries usually bring. No fallen rocks lay near the bodies. There was no sign of a fight or an animal attack. The force had broken bones without breaking skin. That points to extreme pressure, like being caught in an avalanche or crushed by a heavy, even weight.

Some experts say the injuries to Dubinina’s face came after death, from decay and small animals. Others do not find that explanation convincing. They point out that the rest of her body was in good condition.

Since 1959, people have suggested almost every explanation you can imagine. The Soviet investigation was led by Lev Ivanov. In 1959 he decided that the deaths were caused by a “compelling natural force.” This was a deliberately vague answer that satisfied nobody. The case stayed officially open but was effectively closed. The files were secret for decades.

The best-known theory is an avalanche. The hikers cut into the slope to make a flat place for their tent. Strong winds then piled heavy snow on top. A slab of snow, which is a large block of packed snow, broke loose in the night. It struck the tent, hurt some hikers, and sent the rest running in panic.

In 2021, two Swiss researchers, Johan Gaume and Alexander Puzrin, published a study in the journal Communications Earth & Environment. They used advanced computer models. They showed that a rare, delayed avalanche could explain the chest injuries, the cracked skull, and the panicked flight. The slide could have started hours after the slope was cut. Earlier avalanche theories could not explain that delay.

Other theories have lived on beside the avalanche. One is the katabatic wind theory. A katabatic wind is cold air rushing down a slope. The theory says sudden gusts of hurricane strength hit the tent and made it impossible to stay.

The infrasound theory is different. Infrasound is sound too low for people to hear. Wind passing over the round top of Kholat Syakhl might have made such waves. They can bring feelings of panic and dread, and the hikers might have felt a terror they could not explain.

The military weapons test theory looks at the radioactive clothing, the secret files, and the Soviet military sites nearby. It says the hikers may have walked into a testing area and been hurt by weapons. It says officials then covered it up.

The paradoxical undressing theory explains why some hikers had few clothes. In severe cold, a dying body can feel hot by mistake, and people take their clothes off. This explains the missing clothes, but not the injuries.

One of the most interesting people in the case is Lev Ivanov himself. He was the lead investigator in 1959, and for years he was troubled by what he had seen on Kholat Syakhl. In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union fell, he spoke openly about things that had been hidden.

He said that several groups of witnesses had seen strange glowing balls, or “fire orbs,” in the sky over the northern Urals on the night of February 1, 1959. Among them were hikers and weather workers. Ivanov came to believe that something unusual, perhaps an unknown event in the air, had terrified the hikers.

UFO fans have seized on his words, and skeptics have dismissed them. Either way, they add one more layer to a case that already has many. Ivanov died in 1997. To the end, he insisted that the official investigation had not told the whole truth.

In 2020 and 2021, Russian authorities reopened the case. They decided that a snow avalanche was the most likely cause. Many families of the hikers did not accept this. They said it did not explain all the evidence.

The 2021 study by Gaume and Puzrin was the most careful scientific attempt to model the event. Their computer test showed that a slow-forming avalanche could fit the facts. Three things came together: the slope cut by the hikers, snow blown in by the wind, and the shape of the mountain. The result matched the injuries, with broken ribs, a cracked skull, and enough panic to drive the group out of the tent.

Scientists praised the study. But even its authors said they could not prove what happened. They only showed that this was one possible way.

The Dyatlov Pass Incident is more than a cold case. It is like a mirror for each age that studies it. In the 1960s, people saw Cold War secrets and Soviet cover-ups. In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union fell, it looked like a story of government lies finally revealed.

In the 2000s, people turned to the paranormal and strange lights in the sky. In 2021, it became a story about computer science and its power to model a disaster. Each age finds the answer it wants to believe. But the mountain itself says nothing.

Nine young people went into the cold on the night of February 1, 1959, and never came back. They were students, engineers, a veteran, and a photographer. Their tent was cut open from the inside. Their bodies lay across the slope, the forest, and the ravine. Some had injuries like those from a car crash. Some of their clothing was radioactive.

The official explanation was a phrase that explained nothing. Today the pass where they died bears Igor Dyatlov’s name, and a memorial stands where the tent was found. What really drove them out into the night? The mountain keeps its secrets.
