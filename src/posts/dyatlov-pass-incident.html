Dyatlov Pass Incident: The 1959 Mystery Where 9 Hikers Died in Ways That Still Defy Explanation

On the night of February 1, 1959, nine young hikers put up their tent on a frozen mountain slope in Russia. The name of the mountain, Kholat Syakhl, comes from the Mansi people of the region. It is usually said to mean “Dead Mountain,” but the translation is not certain. Most of the hikers were students from the Ural Polytechnic Institute in the Soviet Union. By morning, every one of them would be dead.

Their leader was Igor Dyatlov, a 23-year-old engineering student and experienced trekker. Their trip was rated Category III, the highest level of hiking certificate at that time. The group was fit, well prepared, and used to the harsh winters of the northern Ural Mountains.

Search parties found the tent almost four weeks later. The scene was so strange that it puzzled the Soviet investigators. They said the hikers died from a “compelling natural force.” That phrase answered nothing and raised every question. For more than sixty years, people have argued about what really happened.

The group had nine hikers. Most were in their early twenties. Igor Dyatlov, Rustem Slobodin, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles were 23. Yuri Doroshenko was 21. The others were Zinaida Kolmogorova, Lyudmila Dubinina, Alexander Kolevatov, and Georgy Krivonischenko. Alexander Zolotarev was in his late thirties. He was the oldest, a sports instructor, and a veteran of World War II.

They set out on January 23, 1959. A tenth hiker, Yuri Yudin, turned back a few days later because he was ill. The group was heading toward Mount Otorten in the northern Urals. It was a remote and punishing route, even for skilled winter climbers. The hikers kept diaries and took photographs. Their trip was hard but quiet, until the last night.

On February 1, Dyatlov’s group left their planned route. Nobody knows for sure why. Some think a storm pushed them off course. They made camp on the open slope of Kholat Syakhl. The forest valley nearby would have given them more shelter. The temperature was about -25 to -30°C (-13 to -22°F). What happened next has fed books, films, and scientific papers ever since.

The first search teams reached the slope on February 26, 1959. The group had been expected to send word about two weeks earlier. The tent was collapsed and covered with snow. But someone had cut it open from the inside.

Inside, the hikers’ belongings, shoes, warm clothes, and supplies were still neatly in place. Footprints led away from the tent. Most were made by people wearing socks or nothing on their feet. The footprints went down the slope toward the edge of a forest about 1.5 kilometers away.

That night it was very cold. Something had driven nine experienced hikers to cut their way out of their only shelter. They ran into the freezing dark half-dressed. Whatever it was, it must have been sudden and terrifying.

Investigators tried to piece the night together from the evidence, the autopsies, and the diaries and photographs. Some hikers reached the edge of the forest and a large Siberian pine tree. Others seem to have tried to go back. Their bodies were found along the slope between the tree and the camp, as if they had been trying to return in the dark.

The groups met different ends, but all nine died. The question that has puzzled investigators and amateur detectives for six decades sounds simple. What made them run?

The bodies were found over weeks and months. Each new find made the mystery deeper. The first two were Yuri Doroshenko and Georgy Krivonischenko. They were found at the foot of the pine tree, near the remains of a small fire. They had no shoes and wore only underwear.

The branches of the pine were broken up to about five meters high. Someone may have climbed it in desperation. Perhaps they were looking for the tent. Krivonischenko had burns, and they may have come from the fire the men made to get warm. Both died of hypothermia, which is deadly cold.

Three more bodies were found on the slope between the pine tree and the tent. Igor Dyatlov was found in a place that suggested he was heading back to the tent. Zinaida Kolmogorova was also on the way back to camp.

Rustem Slobodin was found later, also apparently trying to return. The autopsy showed a small crack in his skull. That injury was not thought to be deadly. All three died of hypothermia. It looked as if they had tried to return to shelter in the dark and cold, and had not made it.

Then came a discovery that turned a sad hiking accident into a deep mystery. On May 4, 1959, searchers found the last four bodies. They were in a ravine, about 75 meters from the pine tree. They were buried under four meters of snow. They were Lyudmila Dubinina, Alexander Kolevatov, Alexander Zolotarev, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles.

Their injuries were not what the searchers or doctors expected. Dubinina had a badly crushed chest. A doctor said the force was “equal to the effect of a car crash.” She also had injuries to her face.

Thibeaux-Brignolles had a badly broken skull. Zolotarev also had a crushed chest. Some clothing showed traces of radiation. Some of the bodies had a strange orange-brown color.

Doctors, physicists, and avalanche experts have studied these reports for decades. The key detail is how the last four were hurt. Dubinina’s chest needed a huge force. The doctor compared it to being hit by a car. Thibeaux-Brignolles’ skull was badly broken.

Investigators found no proof of a fight or a crime. Some experts say such injuries point to extreme pressure, like being caught in an avalanche.

Some experts say the injuries to the faces came after death, from the water and small animals. Others do not find that explanation fully convincing.

Since 1959, people have suggested almost every explanation you can imagine. The Soviet investigation was led by Lev Ivanov. In 1959 he decided that the deaths were caused by a “compelling natural force.” This was a vague answer that satisfied nobody. The case was closed.

The best-known theory is an avalanche. The hikers cut into the slope to make a flat place for their tent. Strong winds then piled heavy snow on top. A slab of snow, which is a large block of packed snow, broke loose in the night. It struck the tent, hurt some hikers, and sent the rest running in panic.

In 2021, two Swiss researchers, Johan Gaume and Alexander Puzrin, published a study in the journal Communications Earth & Environment. They used advanced computer models. They suggested that a rare, delayed avalanche could explain the injuries and the panicked flight. The slide could have started about 9 to 14 hours after the slope was cut. Earlier avalanche ideas had trouble explaining that delay.

Other theories have lived on beside the avalanche. One is the katabatic wind theory. A katabatic wind is cold air rushing down a slope. A 2019 expedition from Sweden and Russia said this was possible. The theory says sudden, very strong gusts hit the tent and made it impossible to stay.

The infrasound theory is different. Infrasound is sound too low for people to hear. The writer Donnie Eichar proposed in a 2013 book that wind might have made such waves. He wrote that they can bring feelings of panic and dread.

The military weapons test theory looks at the radioactive clothing. It says the hikers may have walked into a testing area and been hurt by weapons. No evidence for this has been found. Other people say the traces of radiation came from thorium in camping lanterns.

The paradoxical undressing theory explains why some hikers had few clothes. In severe cold, a dying body can feel hot by mistake, and people take their clothes off. This may explain the missing clothes, but not the injuries.

One of the most interesting people in the case is Lev Ivanov himself. He was the lead investigator in 1959, and for years he was troubled by what he had seen on Kholat Syakhl. In 1990, he said that officials had ordered him to dismiss reports of flying spheres. Witnesses had reported orange spheres in the sky in the area.

UFO fans have seized on his words, and skeptics have dismissed them. Either way, they add one more layer to a case that already has many. Ivanov said the team had no clear explanation.

In 2019, Russian authorities reopened the case. In 2020, they named a snow avalanche as the cause. Some people did not accept this. They said it did not explain all the evidence.

The 2021 study by Gaume and Puzrin was a well-known scientific attempt to model the event. Their computer test suggested that a slow-forming avalanche could fit the facts. Two things came together: the slope cut by the hikers and snow blown in by the wind. They said the result matched the injuries in the autopsy reports.

Some critics said it does not explain why the hikers left the tent without their clothes. Even the authors said they do not claim to have solved the mystery. They only showed that this was one possible way.

The Dyatlov Pass Incident is more than a cold case. It is like a mirror for each age that studies it. In the 1960s, people saw Cold War secrets and Soviet cover-ups. In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union fell, it looked like a story of government lies finally revealed.

In the 2000s, people turned to the paranormal and strange lights in the sky. In 2021, it became a story about computer science and its power to model a disaster. Each age finds the answer it wants to believe. But the mountain itself says nothing.

Nine young people went into the cold on the night of February 1, 1959, and never came back. They were students and a veteran sports instructor. Their tent was cut open from the inside. Their bodies lay across the slope, the forest, and the ravine. Some had very serious injuries. Some of their clothing showed traces of radiation.

The official explanation was a phrase that explained nothing. Today the pass where they died bears Igor Dyatlov’s name. What really drove them out into the night? The mountain keeps its secrets.
