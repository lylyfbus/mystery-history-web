Dyatlov Pass Incident: The 1959 Mystery of 9 Hikers Whose Deaths Are Still Argued About

A tent on the Dead Mountain

On the night of February 1, 1959, nine young hikers put up their tent on a frozen mountain slope in Russia. The name of the mountain, Kholat Syakhl, comes from the Mansi people of the region. It is usually said to mean “Dead Mountain,” but the translation is not certain. By morning, every one of the hikers would be dead.

This is a sad story, and we will tell it with care. These were real people, and each one had a name.

Search parties found the tent almost four weeks later. The scene was so strange that it puzzled the Soviet investigators. For more than sixty years, people have argued about what really happened. So let us begin with the hikers themselves.

Nine hikers and one who turned back

Most of the hikers were students from the Ural Polytechnic Institute in the Soviet Union. Their leader was Igor Dyatlov, a 23-year-old engineering student and experienced trekker. Their trip was rated Category III, the highest level of hiking certificate at that time. The group was fit, well prepared, and used to the harsh winters of the northern Ural Mountains.

Most were in their early twenties. Igor Dyatlov, Rustem Slobodin, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles were 23. Yuri Doroshenko was 21. The others were Zinaida Kolmogorova, Lyudmila Dubinina, Alexander Kolevatov, and Georgy Krivonischenko. Alexander Zolotarev was in his late thirties. He was the oldest, a sports instructor, and a veteran of World War II.

They set out on January 23, 1959. A tenth hiker, Yuri Yudin, turned back a few days later because he was ill. The group was heading toward Mount Otorten. It was a remote and punishing route, even for skilled winter climbers. The hikers kept diaries and took photographs. Their trip was hard but quiet, until the last night.

On February 1, the group left its planned route. Nobody knows for sure why. Some think a storm pushed them off course. They made camp on the open slope. The forest valley nearby would have given them more shelter. The temperature was about -25 to -30°C (-13 to -22°F).

A tent cut open from the inside

The first search teams reached the slope on February 26, 1959. The group had been expected to send word about two weeks earlier. The tent was collapsed and covered with snow. But someone had cut it open from the inside.

Inside, the hikers’ belongings, shoes, warm clothes, and supplies were still in place. Footprints led away from the tent. Most were made by people wearing socks or nothing on their feet. The footprints went down the slope toward the edge of a forest about 1.5 kilometers away.

Think about what this means. Nine experienced hikers cut their way out of their only shelter. They went into the freezing dark half-dressed. Whatever it was, it must have been sudden and frightening.

Investigators tried to piece the night together from the evidence, the autopsies, and the diaries and photographs. And the slope held more clues.

What the slope showed

The bodies were found over weeks and months. The first two were Yuri Doroshenko and Georgy Krivonischenko. They lay at the foot of a large Siberian pine tree, near the remains of a small fire. They had no shoes. The branches of the pine were broken up to about five meters high. Someone may have climbed it, perhaps to look for the tent. Both died of hypothermia, which is deadly cold.

Three more were found on the slope between the pine tree and the tent. They were Igor Dyatlov, Zinaida Kolmogorova, and later Rustem Slobodin. They seem to have been trying to go back to the shelter in the dark. Slobodin had a small crack in his skull, but it was not thought to be deadly. All three died of hypothermia.

Then came a discovery that turned a sad hiking accident into a deep mystery. On May 4, 1959, searchers found the last four hikers. They were in a ravine, about 75 meters from the pine tree, under four meters of snow. They were Lyudmila Dubinina, Alexander Kolevatov, Alexander Zolotarev, and Nicolai Thibeaux-Brignolles.

Their injuries were not what the doctors expected. A doctor said the force that hurt Dubinina was “equal to the effect of a car crash.” Thibeaux-Brignolles had a badly broken skull. Investigators found no proof of a fight or a crime. Some clothing showed traces of radiation.

Some experts say such injuries point to very strong pressure, as in an avalanche. So what did the officials decide?

An answer that answered nothing

The Soviet investigation was led by Lev Ivanov. In 1959 he decided that the deaths were caused by a “compelling natural force.” That phrase answered nothing and raised every question. The case was closed.

Ivanov stayed troubled by what he had seen on Kholat Syakhl. In 1990, he said that officials had ordered him to dismiss reports of flying spheres. Witnesses had reported orange spheres in the sky in the area. UFO fans have seized on his words, and skeptics have dismissed them. Ivanov said the team had no clear explanation.

In 2019, Russian authorities reopened the case. In 2020, they named a snow avalanche as the cause. Some people did not accept this. They said it did not explain all the evidence.

Since 1959, people have suggested almost every explanation you can imagine. The best known is the avalanche.

Snow that slid late

The idea is simple. The hikers cut into the slope to make a flat place for their tent. Strong winds then piled heavy snow on top. A slab of snow, which is a large block of packed snow, broke loose in the night. It struck the tent, hurt some hikers, and sent the rest running in panic.

Earlier avalanche ideas had trouble explaining one thing. Why would the snow slide so long after the slope was cut?

In 2021, two Swiss researchers, Johan Gaume and Alexander Puzrin, published a study in the journal Communications Earth & Environment. They used advanced computer models. They suggested that a rare, delayed avalanche could explain the injuries and the panicked flight. The slide could have started about 9 to 14 hours after the slope was cut. They said the result matched the injuries in the autopsy reports.

Some critics say it does not explain why the hikers left the tent without their clothes. Even the authors say they do not claim to have solved the mystery. They only showed that this was one possible way.

But other ideas have lived on beside it.

Wind, sound, and secrets

One is the katabatic wind theory. A katabatic wind is cold air rushing down a slope. A 2019 expedition from Sweden and Russia said this was possible. Sudden, very strong gusts could have hit the tent and made it impossible to stay.

The infrasound theory is different. Infrasound is sound too low for people to hear. The writer Donnie Eichar proposed in a 2013 book that wind might have made such waves. He wrote that they can bring feelings of panic and dread.

The weapons test theory looks at the clothing with traces of radiation. It says the hikers may have walked into a testing area. No evidence for this has been found. Other people say the radiation came from thorium in camping lanterns.

Another idea explains why some hikers had few clothes. In severe cold, a dying body can feel hot by mistake, and people take their clothes off. This is called paradoxical undressing. It may explain the missing clothes, but not the injuries.

The mountain keeps its secrets

Each age finds the answer it wants to believe. In the 1960s, people saw Cold War secrets. After the Soviet Union fell, it looked like a story of hidden lies. Later, people turned to strange lights in the sky. In 2021, it became a story about computer models.

So what do we know? Nine young people went into the cold on the night of February 1, 1959, and did not come back. Their tent was cut open from the inside. Their bodies lay across the slope, the forest, and the ravine. The official answer explained nothing, and an avalanche is now the best-known idea.

What is still open? Why they left the tent, and what exactly happened that night. Today the pass where they died bears Igor Dyatlov’s name. The mountain keeps its secrets.
