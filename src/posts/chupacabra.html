Chupacabra: The Blood-Sucking Beast That Terrorized Latin America and Became a Global Legend

The chupacabra — literally “goat sucker” in Spanish — burst onto the scene in 1995 Puerto Rico, killing livestock across Latin America and becoming one of the most famous cryptids in modern history.

Eight sheep and three small holes

It was March 1995 in Puerto Rico. A farmer went out to his sheep pen and found a strange sight. Eight of his sheep lay dead inside the fence. There was no sign of a struggle.

The animals had no torn flesh, and nothing was scattered around. Instead, each sheep had only three small, round holes in the chest. The story says that every drop of blood was gone.

Some people first thought that a cult had done it, in some kind of dark ritual. But the neat holes puzzled them. So did the lack of blood and the number of dead animals.

Within months, reports came from all over the island. Many goats, chickens, and other animals were said to be found dead and drained of blood. Fear spread through the farming towns. Men with guns searched the hills at night.

Now the creature has a name that you may know. How did it get it?

A name from a comedian

The word “chupacabra” means “goat sucker” in Spanish. It was not made up by a scared farmer or a scientist. It is credited to a Puerto Rican comedian named Silverio Pérez, who invented the name in 1995.

The word was catchy and easy to remember. It spread through Puerto Rican media and became the name that everyone used. Soon it appeared in English-language newspapers too.

Within a year, the legend crossed the Caribbean to mainland Latin America. Then it reached the United States, and then the world. Today the chupacabra is one of the best-known cryptids on Earth. A cryptid is an animal that people report but science has not proved.

Strange stories were already in the air. In 1975, animals in the town of Moca were found drained of blood. People called those events the “vampiro de Moca.” But nothing matched what began in 1995. And soon a woman said she saw the creature itself.

The woman who saw it hop

After the first attack, reports poured in from all over the island. Many came from the town of Canóvanas. In August 1995, a woman named Madelyne Tolentino said that she saw the creature up close.

She described a creature with large eyes and spines along its back. It moved, she said, by hopping like a kangaroo.

A sketch based on her story ran in a Puerto Rican newspaper in August 1995. Her description became the standard picture of the chupacabra. It appeared in many drawings, news stories, and witness reports in the years after.

The panic was real. Some accounts say that up to 150 animals were killed in the Canóvanas area. Armed volunteers searched the hills around the town. They never found the creature. But the news turned a rural folk tale into a global story.

Two monsters, one name

Here is a strange thing. Witnesses in different places describe very different creatures. The original one from Puerto Rico is small and walks on two legs. It has spines and big eyes, and people say it hops.

The chupacabra of Texas and Mexico is another animal altogether. It has four legs and looks like a hairless dog. Its skin is gray-blue, with prominent fangs and a thin, bony look. The two share almost nothing except a reputation for killing farm animals and draining their blood.

Over the next few years, the creature was reported across Latin America. A big wave of reports came from Chile, with sightings around the city of Calama in 2000. It was in Texas that the chupacabra got its second look.

In August 2004, a rancher named Devin McAnally shot a strange animal near Elmendorf, Texas. It weighed about twenty pounds and had a severe overbite. Its skin was blue and bare. Experts at the San Antonio Zoo guessed that it might be a Mexican hairless dog. Then a DNA test at the University of California, Davis gave the answer. DNA is the code in every living thing that shows what it is.

Many people think the Elmendorf Beast was a coyote with mange. The DNA was damaged, so it could only show for sure that the animal was a canine. Mange is a skin disease caused by tiny mites.

Another famous case came from Cuero, Texas, in 2007. A woman named Phylis Canion found a hairless animal that had been killed on the road on her ranch. Her photographs went viral. DNA tests in several labs gave similar results. The animal had a coyote mother and a Mexican wolf father. Canion thought that it might be a new kind of canine. Scientists say that a coyote-wolf mix explains it.

So the bodies were known animals. But what about the first creature, the one that hops?

A monster from the movies

Some researchers say that the first Puerto Rican description was shaped by a film. In 1995, just before the sightings in Canóvanas, the movie Species came out in theaters. It showed an alien-human creature named Sil, played by Natasha Henstridge. Sil walked on two legs and had a row of spines down its back.

An investigator named Benjamin Radford spent five years studying the chupacabra. He found that Madelyne Tolentino had seen the film in the weeks before her sighting. He said that her description matched Sil closely.

Radford wrote about this in his book Tracking the Chupacabra (2011). He suggests that her memory of the film may have shaped what she thought she saw. Or the many pictures of Sil may have prepared people across the island to see a vague shape at night as the movie’s monster.

If he is right, the Puerto Rican chupacabra is a movie creation that walked off the screen. That cannot be proved. But there is a second idea for the Texas creatures, and it is stronger.

The sick coyote

John Tomeček is an associate professor at Texas A&M University who studies wildlife. In his view, most chupacabra sightings are likely coyotes in the late stage of mange.

Tiny mites burrow into the skin of a sick animal. This causes strong itching, loss of fur, sores, scabs, and thick skin. As the mites spread, the animal grows thin. Seen at dusk, it can look eerie.

Tomeček noted something important. Fur is the last to go from between the shoulder blades. That may explain the reports of spines or a ridge on the back.

Mange also changes how an animal acts. Sick, desperate animals grow bolder and go to homes for food. A weak animal may go after small farm animals kept in pens. Blood in a dead animal settles and clots, and that can make it look drained. A bite to the neck can look like puncture marks.

DNA tests on supposed chupacabra bodies have found known animals. No DNA of an unknown species has been reported. The Texas A&M AgriLife service says that most sightings are likely coyotes with late-stage mange. It also notes that cities now have more coyotes and other wild animals, so people meet them more often.

Why the legend stays alive

Even so, the chupacabra will not go away. It appears in television shows and documentaries. It is on T-shirts, coffee mugs, and toys, and in songs, comic books, and video games.

In Latin America, it holds a place like that of the Loch Ness Monster in Scotland or Bigfoot in North America. It started as a local legend and became a world symbol of the unknown.

There is also money in the story. Souvenirs and tours bring in visitors in some places. Cuero, Texas, drew attention after the photographs went viral. As with other cryptid legends, there is a reason to keep the mystery alive. A proven chupacabra would be worth far less than one nobody can find.

Back at the sheep pen

So the chupacabra is almost certainly not one unknown species that drains the blood of farm animals on two continents. The tested bodies were coyotes or coyote-wolf mixes, and mange can make a sick animal look like a monster. The first Puerto Rican description may have come, perhaps strongly, from a film.

But one part is still open. Nobody has shown for sure what killed those eight sheep in March 1995.

So the chupacabra is real in one way. It shows how fear, the news, and old stories can turn something unclear into a monster that many people believe in. Was it only a film and a sick coyote? The evidence points that way. But the farmer’s question from that morning has never been fully answered.
