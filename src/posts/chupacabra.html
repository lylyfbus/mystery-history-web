Chupacabra: The Blood-Sucking Beast That Terrorized Latin America and Became a Global Legend

The chupacabra — literally “goat sucker” in Spanish — burst onto the scene in 1995 Puerto Rico, killing livestock across Latin America and becoming one of the most famous cryptids in modern history.

It was March 1995 in Puerto Rico. A farmer went out to his sheep pen and found a strange sight. Eight of his sheep lay dead inside the fence. There was no sign of a struggle.

The animals had no torn flesh, and nothing was scattered around. Instead, each sheep had only three small, round holes in the chest. The story says every drop of blood was gone.

Some people first thought a cult had done it, in some kind of dark ritual. But the neat holes puzzled them. So did the lack of blood and the number of dead animals.

Within months, reports came from all over Puerto Rico. Many goats, chickens, and other animals were said to be found dead and drained of blood.

Fear spread through the farming towns. Men with guns searched the hills at night. And people gave a name to the creature that witnesses said they saw in the shadows: el chupacabras, which means “the goat sucker.”

Within a year, the legend had crossed the Caribbean to mainland Latin America. Then it reached the United States, and then the world. Now the chupacabra is one of the best-known cryptids on Earth. A cryptid is an animal that people report but science has not proved. Nobody has ever confirmed that the chupacabra exists. But its mark on popular culture is clear. Its story also tells us a lot about how people think, how the media works, and how much we love a good monster story.

Strange stories were already in the air. The island had a rich tradition of folk tales about supernatural creatures. In 1975, animals in the town of Moca were found drained of blood. People called those events the “vampiro de Moca.” They had set a pattern. But nothing matched what began in the spring of 1995.

After the first attack, reports poured in from all over the island. Many came from the town of Canóvanas. In August 1995, a woman named Madelyne Tolentino said she saw the creature up close.

She described a creature with large eyes and spines along its back. It moved, she said, by hopping like a kangaroo.

A sketch based on her story ran in a Puerto Rican newspaper in August 1995. Her description became the standard picture of the chupacabra. It appeared in many drawings, news stories, and witness reports in the years after.

The panic was real and widespread. Some accounts say that up to 150 animals were killed in the Canóvanas area. Armed volunteers searched the hills around the town.

They never found the creature. But news stories at home and around the world turned the chupacabra from a rural folk tale into a global story.

The word “chupacabra” means “goat sucker” in Spanish. It was not coined by a scared farmer or a scientist. It is credited to a Puerto Rican comedian named Silverio Pérez, who made up the name in 1995.

The word was catchy and easy to remember. It spread through Puerto Rican media. It became the name everyone used. Soon “chupacabra” appeared in English-language newspapers too.

One strange thing about the chupacabra is that witnesses in different places describe very different creatures. The original one from Puerto Rico is small and walks on two legs. It has spines, big eyes, and reptile-like skin, and people say it hops.

The chupacabra of Texas and Mexico is another animal altogether. It has four legs and looks like a hairless dog. Its skin is gray-blue, with prominent fangs and a thin, bony look. The two share almost nothing except a reputation for killing farm animals and draining their blood.

Over the next few years, the creature was reported across Latin America. A big wave of reports came from Chile. In 2000, there were sightings around the city of Calama. In each place the descriptions were different. Some witnesses described the two-legged creature from Puerto Rico. Others described something more like a dog. But it was in Texas that the chupacabra got its second, and probably more lasting, look.

In August 2004, a rancher named Devin McAnally shot a strange animal near Elmendorf, Texas. It weighed about twenty pounds and had a severe overbite. Its skin was blue and bare.

Experts at the San Antonio Zoo could not say for sure what it was. They guessed it might be a Mexican Hairless Dog. Then a DNA test at the University of California, Davis gave the answer. DNA is the code in every living thing that shows what it is.

The Elmendorf Beast was a coyote with mange. The DNA was damaged, so it could only show for sure that the animal was a canine. Mange is a skin disease caused by tiny mites. It was not a cryptid or an alien or a new species. The strange look came from a terrible infestation of parasites.

The most famous Texas case came from Cuero, Texas, in 2007. A woman named Phylis Canion found a hairless animal that had been killed on the road on her ranch. She took photographs, and the pictures went viral. She sent DNA samples to several labs. Later the body was preserved and put on display in her living room.

The DNA results were similar in every lab. The animal had a coyote mother and a Mexican wolf father. In other words, it was a mix of a coyote and a wolf. Canion thought it might be a new kind of canine. Scientists say that a coyote-wolf mix explains it.

The “spines” that witnesses described may be a ridge of fur. On sick animals, fur is the last to go from between the shoulder blades.

Here is one of the most surprising twists in the history of cryptid research. Some researchers say the first Puerto Rican description was shaped by a Hollywood film. In 1995, just before the sightings in Canóvanas, the movie Species came out in theaters.

The film showed an alien-human creature named Sil, played by Natasha Henstridge. Sil walked on two legs and had a row of spines down its back.

An investigator named Benjamin Radford spent five years studying the chupacabra. He found that Madelyne Tolentino, the witness whose description set the standard, had seen the film in the weeks before her sighting. He said her description matched Sil closely.

Radford wrote about this in his book Tracking the Chupacabra (2011). He suggests that Tolentino’s memory of the film’s creature may have shaped what she thought she saw. Or the many pictures of Sil may have prepared people across Puerto Rico to see a vague shape at night as the movie’s monster.

If he is right, then the Puerto Rican chupacabra, at least, is not a cryptid at all. It is a movie creation that walked off the screen and into the real world.

John Tomeček, Ph.D., is an associate professor at Texas A&M University who studies wildlife. In his view, most chupacabra sightings are likely coyotes in the late stage of mange.

Tiny mites burrow into the skin of the sick animal. This causes strong itching, loss of fur, sores, scabs, and thick skin. The signs match the chupacabra descriptions very well.

As the mites spread, the animal loses its fur. Its skin thickens and it grows thin. Seen at dusk, it can look eerie.

Tomeček noted something important. Fur is the last to go from between the shoulder blades. This may explain the reports of spines or a ridge on the back.

Mange also changes how an animal acts. Sick, desperate animals grow bolder and go to homes for food. A weak animal may go after small farm animals kept in cages or pens. The bite to the neck of a small animal may make it look as if its blood is gone.

Radford spent five years looking into the chupacabra. His conclusion appeared in his book Tracking the Chupacabra: The Vampire Beast in Fact, Fiction, and Folklore (2011). He wrote that the main Puerto Rican description came from the film. He also wrote that later sightings in Texas and Mexico were mostly sick canines.

Tomeček says that blood in a dead animal settles and clots, and that can make it look drained. Predators often kill by biting the neck, and the bite can look like puncture marks.

DNA tests have been done on supposed chupacabra bodies. They found known animals. The 2004 Elmendorf Beast was a coyote with mange. Phylis Canion’s 2007 Cuero animal was a coyote-wolf mix.

No DNA of an unknown species has been reported. The Texas A&M AgriLife service says that most sightings are likely coyotes with late-stage mange. It also notes that cities now have more coyotes and other wild animals, so people meet them more often.

Even so, the chupacabra will not go away. It has appeared in television shows, documentaries, and reality shows.

The chupacabra appears on T-shirts, coffee mugs, and toys. It shows up in songs, comic books, and video games.

In Latin America, it holds a place like that of the Loch Ness Monster in Scotland or Bigfoot in North America. It started as a local legend and became a world symbol of the unknown.

There is also money in the story. Chupacabra souvenirs and tours bring in visitors in some places. The town of Cuero, Texas, drew attention after Phylis Canion’s photographs went viral. As with other cryptid legends, there is a reason to keep the mystery alive. A proven chupacabra would be worth far less than one nobody can find.

So the chupacabra is almost certainly not real, at least not as one unknown species that drains the blood of farm animals on two continents. The DNA evidence is clear. The bodies that were tested were known animals. They were coyotes or coyote-wolf mixes, and mange can make a sick animal look like a monster.

The first Puerto Rican description may have come, perhaps strongly, from the creature in the film Species. That would be life copying the movies.

Yet the chupacabra story is real in a way that matters. It shows how fear, the media, and old stories can turn unclear experiences into shared certainty. It shows how a comedian’s joke can become a legend. A movie monster can walk off the screen and into the countryside. A sick coyote can become a vampire beast.

Was it only a film and a sick coyote? Or did the first farmer see something that nobody has found since? The chupacabra lives where evidence meets imagination, and there science rarely has the last word.
