Anastasia Romanov: The Princess Who Vanished and the Woman Who Spent 60 Years Pretending to Be Her

Grand Duchess Anastasia Romanova, the youngest daughter of Russia’s last Tsar, whose alleged survival became one of history’s most enduring mysteries

It was the middle of the night on July 17, 1918. In the Russian city of Yekaterinburg, eleven people stood in the basement of a plain house. Then a group of armed men came in and shot them. The men were Bolsheviks, the party that had taken power in Russia.

One of the eleven was Tsar Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia. Another was his wife, Tsarina Alexandra. With them were their four daughters, Olga, Tatiana, Maria, and Anastasia. Their son Alexei, the heir to the throne, was there too. He had hemophilia, an illness of the blood. The other four were loyal servants who had chosen to stay with the family as prisoners.

The killing was cruel and badly done. Afterward, the bodies were put on a truck and driven into the forest. The men tried to hide them in a mine. That did not work well. So they moved the bodies again and buried them in a second secret grave.

The Bolshevik government said that the Tsar was dead. But at first it denied killing the rest of the family. That doubt opened a door, and a very bold pretender walked through it.

For more than sixty years, a woman known as Anna Anderson told the world a story. She said she was Grand Duchess Anastasia Nikolaevna Romanova, the Tsar’s youngest daughter. She said she had lived through the shooting and escaped to the West. Millions of people believed her. Some members of Europe’s royal families believed her too.

Her story led to dozens of court cases. It inspired books and films.

In the end, DNA testing showed the truth. DNA is the code inside every living cell, and it is different for each person. Anna Anderson was not Anastasia at all. Her DNA matched the family of a Polish factory worker named Franziska Schanzkowska. The real Anastasia had died in that basement in 1918. But for sixty years, the world was not so sure.

The Romanov family had ruled Russia for more than three hundred years. Nicholas II became Tsar in 1894. He was stubborn and deeply religious. Many historians think he was not the right man to lead a country that was close to revolution.

His wife was Alexandra Fyodorovna. She was born Alix of Hesse, and she was a granddaughter of Queen Victoria. She came to depend more and more on a holy man named Grigori Rasputin. He seemed able to ease the illness of young Alexei. That gave him great influence inside the royal home.

Anastasia Nikolaevna was born on June 18, 1901. She was the fourth and youngest daughter. Her family called her “shvibzik,” which means something like “little mischief.” She earned the name because she was full of energy, loved tricks, and did not always obey. Many people who knew her loved her.

But the Tsar badly needed a son to rule after him. The family’s hopes rested on her younger brother, Alexei.

Alexei’s hemophilia means that the blood does not stop flowing as it should. The illness came down through his mother’s side, from Queen Victoria. It was the family’s private pain.

World War I destroyed what was left of the Tsar’s power. In March 1917, people in the city of Petrograd rioted because they had no bread. In the old Russian calendar, the month was February. The riots grew into a full revolution. Nicholas was away, leading the Russian army at the front. He was forced to give up his throne.

The new Provisional Government kept the family under guard. First they lived at the Alexander Palace at Tsarskoye Selo. Then they were moved to the Governor’s Mansion in Tobolsk, in Siberia.

The Bolsheviks took power in the October Revolution. In April 1918, they moved the family to Yekaterinburg, a city in the Ural Mountains. They locked them in a merchant’s house that they had taken for this purpose. It was called the House of Special Purpose.

The building was called the Ipatiev House. A high wooden fence stood all around it.

By July 1918, an army called the Czechoslovak Legion was coming closer to Yekaterinburg. Its soldiers were former prisoners of war who were fighting against the Bolsheviks. The Bolsheviks feared that this army might set the Romanovs free.

The man in charge of the house was Yakov Yurovsky. On the night of July 16 to 17, the local Ural Regional Soviet ordered the whole family to be killed. Historians still argue whether Moscow gave the order. A later investigation found no written order from Lenin.

In the middle of the night, Yurovsky woke the family and their servants. He told them to get dressed and come downstairs. He said there was trouble in the town and they had to go somewhere safer.

They were led to a small basement room with one barred window. Chairs were brought for Alexandra and Alexei. Then Yurovsky came in with a group of armed men. He read a short statement. It said the family was to be shot by order of the Ural Regional Soviet, the local Bolshevik council. Then the shooting began.

Everything went wrong. The room was small, and smoke filled the air, so the men could not see.

Some of the children did not die at once. The reason was hidden in their clothes. The grand duchesses had sewn diamonds and other jewels into their clothes. The jewels were secret money, in case the family had to run away. Now the stones partly protected them from the bullets. That made the killing last longer. The whole thing took about twenty minutes. When it ended, all eleven people were dead.

Yurovsky and his men put the bodies on a truck and drove into the Koptyaki forest. They first put the bodies in a shallow mine shaft.

During the next days, the men moved the remains to a second place and buried them in a grave. Yurovsky later wrote an account. It said that two of the bodies were burned and buried apart from the others. Those two were Alexei and one of his sisters. Many years later, that detail became very important.

In July 1918, the Czechoslovak Legion and the White Army captured Yekaterinburg. The White Army was fighting against the Bolsheviks. An investigator named Nikolai Sokolov studied the site. He found many of the family’s belongings. But he never found the hidden grave. With no bodies, stories about survivors could spread freely.

In 1979, two men went looking. One was Alexander Avdonin, a Russian geologist, a scientist who studies rocks and the ground. The other was Geli Ryabov, a filmmaker. They used Yurovsky’s written account. In the Koptyaki forest near Yekaterinburg, they found the grave.

They found skulls and bones. But the two men were afraid of the KGB, the Soviet secret police. So they kept their secret for years.

In 1991, the Soviet Union came to an end. Then the grave was opened officially. Nine bodies were found.

Scientists compared DNA from the bones with blood from living relatives. One of them was Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh. He was related to the Romanovs through Queen Victoria. The tests, finished in 1994, showed who most of the dead were.

But two bodies were still missing. They were Alexei and one of the daughters. Which daughter was it, Anastasia or Maria? That question stayed open for many years.

Now go back to February 1920. In Berlin, a young woman was pulled out of the Landwehr Canal. She had tried to end her own life. She was taken to the Dalldorf Asylum, a hospital for people with mental illness. There she refused to give her name or say anything about who she was.

Another patient said she recognized her as one of the Romanov daughters. Over the next months, the story slowly grew. First the patient said she was Grand Duchess Tatiana. Later the woman was said to be Anastasia.

People called her “Fräulein Unbekannt,” which is German for “Miss Unknown.” Later she was known as Anna Anderson. “Anna” was a short form of Anastasia. She was called “Anderson” when she traveled to the United States. Over sixty years, she became the most famous royal pretender of modern times.

Some people supported her. But Romanov relatives said no. Princess Irene of Hesse, the tsarina’s sister, did not recognize her. Grand Duchess Olga Alexandrovna, the real Anastasia’s aunt, said firmly that this woman was not her niece. In 1927, Alexandra’s brother, the Grand Duke of Hesse, ordered a study that named her as Franziska Schanzkowska.

Anderson’s claim filled the newspapers. Her case went to German courts for decades. She wanted the law to say she was Anastasia. In 1970, the case ended. The court said her claim was not proven, but it did not say for certain who she was. So many people went on believing her.

Anderson was not the only one. Many other people said they were Romanovs who had survived. Most of these claims were dismissed.

None of them was ever proven right. But Anderson’s case was by far the most famous. It was not settled until after she died.

The answer came in the 1990s, after her death in 1984. Scientists took DNA from tissue that a hospital had kept from an operation in 1979. They also tested strands of her hair.

The results were clear. Anna Anderson’s DNA did not match the Romanov family. It matched a relative of Franziska Schanzkowska. She was a Polish factory worker, born in 1896. She was reported missing from her room in Berlin in early 1920. That was the same year Anna Anderson appeared in Berlin.

So the woman who made many people believe she was a Russian princess was not a princess at all. Why she made the claim, we may never know.

In 2000, the Russian Orthodox Church made Nicholas II and his family saints. It named them “passion bearers,” or strastoterptsy in Russian. This is a kind of saint who meets death with Christian faith and a humble heart.

Not everyone agreed. Some said Nicholas II was a ruler with deep faults, and that his choices added to the suffering of millions. Others said the family met death with faith and patience.

In 1998, the nine bodies found in 1991 were laid to rest in the Peter and Paul Cathedral in St. Petersburg. This is the church where the Romanov rulers were buried.

Then, in 2007, the last piece fell into place. Amateur searchers found a small pit near the first grave, just as Yurovsky’s account had said. It held the remains of two more people. DNA tests showed that one was Alexei. Russian experts say the other was his sister Maria. Some American experts said it might be Anastasia. Either way, both were found, and no daughter was missing. In 2008, Russian officials cleared the Romanov family’s name. They said the family had been victims of political repression, which means the state had punished them unfairly.

Some Church leaders have not accepted that the two remains are really Alexei and his sister. Still, nobody has found any sign that a Romanov child lived. All five Romanov children had died in that basement.

At its heart, this is a story about the stories people tell when the truth is too terrible. The real Anastasia was a lively, playful girl of seventeen. She was taken into a basement and killed with her whole family. There was no escape and no secret rescue. No hidden princess was waiting to take back her throne. The DNA evidence is very strong.

Yet the legend lived for a very long time. The missing bodies kept it alive. So did the boldness of Anna Anderson. And so did a deep human wish to believe that a young girl had lived through the worst.

The story lasted because the world was not ready to accept that a princess could die like that. Now we know that she did. One question is left for us. Why do we want so much to believe in a happy ending?
