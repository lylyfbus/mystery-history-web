Anastasia Romanov: The Princess Who Vanished and the Woman Who Spent 60 Years Pretending to Be Her

Grand Duchess Anastasia Romanova, the youngest daughter of Russia’s last Tsar, whose alleged survival became one of history’s most enduring mysteries

It was the middle of the night on July 17, 1918. In the Russian city of Yekaterinburg, eleven people stood in the basement of a plain house. Then a group of armed men came in and shot them. The men were Bolsheviks, the party that had taken power in Russia.

One of the eleven was Tsar Nicholas II, the last Emperor of Russia. Another was his wife, Tsarina Alexandra. With them were their four daughters, Olga, Tatiana, Maria, and Anastasia. Their son Alexei, the heir to the throne, was there too. He had hemophilia, an illness of the blood. The other four were loyal servants who had chosen to stay with the family as prisoners.

The killing was cruel and badly done. Afterward, the bodies were put on a truck and driven into the forest. The men tried to destroy them and hid them in a mine. The mine was not deep enough. So they moved the bodies again and buried them in a second secret grave.

The Bolshevik government said that the Tsar was dead. But at first it denied killing the rest of the family. That doubt opened a door, and a very bold pretender walked through it.

For more than sixty years, a woman known as Anna Anderson told the world a story. She said she was Grand Duchess Anastasia Nikolaevna Romanova, the Tsar’s youngest daughter. She said she had lived through the shooting and escaped to the West. Millions of people believed her. Some members of Europe’s royal families believed her too.

Her story led to dozens of court cases. It inspired books and films.

In the end, DNA testing proved the truth. DNA is the code inside every living cell, and it is different for each person. Anna Anderson was not Anastasia at all. She was a Polish factory worker named Franziska Schanzkowska. The real Anastasia had died in that basement in 1918, just as the records had always suggested. But for sixty years, the world was not so sure.

The Romanov family had ruled Russia for more than three hundred years. Nicholas II became Tsar in 1894. For a ruler with total power, he was not a cruel man. But he was stubborn and deeply religious. He was also the wrong man to lead a country that was close to revolution.

His wife was Alexandra Fyodorovna. She was born Alix of Hesse, and she was a granddaughter of Queen Victoria. She guarded her family fiercely. She came to depend more and more on a holy man named Grigori Rasputin. He seemed able to ease the illness of young Alexei. That gave him great power inside the royal home.

Anastasia Nikolaevna was born on June 18, 1901. She was the fourth and youngest daughter. Her family called her “shvibzik,” which is Russian for “little imp.” She earned the name because she was full of energy, loved tricks, and did not always obey. The people who knew her loved her.

But the Tsar badly needed a son to rule after him. The family’s hopes rested on her younger brother, Alexei.

Alexei’s hemophilia means that the blood does not stop flowing as it should. The illness came down through his mother’s side, from Queen Victoria. It was the family’s private pain. It also helped to cut them off from the Russian people.

World War I destroyed what was left of the Tsar’s power. In March 1917, people in the city of Petrograd rioted because they had no bread. In the old Russian calendar, the month was February. The riots grew into a full revolution. Nicholas was away, leading the Russian army at the front. He was forced to give up his throne.

The new Provisional Government kept the family under guard. First they lived at the Alexander Palace at Tsarskoye Selo. Then they were moved to the Governor’s Mansion in Tobolsk, in Siberia.

The Bolsheviks took power in the October Revolution. In April 1918, they moved the family to Yekaterinburg, a city in the Ural Mountains. They locked them in a merchant’s house that they had taken for this purpose. It was given a new name, the House of Special Purpose.

The building was called the Ipatiev House. A high wooden fence stood all around it.

By July 1918, an army called the Czechoslovak Legion was coming closer to Yekaterinburg. Its soldiers were former prisoners of war who were fighting against the Bolsheviks. The Bolshevik leaders feared that this army might set the Romanovs free.

The man in charge of the house was Yakov Yurovsky. On the night of July 16 to 17, he received orders from Moscow. The whole family was to be killed.

At about 2:00 in the morning, Yurovsky woke the family and their servants. He told them to get dressed and come downstairs. He said there was trouble in the town and they had to go somewhere safer.

They were led to a small basement room with one barred window. Chairs were brought for Alexandra and Alexei. Then Yurovsky came in with a group of armed men. He read a short statement. It said the family was to be shot by order of the Ural Regional Soviet, the local Bolshevik council. Nicholas had time to say “What?” Then the shooting began.

Everything went wrong. The room was small, and smoke filled the air, so the men could not see.

Several of the daughters lived through the first shots. The reason was hidden in their clothes. The grand duchesses had sewn diamonds, emeralds, and other jewels into their underclothes. The jewels were secret money, in case the family had to run away. Now the stones stopped some of the bullets, like rough armor. That only made the killing last longer.

The guards grew angry because their guns did not work quickly. Alexei was still alive, and they shot him again from close by. Anastasia was among the last to die. The whole thing took about twenty minutes. When it ended, all eleven people were dead on the basement floor.

Yurovsky and his men put the bodies on a truck and drove north into the Koptyaki forest. Their first plan was to drop them down a mine shaft, and they did. But the shaft was too shallow, and the bodies could be seen from above.

During the next days, the men tried to destroy the remains with acid and fire. Then they took them to a second place and buried them in a shallow grave. Yurovsky later wrote an account. It said that two of the bodies, Alexei and one daughter, were burned apart from the others. Those two were buried in a different spot. Many years later, that detail became very important.

Later in 1918, the White Army captured Yekaterinburg. The White Army was fighting against the Bolsheviks. An investigator named Nikolai Sokolov studied the site. He found signs of the killing and of what came after. There were burned clothes, pieces of bone, and bullets. But he found no whole bodies. With no bodies, stories about survivors could spread freely.

In 1979, two men went looking. One was Alexander Avdonin, a Russian geologist, a scientist who studies rocks and the ground. The other was Geli Ryabov, a filmmaker. They used Yurovsky’s written account. In the Koptyaki forest near Yekaterinburg, they found the grave.

There were skulls, bones, and other remains that matched the story of the Romanov burial. But the two men were afraid of the KGB, the Soviet secret police. So they buried the remains again. They kept their secret for more than ten years.

In 1991, the Soviet Union came to an end. Then the grave was opened officially. Nine of the eleven bodies were found.

Scientists compared DNA from the bones with blood from living relatives. One of them was Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh. He was related to the Romanovs through Queen Victoria. The tests proved who the dead were, beyond any scientific doubt.

But two bodies were still missing. They were Alexei and one of the daughters. Which daughter was it, Anastasia or Maria? That question stayed open for another sixteen years.

Now go back to February 1920. In Berlin, a young woman was pulled out of the Landwehr Canal. She had tried to end her own life. She was taken to the Dalldorf Asylum, a hospital for people with mental illness. There she refused to give her name or say anything about who she was.

She had scars on her body. She spoke Russian like a person from a high-class family. Another patient said she recognized her as one of the Romanov daughters. The woman did not say yes, and she did not say no.

Over the next months, she slowly let the story grow. First she was Grand Duchess Tatiana. Then she changed her mind. She was Anastasia.

People called her “Fräulein Unbekannt,” which is German for “Miss Unknown.” Later she was known as Anna Anderson. “Anna” came from Anastasia. “Anderson” was the family name of a Russian family she said she had stayed with. Over sixty years, she became the most famous royal pretender of modern times.

Some relatives accepted her at first. One was Grand Duke Ernst Ludwig of Hesse, Alexandra’s brother, who supported her claim in the beginning. Another was Princess Irene of Hesse. But both later took back what they had said.

Other Romanov relatives visited her and said no. One was Grand Duchess Olga Alexandrovna, the real Anastasia’s aunt. She said firmly that this woman was not her niece.

Anderson’s claim filled the newspapers. Her case was the longest-running lawsuit in German legal history. She wanted the law to say she was Anastasia. That would have given her a share of the Romanov fortune.

She lost. But the court never said for certain who she was. So many people went on believing her.

Anderson was not the only one. Between 1918 and the 1990s, more than two hundred people said they were Romanovs who had survived. One was Eugenie Smith, an American woman. In 1963 she published a book about her life, saying she was Anastasia. Another was Natalya Bilikhodze, a woman from Georgia who made the same claim in the 1990s.

None of them was ever proven right. But Anderson’s case was by far the most famous. It was not settled until after she died.

The answer came in 1994, ten years after her death. Scientists took DNA from a small piece of her body that a hospital had kept from a medical test. They also took DNA from a lock of her hair.

The results were clear. Anna Anderson’s DNA did not match the Romanov family. It matched the family of Franziska Schanzkowska. She was a Polish factory worker, born in 1896. She had disappeared from her home in Pomerania in 1920. That was the same year Anna Anderson appeared in Berlin.

So the woman who made millions believe she was a Russian princess was a poor Polish country woman. She had seen a rare chance and taken it.

In 2000, the Russian Orthodox Church made Nicholas II and his family saints. It named them “passion bearers,” or strastoterptsy in Russian. This is a kind of saint who meets death with Christian faith and a humble heart.

Not everyone agreed. Some said Nicholas II was a ruler with deep faults, and that his choices added to the suffering of millions. Others said the family was murdered for their faith, because they would not give up their beliefs.

In 2008, the Russian Supreme Court officially cleared the Romanov family’s name. It ruled that they had been victims of political repression, which means the state had punished them unfairly.

The family’s remains were laid to rest in the SS Peter and Paul Cathedral in St. Petersburg. This is the church where the Romanov rulers were always buried. Relatives of the royal family came to the ceremony, and so did members of the Russian government.

Then, in 2007, the last piece fell into place. Archaeologists found the two missing bodies. They were at a separate spot near the first grave, just as Yurovsky’s account had said.

DNA tests showed that they were Alexei and Maria. They were not Anastasia. The youngest daughter had been in the first grave all the time, among the nine bodies found in 1991. The mystery was over. All five Romanov children had died in that basement.

At its heart, this is a story about the stories people tell when the truth is too terrible. The real Anastasia was a lively, playful girl of seventeen. She was taken into a basement and shot with her whole family. There was no escape. There was no secret rescue. No hidden princess was waiting to take back her throne. The science is certain.

Yet the legend lived for nearly a hundred years. The missing bodies kept it alive. So did the boldness of Anna Anderson. And so did a deep human wish to believe that a young girl had lived through the worst.

The story lasted because the world was not ready to accept that a princess could die like that. Now we know that she did. One question is left for us. Why do we want so much to believe in a happy ending?
