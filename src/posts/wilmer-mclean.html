Wilmer McLean: The Man Who Couldn't Escape the Civil War

A shell in the kitchen fireplace

It is July 1861, in Manassas, Virginia. A cannon shell from the Union side hits a farmhouse. It falls into the kitchen fireplace. And it ruins a dinner meant for a Confederate general.

The general was P.G.T. Beauregard. He and his staff were using the house as their headquarters. Beauregard later wrote about “the destruction of the dinner of myself and staff by a Federal shell.”

The house belonged to a man named Wilmer McLean. He had not asked for any of this. Yet he is now remembered because of what happened in his house, first in 1861 and again four years later. Who was he?

A quiet grocer

Wilmer McLean was born in 1814 and died in 1882. He was not a general, a politician, or a statesman. He was a merchant. He was a wholesale grocer and sugar broker, which means he bought and sold goods in bulk.

In 1853, he married a wealthy widow named Virginia Mason. They settled on her farm, the Yorkshire Plantation, near Manassas, and raised a family. It lay in Prince William County, near a winding stream called Bull Run.

McLean was a retired major in the Virginia militia. He sided with the Confederate cause. But in 1861 he was about 47, too old to serve. Instead he helped the war effort through his business. He sold sugar and other goods to the Confederate Army.

Then the war came to his door. Let us see how.

The general chooses a house

The Civil War broke out in April 1861. In June, General Beauregard came to Manassas and set up his headquarters. He was a showy officer born in Louisiana, and he had led the attack on Fort Sumter that April. He chose McLean’s house as his command post, and McLean let the officers use the property.

On July 18, 1861, the first fighting took place on McLean’s farm. Union troops under General Irvin McDowell met Confederate troops along Bull Run Creek. The full battle broke out on July 21, and the fighting swept over McLean’s land.

This was the First Battle of Bull Run, the first major land battle of the American Civil War. Union cannons fired at the house where Beauregard had his headquarters. That is how the shell landed in the kitchen fireplace.

The battle was a chaotic, bloody Confederate victory. It shocked both sides with its fierceness. For McLean, one thing was enough. Where could he go to get away from the war?

Running away to a quiet village

A second Battle of Bull Run was fought near his property in August 1862. Some sources say he moved soon after that. Others say he moved in spring 1863. He took his family about 120 miles southwest to the tiny village of Appomattox Court House in Virginia.

The village was named for its courthouse. It was a quiet, faraway place, far from the big battlefields. McLean settled his family into a comfortable two-story house on the main road. He went back to his work as a sugar broker, trading with the Confederate Army.

For nearly three years, his plan seemed to work. The war roared across Virginia, at Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville, Gettysburg, the Wilderness, Spotsylvania, and Petersburg. But Appomattox Court House was mostly left alone.

By spring 1865, that was about to change.

The war follows him

Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia was almost finished. General Robert E. Lee’s men were starving, out of ammunition, and cut off from supplies. General Ulysses S. Grant’s much larger forces had them cornered. On April 8, Lee’s men retreated west through Appomattox County, with Grant’s army chasing them.

On the morning of April 9, 1865, Lee’s army made one last desperate try to break through the Union lines west of the village. It failed. Lee sent a message to Grant, asking to meet and talk about surrender.

The two generals needed a calm, handy place to meet. Colonel Charles Marshall, Lee’s military secretary, rode into the village to find a house. He met Wilmer McLean. Marshall asked for a place for the talks.

McLean had spent years trying to avoid the war. He let the generals use his parlor.

Two generals in one parlor

Lee arrived first, around 1:00 PM, with Colonel Marshall. He wore his finest uniform. Grant came about half an hour later. He wore a muddy soldier’s coat with a general’s shoulder straps sewn on. It was a sharp contrast to Lee’s neat dress.

With Grant was his military secretary, Colonel Ely S. Parker, a Seneca Indian, who wrote out the final copy of Grant’s terms. Several of Grant’s officers were also there, including General Edward Ord and General Philip Sheridan.

The meeting was friendly. Grant and Lee had served together in the Mexican-American War. They talked briefly about old times before they got down to business.

Grant wrote out generous terms. Confederate officers could keep their sidearms and personal property. The meeting ended around 3:00 PM, and Lee came out and mounted his horse, Traveller. General Lee had given up to General Grant in McLean’s parlor. That ended the bloodiest war in American history.

McLean is said to have put it this way: “The war began in my front yard and ended in my front parlor.” But something else happened next in that parlor.

The parlor is emptied

As soon as the meeting ended, Union officers began to take things from the McLean House. They knew they had just seen a great moment in American history. They wanted keepsakes.

General Ord bought the table where Lee had sat. He paid $40. General Sheridan bought the table where Grant had sat. He paid $20 in gold. He had General Custer carry it away as a gift for Custer’s wife, Libbie.

Officers also took chairs and other small items as souvenirs. Some paid McLean, and some did not. Soon his parlor was almost bare. He had opened his home to help, and the winners carried off his furniture.

Some items later ended up in museums. The table where Lee sat is now in the Chicago History Museum. The table where Grant sat is in the Smithsonian National Museum of American History.

The house itself had a long road ahead. It was taken apart in 1893, after a plan to show it at the World’s Fair in Chicago fell through. A plan to rebuild it in Washington also failed. The pieces sat for about fifty years.

Congress created the national park in 1940. The National Park Service then rebuilt the house on its old site. It opened to visitors on April 9, 1949, and a formal opening ceremony followed in April 1950.

The man in the middle

The end of the war did not bring McLean peace or money. The surrender at his house did not make him rich. He fell into debt, because after the war he could not repay his loans.

In the fall of 1867, the McLeans left Appomattox and went back to Prince William County. The house was sold at auction in 1869. McLean later lived in Alexandria, Virginia. According to one source, he worked for the Internal Revenue Service, the tax office of the US government, from 1873 to 1876.

It was an ironic fate. He had supplied the Confederate Army, and now he worked for the government that had beaten it. McLean died on June 5, 1882, at the age of 68. He was buried in St. Paul’s Episcopal Cemetery in Alexandria.

Think again of that kitchen fireplace in Manassas. Here is what we know. A shell fell into the house of a quiet grocer in 1861, and the war ended in the parlor of his next house in 1865. Wilmer McLean was not a hero or a villain. He was an ordinary man in very hard times, and he could not get away from the war. Today the rebuilt McLean House at Appomattox still welcomes visitors, and his name is still remembered.
