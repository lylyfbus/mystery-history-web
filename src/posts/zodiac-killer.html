Zodiac Killer: The Unsolved Murders That Terrified California and Defied the FBI

It was the summer of 1969. Hundreds of thousands of young people were gathering on a farm in New York for the Woodstock music festival. Apollo 11 astronauts were walking on the Moon. But in the hills and valleys of Northern California, a very different story was taking place.

A killer was stalking young couples who parked on lonely roads and quiet lakeshores around San Francisco Bay. He did not want only to harm people. He wanted to be known. Between December 1968 and October 1969, he killed at least five people and badly hurt two others.

He sent letters to big newspapers. They held cryptograms, which are messages written in secret code. They held taunts, and they held details of the crimes that only the killer could know. He wore a strange hooded costume with a crossed-circle symbol. He phoned the police from payphones to report his own crimes. He claimed 37 victims, but only seven were ever confirmed.

He called himself the Zodiac. More than half a century later, after one of the biggest investigations in American history, nobody has been charged with his crimes. The Zodiac is still the most famous unsolved serial murder case in the United States. A serial killer is someone who kills several people over time.

Northern California in the late 1960s was a place of sharp contrasts. There was the hippie scene of Haight-Ashbury and anti-war protests at UC Berkeley. There was the early technology boom of Silicon Valley. There were also quiet, working-class towns in the North Bay and in Vallejo.

It had wide-open spaces, winding country roads, and lonely lakeshores. This gave the Zodiac the privacy he needed to stalk, attack, and vanish. Many police groups shared the area. They were the San Francisco Police Department, the Vallejo Police Department, the Napa County Sheriff’s Office, the Solano County Sheriff’s Office, and the FBI. The killer seemed to enjoy the confusion this caused.

The first confirmed attack came on December 20, 1968. It happened on Lake Herman Road, a lonely road on the edge of Vallejo, California. David Faraday, age 17, and his girlfriend Betty Lou Jensen, age 16, were high school students. They had driven to a quiet gravel turnout to talk.

At about 10:15 PM, another car pulled up beside theirs. A man got out with a gun and fired. Both teenagers died. There were no witnesses, no fingerprints, and no leads. Within weeks the case went cold.

Seven months later, on July 4, 1969, Michael Mageau, age 19, and Darlene Ferrin, age 22, were sitting in Ferrin’s car at Blue Rock Springs Park in Vallejo. A man drove up and shone a flashlight in their eyes. Then he fired a 9mm semi-automatic pistol. Ferrin died at the scene.

Mageau was badly hurt but survived. He gave the first description of the attacker. It was a white man, about 26 to 30 years old, stocky, and about 5’8″ tall. Soon after, at about 12:40 AM on July 5, a man called the Vallejo Police Department from a payphone. He claimed both the Blue Rock Springs attack and the Lake Herman Road murders. It was the Zodiac’s first direct contact with the police.

The third confirmed attack came on September 27, 1969. It happened at Lake Berryessa, a large reservoir in Napa County, about 70 miles north of San Francisco. Bryan Hartnell, age 20, and his girlfriend Cecelia Shepard, age 22, were having a picnic on a quiet shore.

A man walked up in a strange hooded costume, like an executioner’s. He wore a black hood with a crossed-circle symbol on the chest. A bib on his front read “By Knife.” He tied up the two young people and attacked them with a knife. Afterward, he used a marker to write a message on the door of their car. He claimed the Lake Herman Road and Blue Rock Springs attacks and drew his crossed-circle symbol.

Shepard died of her injuries two days later. Hartnell survived. He gave a detailed description of the costume and the attack. The Lake Berryessa attack was the most theatrical of the Zodiac’s crimes. It was almost a performance, which suggests a killer who wanted fame as much as he wanted to do harm.

The Zodiac sent at least four cryptograms to newspapers between 1969 and 1970. The first was known as Z 408. It had 408 symbols and was sent in three parts. They went to the Vallejo Times-Herald, the San Francisco Examiner, and the San Francisco Chronicle in late July and early August 1969.

A teacher from Salinas, California, Donald Harden, and his wife Bettye cracked it in a week. They counted how often each symbol appeared. They also guessed that the killer would use the word “killing.” The message said that he liked killing people because it was fun to him. It also said that his victims would be his slaves in the afterlife.

The second and most famous cipher was Z 340. It had 340 symbols and was mailed to the San Francisco Chronicle on November 8, 1969. For 51 years it stayed unsolved. It was one of the longest-lasting code puzzles in history.

An international team finally cracked it in December 2020. The team included David Oranchak, Sam Blake, and Jarl Van Eycke. The message began: “I hope you are having lots of fun in trying to catch me.” But it held nothing that named the killer. Other ciphers are still unsolved or only partly solved. One is Z 13, which begins “My name is___.” Another is Z 32.

The fourth and last confirmed attack came on October 11, 1969. It happened in Presidio Heights, a wealthy neighborhood of San Francisco. Paul Stine, a 29-year-old taxi driver, picked up a passenger near Geary and Masonic Streets. At about 9:55 PM, Stine was found in his cab at Washington and Cherry Streets. He had been shot and had died.

The killer had taken Stine’s wallet and keys. He had also torn pieces from the victim’s shirt. Some of those pieces were later mailed to the San Francisco Chronicle as proof that he was the killer.

Three teenagers across the street saw a man wiping down the cab. Then he walked east on Maple Street toward the Presidio. They described a white man, 25 to 30 years old, 5’8″ to 5’9″, with reddish-brown hair in a crew cut and a heavy build.

But a serious mistake slowed the hunt right away. The police dispatcher, passing on the witnesses’ description, is said to have given the wrong description of the suspect to the officers. This may have given the real killer the minutes he needed to vanish into the dark of the Presidio.

SFPD Inspector Dave Toschi arrived within minutes. He would become one of the most famous detectives in American history. He was the model for Clint Eastwood’s Dirty Harry. A huge manhunt began. Police dogs and officers swept the Presidio. But the Zodiac was gone.

Two days later, a letter reached the San Francisco Chronicle. It held a piece of Paul Stine’s shirt and a detailed account of the crime. It also threatened to attack children on a school bus and to “pick off” people in the streets. The letter was signed with the crossed-circle symbol and the name “the Zodiac.” It was the first time the killer used that name in a confirmed message.

Between July 1969 and January 1974, the Zodiac sent at least 21 confirmed letters. They went to newspapers, police departments, and other people. They were handwritten in a sharp, angular style. They often carried the crossed-circle symbol. They held cryptograms, crime details, threats, and taunts for the police.

“I am crackproof,” he wrote in one letter. In another: “I am not afraid of the gas chamber because it will send me to paradise.” He threatened to blow up school buses and to shoot people on the street. He also threatened to torment his victims’ souls in the afterlife.

The letters turned the Zodiac from a killer into a kind of terrorist of the mind. He used fear as a weapon as well as he used guns and knives. The last confirmed letter came in January 1974. After that, the letters stopped.

In the decades since, police have looked at dozens of suspects. The most famous is Arthur Leigh Allen, who lived from 1933 to 1992. He was a Vallejo schoolteacher who had been convicted of an unrelated crime. Several police groups investigated him closely.

Allen was a large man who looked like the composite sketch. A composite sketch is a drawing of a suspect made from witnesses’ memories. He owned weapons like those the Zodiac used. He is known to have been near several of the attack sites. He is said to have told a friend, before the killings began, that he planned to kill couples in lovers’ lanes and mock the police with letters. If true, that is a strange coincidence.

But DNA taken from the stamps on Zodiac letters in the 2000s did not match Allen. DNA is the code in every living cell that is different for each person. Handwriting experts also kept finding that Allen did not write the letters. He died of a heart attack in 1992, before he could be charged. The strong circumstantial case and the lack of proof from science make him the most debated suspect. Circumstantial means based on clues, not on direct proof.

Other suspects drew some attention. Richard Gaikowski was a newspaper editor and anti-war activist. Detective Narlow investigated him, and he shared features with the composite sketch. Lawrence Kane was linked to the case through Kathleen Johns. She said the Zodiac had taken her and her baby daughter near Modesto in March 1970.

Gary Francis Poste was named as the Zodiac in 2021 by a volunteer cold-case group called “Case Breakers.” They said his name was hidden in the Z 340 cipher. Many people doubt this claim.

Robert Graysmith was a cartoonist at the San Francisco Chronicle. He grew obsessed with the case. He wrote two key books, Zodiac (1986) and Zodiac Unmasked (2002). In the end he pointed to Allen. But people have criticized his work for claiming too much from the evidence and for mistakes in his analysis.

The Zodiac has left a huge mark on culture. The case inspired the 1971 film Dirty Harry. In it, Clint Eastwood plays Inspector “Dirty” Harry Callahan, who hunts a killer like the Zodiac called “Scorpio.” The 2007 film Zodiac was directed by David Fincher. It starred Jake Gyllenhaal, Robert Downey Jr., and Mark Ruffalo. Many people think it is one of the most accurate and moody true crime films ever made.

The Zodiac has also shaped many other works. They range from Thomas Harris’s The Silence of the Lambs to the TV series Mindhunter. He has become the model of the “clever killer who taunts the police.” That idea now shows up all over true crime stories.

People have often tried to use modern science on the evidence from the late 1960s. In the 2000s, experts tested saliva found on the stamps and envelopes of Zodiac letters. They found a partial DNA profile. It did not match the main suspect, Arthur Leigh Allen. But some question how reliable this DNA is. Many people touched the letters over the decades, and the DNA may have been mixed up with other people’s.

In 2020, the cracking of the Z 340 cipher showed that modern computer methods can still give new clues. Some investigators think forensic genealogy might help. This is the method that identified the Golden State Killer in 2018. It uses family-tree databases to find relatives of an unknown person. It could be tried on the partial Zodiac DNA. The case is still officially open with the SFPD, the Vallejo Police Department, and the FBI.

At its heart, the Zodiac case is a story about what is missing. There is no name, no face that is certain, no conviction, and no answer. The killer built his identity from letters, symbols, codes, taunts, threats, and a piece of a dead man’s shirt. His made-up persona has lasted for more than half a century.

The confirmed attacks lasted only ten months. But the Zodiac’s shadow has stretched across decades. It has inspired films, books, documentaries, websites, and a crowd of amateur investigators. They keep studying the evidence, the ciphers, and the theories.

The Z 13 cipher, “My name is___,” is still unsolved. The DNA is still partial. The suspects are still based on clues, not proof. The case is still open. And the question the Zodiac asked the world in his own secret code is still unanswered. Who am I?
