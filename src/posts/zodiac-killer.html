Zodiac Killer: The Unsolved Murders That Terrified California

Two teenagers on a quiet road

On December 20, 1968, two high school students drove to a quiet gravel turnout on Lake Herman Road, near Vallejo, California. David Faraday was 17. His girlfriend, Betty Lou Jensen, was 16. They had come there to talk. Some sources place the road in nearby Benicia.

Another car pulled up beside theirs. A man got out with a gun and fired. Both teenagers died.

That was the first confirmed attack of a killer who wanted to be known. Between December 1968 and October 1969, he killed at least five people and badly hurt two others. He sent letters to newspapers, and he gave himself a name: the Zodiac.

More than half a century later, nobody has been charged with his crimes. Let us look at what he did, and at why he was never caught.

Summer nights in Vallejo

Seven months later, on July 4, 1969, Michael Mageau, age 19, and Darlene Ferrin, age 22, sat in Ferrin’s car at Blue Rock Springs Park in Vallejo. A man drove up and shone a flashlight in their eyes. Then he fired a 9mm pistol. Ferrin died. Mageau was badly hurt, but he lived.

Mageau told the police what he remembered. The attacker was a heavyset white man, about 5 feet 8 inches tall.

Soon after, at about 12:40 AM on July 5, a man called the Vallejo Police Department from a payphone. He claimed both the Blue Rock Springs attack and the Lake Herman Road murders. It was the Zodiac’s first direct contact with the police.

Northern California made the hunt hard. The area had wide-open spaces, winding country roads, and lonely lakeshores. Many police groups shared it, including the San Francisco Police Department, the Vallejo Police Department, the Napa County Sheriff’s Office, the Solano County Sheriff’s Office, and the FBI.

Then the killer showed himself in a new way.

The man in the hood

On September 27, 1969, Bryan Hartnell, age 20, and Cecelia Shepard, age 22, were having a picnic on the shore of Lake Berryessa, a large reservoir in Napa County. A man walked up in a strange hooded costume, like an executioner’s. On the chest of his black hood was a crossed-circle symbol.

He attacked the two young people. Afterward, he used a marker to write on the door of their car. He drew his crossed-circle symbol, wrote the dates of the earlier attacks, and added the words “by knife.”

Shepard died of her injuries two days later. Hartnell survived.

This was the most theatrical of the Zodiac’s crimes. It was almost a performance. Some think that it shows a killer who wanted fame as much as he wanted to do harm.

The next attack was in a city.

A taxi and a piece of shirt

On October 11, 1969, in Presidio Heights, a wealthy neighborhood of San Francisco, a taxi driver named Paul Stine was shot in his cab at about 9:55 PM. It happened at Washington and Cherry Streets. He died.

Witnesses saw a man near the cab and described a white man. But a serious mistake slowed the hunt at once. A police radio call wrongly described the suspect as a Black man. Officers passed over a white man who fit the real description.

SFPD Inspector Dave Toschi would become the best-known detective on the case. A huge manhunt began, but the Zodiac was gone.

Two days later, a letter reached the San Francisco Chronicle. Inside was a piece of Paul Stine’s shirt, sent as proof that the writer was the killer. The letter also threatened children on a school bus.

The confirmed attacks were over. The letters were not.

Letters, taunts, and codes

Between July 1969 and 1974, the Zodiac sent many letters to newspapers and the police. Experts count between about 15 and 22 as real. A taunt is a message that is meant to mock someone. The letters held taunts, crime details, and threats, and many carried the crossed-circle symbol. The killer had first used the name “the Zodiac” in a letter in August 1969. He also wrote, “I am crackproof.” The last letters came in 1974, and after that they stopped.

Some letters held cryptograms, which are messages written in secret code. The first, called Z 408, had 408 symbols. It was sent in three parts to three newspapers in late July and early August 1969. A teacher from Salinas named Donald Harden and his wife Bettye cracked it within days. They were not codebreakers by trade. The message said that he liked killing people because it was fun to him.

The second and most famous cipher was Z 340. It was mailed to the San Francisco Chronicle on November 8, 1969. For 51 years, it stayed unsolved. In December 2020, a team of David Oranchak, Sam Blake, and Jarl Van Eycke finally cracked it. The message began, “I hope you are having lots of fun in trying to catch me.” It held nothing that named the killer.

Another cipher, Z 13, is still unsolved. Some people read it as “My name is___.”

So who was he?

Suspects, but no proof

Police have looked at about 2,500 suspects, and only about six were seen as credible. The most famous is Arthur Leigh Allen, who lived from 1933 to 1992. He was a former elementary school teacher who was convicted of an unrelated crime in 1974. Several police groups looked at him closely.

Allen denied being the Zodiac. Detective Dave Toschi later said that the evidence against him “turned out to be negative.” DNA is the code in every living cell that is different for each person. In 2002, experts tested the stamps on Zodiac letters, and the DNA did not match Allen. But some question how reliable that DNA is, because it may have come from someone else who touched the stamp. Allen died in 1992, before he could be charged.

Robert Graysmith was a cartoonist at the Chronicle who grew obsessed with the case. He wrote Zodiac in 1986 and Zodiac Unmasked in 2002, and in the end he pointed to Allen. Many experts doubt his conclusion.

Other names have come up, too. In 2021, a volunteer group called “Case Breakers” named a man, Gary Francis Poste. Police said that the claim does not hold up.

A shadow over films and books

The Zodiac has left a mark on culture. The 1971 film Dirty Harry has a villain called “Scorpio,” who was inspired by the Zodiac. The 2007 film Zodiac, directed by David Fincher, is based on Graysmith’s books and points toward Allen.

He has also become the model of a clever killer who taunts the police. Yet his fame came from the letters, the symbols, and the codes, not from any proof about who he was.

The question still open

People still try new tools. In 2020, the cracking of the Z 340 cipher showed that modern computer methods can give new clues. Some investigators think that forensic genealogy might help. This method helped catch the Golden State Killer in 2018. It uses family-tree databases to find relatives of an unknown person. Vallejo police tried it on this case in 2018.

So what do we know? Five people died, and two survived. We know the dates, the places, and the names of the people he hurt. What is open is the most important thing: who he was. The case is still listed as open by the FBI, the Vallejo Police Department, and other agencies.

Go back to that gravel turnout on Lake Herman Road. Two young people stopped there to talk, and they never drove home. More than half a century later, there is still no name. The Z 13 cipher still waits for someone to read it.
