Sacsayhuamán: The Inca Fortress Built With Impossible Stones

High on a hill above the old city of Cusco, in Peru, stand three great stone walls. They are 3,701 meters (12,142 feet) above the sea. The air is so thin up there that a modern tourist has to stop and gasp for breath. For more than five hundred years, people have looked at these walls and asked how they were made.

The place is called Sacsayhuaman. You say it SACK-sy-wuh-mun. In Quechua, the language of the Inca, it is written Saqsaywaman, and it means “fortress of the royal falcon.” The first Spanish soldiers who saw it could not believe that human hands had built it.

The three walls run in a zigzag line. Some of their stones are thought to weigh up to 200 tons, which is as heavy as a blue whale. The stones came from quarries 20 to 35 kilometers away, across rough mountain land. A quarry is a place where stone is cut from the ground. The builders used no mortar, the paste that holds bricks together. Yet the stones fit so tightly that you cannot push a razor blade between them.

The Inca builders had no wheels and no iron tools. They had no written language. They had no animals that could pull such weights. The llama was the largest farm animal in the Andes mountains, and it can carry only about 45 kilograms (100 pounds). Still, the Inca raised some of the greatest giant-stone walls on Earth.

Sacsayhuaman sits on a steep hill at the northern edge of Cusco. It is about 2 kilometers from the main square of the city. Cusco was the capital of the Inca Empire. The Inca called their empire Tawantinsuyu, or “Realm of the Four Parts.” It was the largest empire in America before Columbus arrived. At its largest, it reached from modern Colombia to Chile.

The Inca did not write, so they passed their history on by speaking. Spanish writers later wrote these stories down. They say that the building began under Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui, who ruled from 1438 to 1471. He was the emperor who turned a small kingdom into a huge empire. The work went on under the next rulers, Topa Inca Yupanqui and Huayna Capac.

Some people estimate that the building took more than 70 years. Thousands of workers were called in from all parts of the empire. The site covers about 3,093 hectares (7,643 acres). It holds the famous zigzag walls, and also temples, storehouses, water channels, and other buildings. In 1983, UNESCO made Sacsayhuaman a World Heritage Site, as part of the City of Cusco.

The three great walls run side by side, one above the other. Their zigzag line makes 22 sharp corners along the outer face. The longest wall is about 400 meters (1,312 feet) long. In some places the walls are 6 meters (20 feet) tall. No other Inca site has walls with this zigzag shape, and people have argued about its purpose for centuries.

The most common idea is that the zigzag helped in a fight. Defenders on the walls could shoot at attackers from several directions at once. Anyone who tried to climb the walls could be hit from the side.

But the shape also helps in an earthquake. The corners lock together and spread the force of the shaking better than a straight wall can. The Andes have many earthquakes. So some researchers think the zigzag was partly a way to keep the walls standing.

The stonework is what makes Sacsayhuaman so special. At other Inca sites, the stones are cut like bricks and laid in even rows. Here the builders used a style called polygonal stonework. A polygon is a shape with many sides. Each stone was carved into its own odd shape, and it locks exactly into the stones around it.

These are huge blocks. Some have 10, 12, or even more sides. They fit together like the pieces of a giant jigsaw puzzle. More than 500 years have passed, and several terrible earthquakes have shaken them. Even so, you cannot slide a sheet of paper into the joints.

The largest stones are giants. Experts estimate that the heaviest blocks weigh 100 to 200 tons. Some single stones are 5 meters (16 feet) long or more. They are among the largest stones ever moved and fitted in the western half of the world.

So here is the question that has troubled experts for centuries. It sounds simple. How did the Inca move and place stones of 100 to 200 tons? They had no wheels for transport, no iron tools, and no heavy machines. A llama that carries 45 kilograms is no help at all with a 200-ton block.

The most accepted answer is that huge teams of people did the work. This idea comes from a writer named Garcilaso de la Vega, who lived from 1539 to 1616. He was a historian with both Inca and Spanish parents. He wrote down the spoken history of the Inca in his 1609 book, Royal Commentaries of the Incas.

Garcilaso wrote that 20,000 men worked to move one single large stone. They dragged it over rollers and ramps of earth. Their ropes were made of plant fiber and leather. His number may be too big. But the general method fits what we know about how the Inca organized work.

The Inca Empire had a work system called the mita. Under the mita, every person the empire ruled had to work for the state for part of each year. It was a kind of tax, paid in work and not in money.

The builders may have used other methods too. They may have built ramps of earth up to the top of the wall as it grew. They may have put log rollers under the stones and used wooden beams as levers. They may also have cut smooth, wet channels into the ground. A heavy stone slides more easily along a slippery channel.

In the 1980s and 1990s, a professor named Jean-Pierre Protzen studied Inca stonework. He taught architecture, the art of designing buildings, at the University of California, Berkeley. His experiments were the first of their kind. They showed how the Inca could have made joints that looked impossible.

The method was to shape a stone, test it, and shape it again. Workers put the stone roughly in its place. They marked the points where it touched the stones next to it. Then they took it away. They pounded down the high spots with hammer stones, which are harder rocks used as tools. Then they put the stone back to check the fit.

They did this again and again, sometimes dozens of times, until the stone sat perfectly. Protzen’s work showed that the “impossible” joints did not need any unknown tools. They needed patience, skill, and a huge amount of human work.

The walls are also very good at staying up. In the past five centuries, several terrible earthquakes have hit the Cusco region. Big ones came in 1650, 1950, and 1986. Each time, the Spanish buildings in Cusco were badly damaged, or they fell down completely. Those buildings were made in the usual way, with mortar and brick or stone. The Inca walls at Sacsayhuaman were left almost untouched.

The reason is the way the Inca built. The stones fit exactly, and only their own weight holds them in place. So the walls can bend and move a little during an earthquake. They take in the force and spread it out, and they do not crack. The odd shapes of the stones help too. Joints that lock together resist a push from the side far better than straight joints filled with mortar.

Sacsayhuaman also has a place in the story of the Spanish conquest of Peru. The Spanish took Cusco, and three years later, in 1536, the Inca emperor Manco Inca Yupanqui rose up against them. He surrounded Cusco with a huge army. It is thought to have had tens of thousands of fighters.

The Inca used the fortress as their base, and the Battle of Sacsayhuaman became a key fight. The Spanish were led by the brothers of Francisco Pizarro. They made a desperate attack on the fortress. During the battle, Juan Pizarro was killed as he led a charge at the walls. He was one of the most important Spaniards to die in the whole conquest.

After a fierce fight, the Spanish took Sacsayhuaman, but they paid a high price. Afterward, they pulled much of the fortress apart, piece by piece. They used its stones to build their own new Cusco. They took down the upper parts of the walls, which were made of smaller stones that were easier to carry.

The giant blocks at the bottom were too large to move. That is the only reason these walls still stand today.

What was Sacsayhuaman really for? Was it a fortress, a place for ceremonies, or both? Nobody has fully settled the question.

Some experts point to the shape of Cusco. Seen from above, the city seems to have the form of a puma, a mountain lion. Sacsayhuaman is the head of the animal. Some researchers believe that the zigzag walls are the puma’s teeth.

The puma was a holy animal to the Inca. It stood for power and for the world of the earth. If this idea is right, Sacsayhuaman was more than an army base. It was a picture of how the Inca saw the world, built into the land of their capital.

Sound also carries very well at the site. The stepped walls work like a natural open-air theater. It could have been used for ceremonies and large public meetings.

The site is still used today. Each year on June 24, it holds Inti Raymi, the Festival of the Sun. About 50,000 people come to watch. Actors perform the Inca sun festival again. In the time of the empire, it was the most important ceremony of the year.

Sacsayhuaman is one of the grandest and most puzzling ancient sites in the world. It shows the building skill of the Inca, their gift for organizing people, and their eye for beauty. In the five centuries since the walls went up, nobody has done better work with 200-ton stones and no mortar.

Modern research, most of all the work of Jean-Pierre Protzen, has shown that the Inca had the means to make these joints. But much about the project is still hard to picture. Think of the number of workers. Think of the giant stones dragged across mountains in thin air. Think of the planning needed to run such a job.

The stones of Sacsayhuaman have lived through earthquakes, wars, and conquest. They even outlasted the Spanish, who tore down so much of the site. They will probably still stand when the buildings of our own age have turned to dust. And we may never know exactly how each giant stone made its journey to the hill.
