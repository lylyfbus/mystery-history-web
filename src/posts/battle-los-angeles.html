The Battle of Los Angeles: Did UFOs Attack in 1942?

Searchlights converging on an unidentified object over Los Angeles during the infamous 1942 anti-aircraft barrage

It was 2:25 in the morning on February 25, 1942. Air raid sirens began to wail across the city of Los Angeles, California. More than a million people woke up in fear. Within minutes, a total blackout was ordered. Street lights went out. Homes went dark. The huge city vanished into the night.

Then the searchlights came on. They were massive, bright white beams that swept the sky from guns all over the city. They came together on something in the air above Culver City and Santa Monica.

At 3:16 AM, the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade opened fire. For nearly one hour, the night sky over Los Angeles roared with anti-aircraft guns. Shells flashed as they burst. Searchlights followed something that thousands of people on the ground could see, but nobody could name. Over 1,400 rounds of 37mm and 50-caliber anti-aircraft ammunition were fired into the sky.

The firing stopped at 4:14 AM. The “all clear” sounded at 7:21 AM. People came out to find that no enemy aircraft had been shot down. There were no bomb craters. There were no foreign planes. But five civilians were dead. Falling pieces of metal from the shells had caused a lot of damage. The Battle of Los Angeles is still one of the strangest military events in American history.

It happened less than three months after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. That attack had pulled the United States into World War II. It had sent a wave of fear along the whole West Coast. A Japanese air raid on an American city on the mainland felt very possible. Los Angeles had aircraft factories, shipyards, and military bases. People thought it was a prime target.

One more event made the fear worse, and it had happened only a day before. On February 23, 1942, the Japanese submarine I-17 rose to the surface off the coast near Santa Barbara. It fired about 25 shells at storage tanks and piers at the Ellwood Oil Field. It was the first enemy attack on the mainland United States since the War of 1812.

The damage was small. But the shock was huge. If a Japanese submarine could surface off California and shell an oil field, could Japanese aircraft carriers be hiding just over the horizon? Could they be ready to launch a terrible air raid on Los Angeles?

The Battle of Los Angeles began with a radar contact. In the early evening of February 24, 1942, military radar operators along the California coast spotted an unknown contact. It was about 120 miles west of Los Angeles. They tracked it as it moved toward land. When it was about three miles off the coast, the military went on high alert.

The Regional Controller of the Army’s Western Defense Command ordered a full air raid alert. At 2:25 AM on February 25, the sirens began to sound. The city’s air raid wardens hurried to their posts. The blackout was complete, and every light in the city was ordered out. A city that had been blazing with light a moment before fell into total darkness. Only the sweeping beams of the military searchlights lit the sky.

What happened next depends on which witnesses you believe. There were thousands of them. The searchlights swept the sky and came together on something. People have argued about what it was ever since.

Some witnesses described a large, slow-moving object. They said it was the size of “several city blocks.” It was a huge, dark shape drifting at about 12,000 to 20,000 feet. The anti-aircraft shells bursting around it seemed to have no effect.

Others saw several smaller objects. They darted between the searchlight beams at high speed and changed direction suddenly. Still others described one silvery or glowing object. It seemed to hover, or to move very slowly from northwest to southeast across the city.

The most famous picture of the event is a photograph printed by the Los Angeles Times on February 26, 1942. It shows searchlights meeting on a bright spot in the sky, with shell bursts all around. UFO researchers say that the bright spot shows a solid object with a structure. Skeptics say that it is only the searchlights meeting in empty sky. They say any solid shape comes from the photo process or from wartime photo editing. The picture has been studied, argued over, and sharpened countless times since.

At 3:16 AM, the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade got permission to fire. What followed was a full-scale barrage against an unknown target over a major American city. It was one of the most remarkable military actions in American history.

The brigade’s 37mm anti-aircraft guns could fire shells up to about 20,000 feet. They began firing round after round into the sky. Machine guns of 50 caliber joined them from positions around the city. The noise was deafening. It shook buildings and rattled windows for miles.

People came out of their homes in bathrobes and pajamas. They stood in their yards and on their roofs and stared at the sky. It looked like a strange fireworks show. There were the bright white beams of the searchlights. There were the orange and yellow flashes of exploding shells. And there was the strange glow of whatever the guns were shooting at.

The firing lasted about an hour, from 3:16 AM to about 4:14 AM. The military fired an estimated 1,400 rounds. That was a huge amount of fire, and the results were nothing. The target was lit by searchlights and clear to thousands of witnesses. Yet nothing was shot down. No wreckage fell from the sky. No aircraft was seen to crash or be damaged.

Some witnesses said the shells hit the object but bounced off, or burst harmlessly around it. They said it seemed to take no harm from normal weapons. Others said the object changed speed and direction when the guns fired. It was almost as if it was playing with the gunners.

When the firing stopped, the searchlights kept sweeping the sky for another three hours. No more targets were fired on. The “all clear” sounded at 7:21 AM. The people of Los Angeles came out into daylight. They found their city littered with pieces of metal from the shells. These had fallen back to earth. They damaged buildings, broke windows, made holes in roofs, and scattered fragments over cars and streets.

All the harm was caused by the military’s own actions. Five civilians died. Three died in traffic accidents during the chaotic blackout, when drivers raced through dark crossings. Two died of heart attacks brought on by fear of the bombardment.

The property damage was wide. Reports told of broken windows, holed roofs, and dented cars from one end of Los Angeles to the other. The emotional damage was harder to measure, but it was just as real. The people had lived through a terrifying hour of fire from their own army. And the explanation for what had happened was not comforting.

Suppose the military had fired 1,400 shells at something that was not there. That would show terrible mistakes. Suppose they had fired 1,400 shells at something that was there. Suppose it took 1,400 shells and was not affected. That was far more frightening than any Japanese air raid.

The aftermath was a political storm. The military, the Navy, and the government gave different and sometimes opposite explanations. Secretary of the Navy Frank Knox held a press conference on February 25, 1942, just hours after the event. He said the whole thing was a false alarm. There had been no enemy aircraft over Los Angeles, Knox said. The firing was set off by “jittery nerves” and possibly by commercial aircraft in the area. The Navy’s position was clear. Nothing had happened, and the military had overreacted.

Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson gave a different explanation. He told the press that between one and five aircraft had flown over Los Angeles that night. They might have been commercial planes in the area, or perhaps enemy scout planes. His words clashed with Knox’s false alarm story. They suggested that something was in the sky, though he could not say for certain what.

The Army’s Western Defense Command offered yet another explanation. The objects were weather balloons, it said. One or more balloons had been let go from a local weather station. They drifted over the city, where nervous watchers took them for enemy planes.

The weather balloon story sounds familiar to anyone who has read about the later Roswell incident of 1947. There the military also named a weather balloon to explain a strange object in the sky.

The Battle of Los Angeles is one of the founding events in UFO research. It came before the modern UFO age, which usually starts with Kenneth Arnold’s “flying saucers” sighting in June 1947. The Battle of Los Angeles came five years before that, and five months before the Roswell incident.

UFO researchers say it was one of the first recorded cases of thousands of witnesses seeing an unknown object in the sky. Military radar backed it up. A major newspaper photographed it. And the official response shows that the object was physically real. They say it was not just a wrong guess or a hallucination.

They also say that the military fired 1,400 shells at the object and missed. This suggests it was not a normal aircraft. A normal plane would have been fairly easy to hit with that much fire at the reported height.

Edward Ruppelt headed Project Blue Book. This was the Air Force’s official investigation of UFO sightings from 1952 to 1969. In his 1956 book The Report on Unidentified Flying Objects, he mentioned the Battle of Los Angeles. He called it one of the earliest major UFO events in the military’s records.

In 1983, the Office of Air Force History wrote a report on the event. It said the cause was most likely a mix of jittery nerves, weather balloons, and possibly a lost aircraft. This answer satisfied no one.

UFO researchers point to what witnesses described. They saw a large, slow-moving object that seemed to be hit by shells without being affected. The researchers say this proves it was not a balloon or a normal aircraft. Skeptics reply that the witnesses were frightened. The conditions were poor. The object was almost surely a mix of weather balloons, mass hysteria, and trigger-happy gunners.

The Japanese military denied having any aircraft over Los Angeles on the night of February 24-25, 1942. No Japanese records have ever been found to say otherwise.

The most likely normal explanation is still a mix of weather balloons, nervous radar operators, and anti-aircraft crews on edge. They were ready for an attack after the Ellwood shelling the day before.

But some things keep the UFO idea alive. Witnesses described a large, slow object that seemed to soak up shell fire. The military could not shoot down any target with 1,400 rounds. And the Los Angeles Times photograph is still a mystery.

Was the object over Los Angeles a weather balloon, a lost aircraft, a Japanese scout plane, or something far stranger? We still do not know. In the fog of war, and in the dark of the night sky, not everything can be explained.
