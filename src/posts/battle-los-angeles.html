The Battle of Los Angeles: Did UFOs Attack in 1942?

Searchlights converging on an unidentified object over Los Angeles during the infamous 1942 anti-aircraft barrage

It was 2:25 in the morning on February 25, 1942. Air raid sirens began to wail across the city of Los Angeles, California. Many thousands of people woke up in fear. Within minutes, a total blackout was ordered. Street lights went out. Homes went dark. The huge city vanished into the night.

Then the searchlights came on. They were massive, bright white beams that swept the sky from guns all over the city. They came together on something in the air over the city.

At 3:16 AM, the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade opened fire. For nearly one hour, the night sky over Los Angeles roared with anti-aircraft guns. Shells flashed as they burst. Searchlights followed something that thousands of people on the ground could see, but nobody could name. About 1,400 rounds of anti-aircraft ammunition were fired into the sky.

The firing stopped at 4:14 AM. The “all clear” sounded at 7:21 AM. People came out to find that no enemy aircraft had been shot down. There were no bomb craters. There were no foreign planes. But five civilians were dead. Falling pieces of metal from the shells had caused a lot of damage. The Battle of Los Angeles is still a strange event in American history.

It happened less than three months after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. That attack had pulled the United States into World War II. It had sent a wave of fear along the whole West Coast. A Japanese air raid on an American city on the mainland felt very possible. Los Angeles had aircraft factories, shipyards, and military bases. People thought it was a prime target.

One more event made the fear worse, and it had happened only two days before. On February 23, 1942, the Japanese submarine I-17 rose to the surface off the coast near Santa Barbara. It fired between 12 and 25 shells at the Ellwood Oil Field. It was the first shelling of the United States by a foreign power since the War of 1812.

The damage was small. But the shock was huge. If a Japanese submarine could surface off California and shell an oil field, could Japanese aircraft carriers be hiding just over the horizon? Could they be ready to launch a terrible air raid on Los Angeles?

The Battle of Los Angeles began with a warning. On February 24, 1942, Navy intelligence warned that an attack on California could come soon. Early on February 25, military radar picked up a possible enemy contact about 120 miles west of Los Angeles. At 2:25 AM, the sirens began to sound. The city’s air raid wardens hurried to their posts. The blackout was complete, and every light in the city was ordered out. A city that had been blazing with light a moment before fell into total darkness. Only the sweeping beams of the military searchlights lit the sky.

What happened next depends on which witnesses you believe. There were thousands of them. The searchlights swept the sky and came together on something. People have argued about what it was ever since.

Witnesses gave very different reports. Some described a large, slow-moving object. They said the anti-aircraft shells bursting around it seemed to have no effect.

Others saw several smaller objects that darted between the searchlight beams. Reported heights ranged from a few thousand feet to more than 20,000 feet. Reported speeds ranged from very slow to over 200 miles per hour.

The most famous picture of the event is a photograph printed by the Los Angeles Times on February 26, 1942. It shows searchlights meeting on a bright spot in the sky, with shell bursts all around. UFO researchers say that the bright spot shows a solid object with a structure. Skeptics say that it is only the searchlights meeting in empty sky. They say any solid shape comes from the photo process or from wartime photo editing. The picture has been studied, argued over, and sharpened countless times since.

At 3:16 AM, the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade got permission to fire. What followed was a full-scale barrage against an unknown target over a major American city.

The brigade began firing 12.8-pound shells from its anti-aircraft guns. Machine guns of 50 caliber joined them from positions around the city. The noise was deafening. It shook buildings and rattled windows for miles.

People came out of their homes in bathrobes and pajamas. They stood in their yards and on their roofs and stared at the sky. It looked like a strange fireworks show. There were the bright white beams of the searchlights. There were the orange and yellow flashes of exploding shells. And there was the strange glow of whatever the guns were shooting at.

The firing went on, off and on, from 3:16 AM to about 4:14 AM. The Office of Air Force History counts about 1,440 rounds. That was a huge amount of fire, and the results were nothing. Many witnesses said the target was lit by searchlights. Yet nothing was shot down. No wreckage fell from the sky. No aircraft was seen to crash or be damaged.

Some witnesses said the shells hit the object but bounced off, or burst harmlessly around it. They said it seemed to take no harm from normal weapons. Others said the object changed speed and direction when the guns fired. It was almost as if it was playing with the gunners.

When the firing stopped, no more targets were fired on. The “all clear” sounded at 7:21 AM. The people of Los Angeles came out into daylight. They found pieces of metal from the shells that had fallen back to earth. They had broken windows and damaged buildings.

No enemy bomb fell. Five civilians died. Three died in car crashes during the blackout. Two died of heart attacks.

There was damage to property too. Reports told of broken windows and damaged buildings in several parts of the city. The emotional damage was harder to measure, but it was just as real. The people had lived through a terrifying hour of fire from their own army. And the explanation for what had happened was not comforting.

Suppose the military had fired about 1,400 shells at something that was not there. That would show terrible mistakes. Suppose they had fired at something that was there, and it was not hurt. Many people found that more frightening than any Japanese air raid.

The aftermath was a political storm. The military, the Navy, and the government gave different and sometimes opposite explanations. Secretary of the Navy Frank Knox held a press conference on February 25, 1942, just hours after the event. He said the whole thing was a false alarm. He blamed “jittery nerves.” The Navy’s position was clear. Nothing had happened, and the military had overreacted.

Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson gave a different explanation. He first told the press that as many as 15 aircraft had flown over Los Angeles that night. They might have been commercial planes flown by enemy agents. He later took back some of these claims. His words clashed with Knox’s false alarm story. They suggested that something was in the sky, though he could not say for certain what.

A later Army account offered yet another explanation. It said a weather balloon sent up around 1:00 AM had started all the shooting. Nervous watchers took it for an enemy plane.

The weather balloon story sounds familiar to anyone who has read about the later Roswell incident of 1947. There the military also named a weather balloon to explain a strange object in the sky.

Some UFO writers call the Battle of Los Angeles an early event in UFO history. It came before the modern UFO age, which usually starts with Kenneth Arnold’s “flying saucers” sighting in June 1947. The Battle of Los Angeles came about five years before that. The Roswell incident also came in 1947.

UFO researchers say it was one of the first recorded cases of thousands of witnesses seeing an unknown object in the sky. Military radar backed it up. A major newspaper photographed it. They say the confused official response shows that the object was physically real, and not just a wrong guess.

They also say that the military fired about 1,400 shells at the object and missed. This suggests it was not a normal aircraft. A normal plane would have been fairly easy to hit with that much fire at the reported height.

In 1983, the Office of Air Force History wrote a report on the event. It said the cause was “war nerves.” These were set off by a lost weather balloon and made worse by stray flares and shell bursts. Not everyone accepts this answer.

UFO researchers point to what witnesses described. They saw a large, slow-moving object that seemed to be hit by shells without being affected. The researchers say this shows it was not a balloon or a normal aircraft. Skeptics reply that the witnesses were frightened and the conditions were poor. They think it was a weather balloon, fear, and nervous gunners.

After the war, the Japanese government said it had flown no aircraft over Los Angeles.

The most likely normal explanation is still a weather balloon and gun crews on edge. They were ready for an attack after the Ellwood shelling two days before.

But some things keep the UFO idea alive. Witnesses described a large, slow object that seemed to soak up shell fire. The military could not shoot down any target with about 1,400 rounds. And the Los Angeles Times photograph is still a mystery.

Was the object over Los Angeles a weather balloon, a lost aircraft, a Japanese scout plane, or something far stranger? We still do not know. In the fog of war, and in the dark of the night sky, not everything can be explained.
