The Battle of Los Angeles: Did UFOs Attack in 1942?

Searchlights converging on an unidentified object over Los Angeles during the infamous 1942 anti-aircraft barrage

Sirens in the night

Imagine that you are asleep in Los Angeles, California. It is 2:25 in the morning on February 25, 1942. Suddenly air raid sirens begin to wail across the city. Many thousands of people wake up in fear.

Within minutes, a total blackout is ordered. Street lights go out. Homes go dark. The huge city vanishes into the night.

Then the searchlights come on. They are bright white beams that sweep the sky from guns all over the city. They come together on something in the air above Los Angeles. What was it? People still argue about the answer. To understand why the city was so afraid, we must go back a few months.

A city that expected an attack

On December 7, 1941, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. That attack pulled the United States into World War II. It sent a wave of fear along the whole West Coast. Los Angeles had aircraft factories, shipyards, and military bases. People thought it was a prime target.

Then came an event that made the fear worse, only two days before the sirens. On February 23, 1942, the Japanese submarine I-17 rose to the surface off the coast near Santa Barbara. It fired between 12 and 25 shells at the Ellwood Oil Field. It was the first shelling of the United States by a foreign power since the War of 1812.

The damage was small. But the shock was huge. If a submarine could shell an oil field, could aircraft carriers be hiding just over the horizon?

On February 24, Navy intelligence warned that an attack on California could come soon. Early on February 25, military radar picked up a possible enemy contact about 120 miles west of Los Angeles. Then the sirens began. The air raid wardens hurried to their posts, and the city went dark.

One hour of fire

At 3:16 AM, the 37th Coast Artillery Brigade got permission to fire. It began shooting 12.8-pound shells from its anti-aircraft guns. Machine guns of 50 caliber joined in from around the city. The noise shook buildings and rattled windows for miles.

People came out of their homes in bathrobes and pajamas. They stood in their yards and on their roofs and stared at the sky. There were the white beams of the searchlights. There were the orange and yellow flashes of bursting shells. And there was a strange glow on whatever the guns were shooting at.

The firing went on, off and on, until about 4:14 AM. The Office of Air Force History counts about 1,440 rounds. Many witnesses said the target was lit by searchlights. Yet nothing was shot down. No wreckage fell. No aircraft was seen to crash or be damaged.

Some witnesses said the shells hit the object but bounced off, or burst harmlessly around it. Others said it changed speed and direction when the guns fired. So what did the people on the ground really see?

What the witnesses said

There were thousands of witnesses, and their reports were very different. Some described a large, slow-moving object. Others saw several smaller objects that darted between the searchlight beams.

Reported heights ranged from a few thousand feet to more than 20,000 feet. Reported speeds ranged from very slow to over 200 miles per hour. That is a wide range for one object in one sky.

The most famous picture of the event was printed by the Los Angeles Times on February 26, 1942. It shows searchlights meeting on a bright spot in the sky, with shell bursts all around. UFO researchers say that the bright spot shows a solid object with a structure. Skeptics say it is only the searchlights meeting in empty sky. They say any solid shape comes from the photo process or from wartime photo editing.

The picture has been studied, argued over, and sharpened countless times since. It settles nothing. Next, let us see what happened when the sun came up.

A morning with no enemy

The “all clear” sounded at 7:21 AM. People came out into daylight. No enemy bomb had fallen. No enemy plane was found and there were no craters.

But the night had not been harmless. Pieces of metal from the shells had fallen back to earth. They broke windows and damaged buildings in several parts of the city. Five civilians died. Three died in car crashes during the blackout. Two died of heart attacks.

The people of Los Angeles had lived through a terrifying hour of fire from their own army. And the answer to what had happened was not comforting. Suppose the military had fired about 1,400 shells at something that was not there. That would show terrible mistakes. Suppose they had fired at something that was there, and it was not hurt. Many people found that even more frightening.

Three leaders, three answers

Leaders gave different explanations, and some of them clashed. Secretary of the Navy Frank Knox held a press conference on February 25, just hours after the event. He said the whole thing was a false alarm. He blamed “jittery nerves.”

Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson told the press something else. He first said that as many as 15 aircraft had flown over Los Angeles that night. They might have been commercial planes flown by enemy agents. He later took back some of these claims. His words suggested that something was in the sky, though he could not say for certain what.

A later Army account gave a third answer. It said a weather balloon sent up around 1:00 AM had started all the shooting. Nervous watchers took it for an enemy plane.

This story sounds familiar to anyone who has read about the Roswell incident of 1947. There, too, the military named a weather balloon to explain a strange object in the sky.

The case for something strange

Some UFO writers call the Battle of Los Angeles an early event in UFO history. It came about five years before the sighting by Kenneth Arnold in June 1947, which usually marks the start of the modern UFO age.

UFO researchers say it was one of the first recorded cases of thousands of witnesses seeing an unknown object in the sky. Military radar backed it up. A major newspaper photographed it. They say the confused official response shows that the object was real, and not just a wrong guess.

They also say that the gunners fired about 1,400 shells at the object and missed. A normal plane would have been fairly easy to hit with that much fire at the reported height. And they point to the witnesses who saw a large, slow object that seemed to take no harm from the shells.

Skeptics answer that the witnesses were frightened and the conditions were poor. They think the cause was a weather balloon, fear, and nervous gunners.

What we know, and what we do not

In 1983, the Office of Air Force History wrote a report on the event. It said the cause was “war nerves.” These were set off by a lost weather balloon and made worse by stray flares and shell bursts. Not everyone accepts this answer. After the war, the Japanese government said it had flown no aircraft over Los Angeles.

The most likely normal explanation is still a weather balloon and gun crews on edge. They were ready for an attack after the Ellwood shelling two days before.

But some things keep the other idea alive. The military could not bring down any target with about 1,400 rounds. And the Los Angeles Times photograph is still argued over.

Go back to that sleeper in the dark city at 2:25 in the morning. The sirens were real. The fear was real. The five deaths were real. Was the object a weather balloon, a lost aircraft, a Japanese scout plane, or something far stranger? We still do not know.
