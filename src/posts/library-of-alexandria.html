The Library of Alexandria: The Greatest Collection of Knowledge the Ancient World Ever Knew

A ship sails into the harbor of Alexandria, on the coast of Egypt. It is the third century BCE. Before the sailors can unload, the king’s officers come on board. They are not looking for gold or weapons. They are looking for books.

According to the doctor Galen, any books they found were taken to the library and copied by hand. Trained writers called scribes did the work. The library kept the originals, and the owners got the copies. This was the Library of Alexandria. A family of Greek pharaohs built it.

It was more than a library. It was a bold attempt to collect the written knowledge of the known world. The king’s agents traveled across the Mediterranean. They bought texts, and they were told to favor older copies.

Nobody knows how big the library was. Modern guesses range from about 40,000 to 700,000 scrolls, and some scholars say about 100,000 books. It was joined to the Mouseion, a place of research named for the Muses, the Greek goddesses of art and learning. The government paid for the scholars there.

Eratosthenes worked in Alexandria, and he was the chief librarian. He worked out the distance around the Earth with a stick, a shadow, and geometry. His answer was remarkably good. Callimachus made a catalogue of the library in 120 books. It is one of the earliest known library catalogues.

Then, over several centuries, the library faded away. Many people call its loss a great disaster for human knowledge. Nobody can say how much was lost, because we do not know what it held.

The story of the library is not only a sad one. It is also a mirror. We like to believe that knowledge lasts forever. The Library of Alexandria shows that this is not always true.

Knowledge needs institutions, such as libraries and schools, to keep it safe. Institutions need money and leaders who care. When the institutions fall, the knowledge can fall with them.

Alexandria lost more than a pile of papyrus scrolls. Much of the writing of the ancient world was lost over time. Some of it never came back at all.

The idea of the library was born after Alexander the Great died in 323 BCE. His generals cut his empire into pieces. One of his most trusted commanders was Ptolemy I Soter. He seized Egypt and founded a line of kings, the Ptolemaic dynasty.

Ptolemy chose Alexandria as his capital. Alexander had founded the city on the Mediterranean coast. Ptolemy set out to make it a center of learning.

Some stories say that the idea of a library of everything came from Demetrius of Phalerum. He was a statesman and philosopher from Athens who found safety at Ptolemy’s court. But the story comes from a late and unreliable text, so we cannot be sure.

Ptolemy I may have planned the library. But it probably became a real institution under his son, Ptolemy II Philadelphus. He ruled from 283 to 246 BCE.

The name Mouseion means “Temple of the Muses.” The library was part of it. It stood in the Brucheion, the royal part of the city. Scholars received free food and lodging, a salary, and freedom from taxes.

The Mouseion also had reading rooms and gardens. Scholars came from all over the Greek-speaking world. In many ways it was like a modern university. Modern scholars know frustratingly little about it.

The place was alive with ideas. Poets, scholars, and scientists worked side by side. Among the famous names were Zenodotus, Callimachus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Eratosthenes, and Aristophanes of Byzantium.

Eratosthenes lived from about 276 to 194 BCE. At noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, now Aswan. In Alexandria, a rod still cast a shadow. He measured its angle and used the distance between the two cities.

From this he worked out the size of the Earth. His answer was within about one percent of the true size, by one way of counting. But nobody knows exactly how long his unit of length, the stade, was. The modern figure is about 40,075 kilometers around the equator.

Callimachus wrote the Pinakes, a catalogue of authors and their works in 120 books.

Hero of Alexandria lived much later, in Roman times. He is known for a description of an early steam engine. He shows that Alexandria stayed a place of learning long after the first Ptolemies.

The Ptolemies were bold collectors. Royal agents bought texts aggressively, and they looked for the oldest copies. Books that arrived on ships were taken to the library and copied.

One story is about three great Athenian writers of tragedies: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Athens kept official copies of their plays. Ptolemy III is said to have borrowed these copies and left a deposit of fifteen talents of gold. Then he kept the originals and sent back fine copies.

Ancient writers gave very large numbers for the scrolls, but modern scholars argue about them. One scroll usually held one work, or part of a longer work. So the number of separate texts was probably much smaller than the number of scrolls.

We know little about what the scrolls said. Many ancient books are lost, and we know some of them only because later writers quoted them. The same is probably true of the library.

In time the main library may have grown too big for the Mouseion building. In the reign of Ptolemy III Euergetes, from 246 to 221 BCE, a “Daughter Library” was set up in the Serapeum. This was the temple of the god Serapis, in Alexandria. Serapis was a god who mixed Greek and Egyptian beliefs.

Scholars agree that the Serapeum held a daughter library. We do not know what was in it. It was probably not touched by the fire of 48 BCE.

Many people believe that one great fire destroyed the Library of Alexandria. Most modern scholars do not think so. The library seems to have faded over centuries. Classical historian Roger Bagnall called its fate a murder mystery with many suspects.

The first suspect is Julius Caesar’s fire in 48 BCE. During a war, Roman forces set fire to ships in the harbor. The fire spread to buildings near the docks. The Roman writer Seneca, quoting Livy, said that 40,000 scrolls burned.

The writer Plutarch said that the whole library burned. But he wrote at least a century later. Another writer, Cassius Dio, seems to speak of warehouses. Some scholars think only papyri waiting in warehouses were lost. Later writers still speak of scholars working in Alexandria.

In 145 BCE, King Ptolemy VIII sent foreign scholars out of Alexandria. They scattered across the Mediterranean. Some historians see this as the start of a long decline. Later kings gave the post of head librarian to their own supporters.

In 272 CE, the Roman emperor Aurelian fought the rebels of Palmyra in Alexandria. His soldiers destroyed the Brucheion, the royal quarter. One scholar thinks this is the most likely time for a big loss of library buildings. No writer of that time mentions the library, so little may have been left.

In 391 CE, Christian crowds tore down the Serapeum. Bishop Theophilus of Alexandria oversaw the destruction. Some writers later called this the end of the library. But it is not clear that a library still stood there.

Last came the Arab conquest of Alexandria in 642 CE. A story that appears about six centuries later says that Caliph Omar ordered the books burned to heat the city’s baths. Scholars widely think that this story is fiction.

Experts still argue about which events mattered most. Many think that neglect and slow decay did as much harm as any single act of destruction.

Hypatia of Alexandria lived long after the library’s best years. She was born between about 350 and 370 CE and died in 415. She was a mathematician, an astronomer, and a philosopher of the school called Neoplatonism.

Her father, Theon, led a school that took the name Mouseion, in memory of the old one. The old Mouseion had stopped working as a group of scholars in the 260s CE. Hypatia taught philosophy and astronomy to students from across the Mediterranean.

She wrote a commentary on the Arithmetica of Diophantus, a Greek mathematician. She also wrote about the conic sections of Apollonius of Perga, but that work is lost. She built astrolabes, which measure the positions of stars. She did not invent them, because they were used long before she was born.

She was also a close friend of Orestes, the Roman prefect, or governor, of Alexandria. So she was a figure in politics as well as in learning.

In March 415 CE, Alexandria was torn by violence between religious groups. A mob led by a man named Peter pulled Hypatia from her carriage. They took her to a church, a former temple, and killed her there. Later accounts say that they burned her body.

People have sometimes linked her death to the end of the Library of Alexandria. But the library no longer existed in her lifetime. Her death does stand as a sad moment in the history of Alexandria.

Nobody can measure what was lost with the Library of Alexandria. But we know that many works from the ancient world are gone. Some we know only from the names and quotations in other books.

For many ancient books, we know only that they existed. Whole fields of ancient writing were lost over the centuries. Fire was only one of the causes.

Some people have wondered whether the loss set science back by a long time. If the library had survived, they say, modern science might have begun sooner. This is only a guess.

The Library of Alexandria was probably not destroyed by one cruel act. Neglect, war, and unsteady government all played a part. Kings, emperors, and officials in turn let it decline.

The Ptolemies built it, and later Ptolemies handed its leadership to supporters. Roman rule brought less interest. Wars and fires damaged the city. The Arab conquest came last, and by then the library had probably been gone for a long time.

So the library did not die in one great blaze. It faded over centuries.

And yet the Library of Alexandria is still one of the strongest symbols in human history. It shows what we can do when we give ourselves to the search for knowledge. It shows what we lose when we stop caring.

In 2002, a large modern library opened in Alexandria. It is called the Bibliotheca Alexandrina. It honors what was lost.

But the scrolls that filled the first library are gone for good. Knowledge is precious, and it can be easy to break. It can be built over centuries and lost in a short time. And one question stays open. What was written on all those lost scrolls?
