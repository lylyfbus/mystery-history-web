The Library of Alexandria: The Most Famous Library of the Ancient World

The ship and the book hunters

A ship sails into the harbor of Alexandria, on the coast of Egypt. It is the third century BCE. Before the sailors can unload, the king’s officers come on board. They are not looking for gold or weapons. They are looking for books.

According to the doctor Galen, any books that they found were taken to the library and copied by hand. Trained writers called scribes did the work. The library kept the originals, and the owners got the copies.

This was the Library of Alexandria. It was more than a place to store scrolls. It was a bold attempt to collect the written knowledge of the known world. The king’s agents traveled across the Mediterranean. They bought texts, and they were told to favor older copies.

Today the library is gone, and many people call its loss a great disaster for human knowledge. But how did it end? To answer that, we must first see how it began.

A king, a city, and a library

The idea of the library was born after Alexander the Great died in 323 BCE. His generals cut his empire into pieces. One of his most trusted commanders was Ptolemy I Soter. He seized Egypt and founded a line of kings, the Ptolemaic dynasty.

Ptolemy chose Alexandria as his capital. Alexander had founded the city on the Mediterranean coast. Ptolemy set out to make it a center of learning.

Some stories say that the idea of a library of everything came from Demetrius of Phalerum. He was a statesman and philosopher from Athens who found safety at Ptolemy’s court. But the story comes from a late and unreliable text, so we cannot be sure. Ptolemy I may have planned the library. But it probably became a real institution under his son, Ptolemy II Philadelphus, who ruled from 283 to 246 BCE.

The library was part of the Mouseion, a name that means “Temple of the Muses.” The Muses were the Greek goddesses of art and learning. It stood in the Brucheion, the royal part of the city. Scholars there received free food and lodging, a salary, and freedom from taxes. The Mouseion also had reading rooms and gardens. In many ways it was like a modern university. Modern scholars know frustratingly little about it.

Scholars and a shadow

The place was alive with ideas. Poets, scholars, and scientists worked side by side. Among the famous names were Zenodotus, Callimachus, Apollonius of Rhodes, and Aristophanes of Byzantium.

One of them was Eratosthenes, who lived from about 276 to 194 BCE and became the chief librarian. At noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene, now Aswan. In Alexandria, a rod still cast a shadow. He measured the angle of that shadow and used the distance between the two cities.

From this he worked out the size of the Earth. His answer was within about one percent of the true size, by one way of counting. But nobody knows exactly how long his unit of length, the stade, was.

Callimachus wrote the Pinakes, a catalogue of authors and their works in 120 books. It is one of the earliest known library catalogues.

The kings were bold collectors. One story is about three great Athenian writers of tragedies: Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides. Athens kept official copies of their plays. Ptolemy III is said to have borrowed these copies and left a deposit of fifteen talents of gold. Then he kept the originals and sent back fine copies.

So how many scrolls did the library hold?

How big was it?

Nobody knows. Modern guesses range from about 40,000 to 700,000 scrolls, and some scholars say about 100,000 books. Ancient writers gave very large numbers, but modern scholars argue about them. One scroll usually held one work, or part of a longer work. So the number of separate texts was probably much smaller than the number of scrolls.

We know little about what the scrolls said. Many ancient books are lost, and we know some of them only because later writers quoted them. The same is probably true of this library.

In time, the main library may have grown too big for the Mouseion building. In the reign of Ptolemy III Euergetes, from 246 to 221 BCE, a “Daughter Library” was set up in the Serapeum. This was the temple of the god Serapis, in Alexandria. Scholars agree that it held a daughter library, but we do not know what was in it.

Now the question that everyone asks. Who burned it?

Was it Caesar’s fire?

Many people believe that one great fire destroyed the Library of Alexandria. Most modern scholars do not think so. The classical historian Roger Bagnall called its fate a murder mystery with many suspects. Let us meet them, one by one.

The first suspect is Julius Caesar. In 48 BCE, during a war, Roman forces set fire to ships in the harbor. The fire spread to buildings near the docks. The Roman writer Seneca, quoting Livy, said that 40,000 scrolls burned.

The writer Plutarch said that the whole library burned. But he wrote at least a century later. Another writer, Cassius Dio, seems to speak of warehouses. Some scholars think that only papyri waiting in warehouses were lost. Later writers still speak of scholars working in Alexandria. The Daughter Library was probably not touched by this fire.

So the library lived on. But it was already in trouble.

A slow fading

In 145 BCE, King Ptolemy VIII sent foreign scholars out of Alexandria. They scattered across the Mediterranean. Some historians see this as the start of a long decline. Later kings gave the post of head librarian to their own supporters.

In 272 CE, the Roman emperor Aurelian fought the rebels of Palmyra in Alexandria. His soldiers destroyed the Brucheion, the royal quarter. One scholar thinks that this is the most likely time for a big loss of library buildings. No writer of that time mentions the library, so little may have been left.

In 391 CE, Christian crowds tore down the Serapeum. Bishop Theophilus of Alexandria oversaw the destruction. Some writers later called this the end of the library. But it is not clear that a library still stood there.

Experts still argue about which events mattered most. Many think that neglect and slow decay did as much harm as any single act of destruction. One report even links the slow decline to a lack of funding.

Two more stories are often told, and both are shaky.

Two stories that do not fit

The first is about the Arab conquest of Alexandria in 642 CE. A story that appears about six centuries later says that Caliph Omar ordered the books burned to heat the city’s baths. Scholars widely think that this story is fiction.

The second is about Hypatia of Alexandria. She was born between about 350 and 370 CE and died in 415. She was a mathematician, an astronomer, and a philosopher of the school called Neoplatonism. Her father, Theon, led a school that took the name Mouseion, in memory of the old one. The old Mouseion had stopped working as a group of scholars in the 260s CE.

Hypatia taught philosophy and astronomy to students from across the Mediterranean. She wrote a commentary on the Arithmetica of Diophantus, a Greek mathematician. She built astrolabes, which measure the positions of stars, but she did not invent them. In March 415 CE, Alexandria was torn by violence between religious groups, and a mob led by a man named Peter killed her.

People have sometimes linked her death to the end of the Library of Alexandria. But the library no longer existed in her lifetime. Her death does stand as a sad moment in the history of the city.

What was written on the scrolls?

Nobody can measure what was lost with the Library of Alexandria. But we know that many works from the ancient world are gone. For many ancient books, we know only that they existed. Fire was only one of the causes.

Some people wonder whether the loss set science back by a long time. If the library had survived, they say, modern science might have begun sooner. This is only a guess.

The library was probably not destroyed by one cruel act. Neglect, war, and unsteady government all played a part. The Ptolemies built it, and later Ptolemies handed its leadership to supporters. Wars and fires damaged the city. By the time of the Arab conquest, the library had probably been gone for a long time. So it did not die in one great blaze. It faded over centuries.

In 2002, a large modern library opened in Alexandria. It is called the Bibliotheca Alexandrina. It honors what was lost.

Think again of that ship in the harbor, and the officers who came for the books. The scrolls that they gathered are gone for good. Knowledge can be built over centuries and lost in a short time. And one question stays open. What was written on all those lost scrolls?
