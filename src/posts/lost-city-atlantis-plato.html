The Lost City of Atlantis: Plato's Ancient Warning About Hubris and Cataclysm

Sail west past the Pillars of Hercules. That was the ancient name for the Strait of Gibraltar, where the Mediterranean Sea meets the open Atlantic. There, says the Greek philosopher Plato, an island once stood. Its people had amazing power and beauty.

The island was larger than Libya and Asia combined. It had rings of land and water, one inside the other. Skilled builders had made canals and bridges. A palace stood at its center. So did a temple to Poseidon, covered in silver and gold.

The people were wise, wealthy, and strong. They had a navy and an army of 10,000 chariots, and they ruled the seas. Then, in “a single day and night of misfortune,” earthquakes and floods destroyed the island. It sank beneath the waves forever.

This is the story of Atlantis. Plato told it in two books called dialogues, Timaeus and Critias. He wrote them around 360 BCE. They are the only ancient source for the story. Every version, theory, film, novel, and documentary in the 2,400 years since traces back to these two texts.

The question has haunted the Western world ever since. Was Atlantis real? Or was it one of the most lasting works of fiction ever created?

Atlantis is one of the best-known lost-world mysteries. The Library of Alexandria was real. The Antikythera Mechanism was pulled from the seafloor. But Atlantis has never been found, because it may never have existed.

Yet the search goes on, as steadily as the tides that supposedly swallowed the island. People have “located” Atlantis in the Azores, the Bahamas, Antarctica, the North Sea, and many other places.

It inspired Ignatius Donnelly’s bestseller in the 19th century. It inspired the psychic visions of Edgar Cayce and the Theosophical movement. It inspired countless Hollywood films. It has become a name for any lost, advanced civilization, and for any golden age destroyed by disaster.

Plato could not have known what he was starting when he wrote a story about the fall of a corrupt empire. Or perhaps he could. Perhaps that was exactly the point.

Every detail of the Atlantis story comes from those two texts, written around 360 BCE. In the Timaeus, Plato describes Atlantis as part of a bigger talk about the cosmos and the ideal state. A character named Critias tells a story that he says came down through his family. It came from Solon, a great Athenian lawgiver. Solon heard it from Egyptian priests in the city of Sais, between 590 and 580 BCE.

The priests told Solon that the war with Atlantis took place 9,000 years earlier. That places it around 9600 BCE. They said Athens had beaten the Atlantean empire in war. Then the gods destroyed Atlantis with earthquakes and floods.

The Critias gives far more detail, though it is unfinished. The text breaks off before the end, and Plato apparently never finished it. He describes the geography of the island. There was a large, fertile plain with mountains around it. The capital hill was circled by rings of land and water, joined by bridges and a canal.

At the center stood the palace of the kings. Beside it was a grand temple to Poseidon, covered in silver, with gold and ivory. The Atlanteans had hot and cold springs, plenty of wildlife, and exotic fruits. They had huge mineral wealth, including a metal called orichalcum, which was valued second only to gold.

They had an army with 10,000 chariots and a vast navy. Their empire reached parts of North Africa and Europe. But over generations, the Atlanteans grew corrupt and greedy for power. They launched a war of conquest against Athens and its allies. They were defeated.

The Critias breaks off just as Zeus calls the gods together to speak about the pride of Atlantis. In the Timaeus, earthquakes and floods destroy Atlantis in a single day and night. The island sank into the sea. It left only a shoal of mud that made that sea impossible to sail.

The big debate is whether Plato meant his story as real history or as a lesson. In the Timaeus, Critias says the tale, though strange, is true. But in The Republic, Plato describes an ideal state that is a model, not a real place. Many scholars think Atlantis serves the same purpose. It would be a warning about what happens when a civilization becomes corrupt and greedy for power.

The clue is in the structure. Atlantis is a mirror image of Athens, but spoiled. Athens is virtuous and disciplined. Atlantis is wasteful and aggressive. Athens wins through courage and wisdom. Atlantis falls through pride.

The fall of Atlantis stands for the fall of every empire that thinks it cannot be beaten. That theme is as true today as it was in 360 BCE. Most classical scholars believe Atlantis was fiction, a tool Plato invented to explain his ideas.

But the details seem real. There are the Pillars of Hercules, the size of the island, and the rings. They have convinced generations of seekers that Plato described a real place.

If Atlantis came from a real civilization, the most popular candidate is the Minoan civilization of Bronze Age Crete. In the second millennium BCE, the Minoans were one of the most advanced peoples in Europe. They were a great sea power. They had a magnificent palace at Knossos. They had lively art, with frescoes and pottery, and a writing system called Linear A.

The Minoans led trade in the eastern Mediterranean. The Egyptians and the Mycenaeans knew them well. Then, around 1600 BCE, disaster struck. Experts still argue about the exact date.

The island of Thera, now called Santorini, lies about 70 miles north of Crete. It was the site of one of the largest volcanic eruptions in human history. It blew the island apart.

It left a huge caldera, a collapsed volcanic crater, that you can still see from space today. The eruption made huge tsunamis. They hit the north coast of Crete. Some experts think the eruption helped bring down Minoan civilization. Others say the Minoans recovered quickly, and the question is still argued.

Some people see links to Plato’s Atlantis. An advanced island civilization was hit by a natural disaster. Some compare the Minoan palaces to Plato’s Atlantean buildings. Some say Minoan bull-leaping echoes Plato’s bull rituals. And some say the Theran caldera, seen from above, looks like Plato’s rings of water and land. But most scholars doubt the link. The date does not match Plato’s 9,000 years, and Plato’s island lay beyond the Pillars of Hercules.

There is another possible source that people often overlook. In 373 BCE, in Plato’s own lifetime, a huge earthquake and tsunami destroyed the Greek city of Helike. It stood in Achaea, near the southern shore of the Gulf of Corinth. Ancient writers say the city sank and the sea covered it. In 2001, a team said it had found the site buried in an old lagoon.

The loss of Helike was a major event in the Greek world. Many people discussed and wrote about it. Plato was about 54 years old at the time. Some scholars suggest that Helike helped shape his tale of Atlantis. A whole city was swallowed by an earthquake and a flood, much like Atlantis.

The Thera eruption around 1600 BCE threw a huge amount of material into the air. The latest estimate is about 28 to 41 cubic kilometers of rock. The plume may have risen about 20 miles into the stratosphere, the high layer of the sky. Estimates for the tsunamis vary widely, up to about 490 feet in some places.

Some experts think gases from the eruption cooled the weather for a time. The caldera is now a ring of islands around a bay. It is a dramatic place, and a popular one for tourists.

One geologist has even linked the eruption to the plagues of Egypt in the Book of Exodus. That is only an idea, and not all scholars accept it. Still, it reminds us that some ancient myths may be rooted in real disasters.

For most of Western history, people saw Atlantis as what Plato probably meant it to be, a story with a lesson. In 1882, that changed. A former United States Congressman named Ignatius Donnelly published Atlantis: The Antediluvian World.

Donnelly argued that Atlantis was real. He said it was the common origin of ancient civilizations in Africa, Europe, and the Americas. He said it was destroyed in the flood that the Bible remembers. His book sold well and started the modern Atlantis craze.

Scholars and scientists have rejected his arguments. One later writer said that most of what Donnelly wrote was questionable or wrong. Even so, Donnelly opened the floodgates.

In the 20th century, the American psychic Edgar Cayce (1877–1945) said he had “seen” Atlantis in trances. He described an advanced civilization that sank long before recorded history.

Cayce is said to have predicted that part of Atlantis would rise near Bimini in 1968 or 1969. In 1968, the Bimini Road was found. It is an underwater rock formation in the Bahamas. Believers hailed it as proof of Cayce’s prophecy. Most geologists say it is a natural formation.

The Theosophical movement was founded by Helena Blavatsky. It counted Atlantis as one of several “root races” in its secret teachings. By the late 20th century, Atlantis was a cultural phenomenon. It was a blank canvas for every dream of lost golden ages and hidden knowledge.

Over the centuries, people have “located” Atlantis in dozens of places. Some proposed the Azores, which lie in the mid-Atlantic, beyond the Pillars of Hercules. But the Azores are volcanic islands. They are not the remains of a sunken continent.

Others claimed the Bahamas and the Bimini Road as an Atlantean road. Geologists say it is natural beachrock. In 1974, an Italian writer named Flavio Barbiero said Atlantis was really Antarctica. Scientists do not accept this.

Doggerland was a real landmass that joined Britain to Europe in the last Ice Age. Rising seas covered it thousands of years ago. It has been suggested too, but there is no sign of an advanced civilization there.

Of all the candidates, Santorini and the Minoans are the most popular. Fans say the caldera matches Plato’s rings, the Minoans match his sea-going people, and the sudden destruction matches his earthquakes and floods. But most scholars still call Atlantis a story.

Modern maps of the seafloor, and the science of plate tectonics, have ruled out a lost continent in the Atlantic. Plate tectonics was developed in the 1960s. It explains the Earth’s crust as large plates that move, collide, and separate over geological time. The Atlantic Ocean was formed as the North American, South American, European, and African plates moved apart.

There is no room on any plate for a lost continent as large as Libya and Asia combined. Sonar, satellites, and submersible expeditions have mapped the seafloor widely. No sunken landmass like Plato’s has ever been found.

The floor of the Atlantic is made of fairly young basalt rock. It is not the continental crust we would expect if a large landmass had once been there. Continents do not sink. They are made of light rock that floats on the denser mantle below. A continent the size of Plato’s Atlantis could not have sunk in “a single day and night,” or at any other time.

The geological evidence is clear. Atlantis, as Plato described it, never existed. But that does not mean real events did not inspire his story.

Atlantis is one of the most lasting mysteries in Western culture. It is so not because it is real, but because it is a story people love to tell. Plato understood something deep about people. We are fascinated by golden ages, lost wisdom, and sudden falls.

Every generation sees its own civilization in Atlantis, wealthy, powerful, and clever with machines. And each wonders whether the same fate awaits it. The myth lasts because it is not really about the past. It is about the future. It tells us that civilizations fall.

The Minoans fell. The Romans fell. The Maya fell. One day, perhaps, we will fall too, from climate change, nuclear war, disease, or some disaster we cannot yet imagine. Atlantis is the ghost of every civilization that has ever existed. It is also the shadow of every one that will exist.

Plato, whether he meant to or not, made a myth for the ages. It has survived 2,400 years and shows no sign of fading. The search for Atlantis may never end. Not because there is something to find, but because the search itself is the point.

We look for Atlantis because we see ourselves in it. We see our wealth, our power, and our achievements. And we see how easily they could be lost. Atlantis is not a lost city. It is a mirror. In it we see how fragile everything we have built is. Was there ever a real Atlantis at all?
