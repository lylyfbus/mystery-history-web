The Philadelphia Experiment: Did the Navy Make a Ship Invisible?

It is autumn in 1943, at the Philadelphia Naval Shipyard. A Navy ship, the destroyer escort USS Eldridge (DE-173), sits in the water. According to a famous legend, the date is on or around October 28.

The story says that the Navy switched on powerful generators aboard the ship. A strange green-blue glow wrapped around the hull. Then the ship vanished. It became invisible, not just to radar, but to the naked eye.

The legend goes on. The ship is said to have reappeared at the Norfolk Naval Shipyard in Virginia, about 200 miles away. Then it came back to Philadelphia.

The story also says the experiment had a terrible cost. Some of the crew were said to be trapped inside the metal of the ship. Others went mad, faded in and out of reality, or burst into flames.

The legend says the experiment used Albert Einstein’s unified field theory. It was meant to give Allied ships an edge in the Battle of the Atlantic, the long fight against German U-boats, which were submarines. It is a tale of science gone wrong and of government secrecy. But official history says the experiment never happened.

This story did not come from secret papers or from a whistleblower, a person who reports wrongdoing inside an organization. It began with a handwritten letter.

In January 1956, letters began to reach Morris K. Jessup. They were signed Carl Allen or Carlos Miguel Allende. Jessup was an astronomer and author. In 1955, he had published a book called The Case for the UFO.

Allen’s letters were rambling and strange. He said he had seen the experiment himself. He was standing on the deck of the SS Andrew Furuseth, a merchant ship tied up in Philadelphia.

He described the Eldridge wrapped in a glowing field. He said it vanished, then reappeared in Norfolk, then came back to Philadelphia. His letters talked about Einstein’s theory, invisible force fields, and government cover-ups.

Nothing in the letters could be checked.

In 1980, a writer named Robert Goerman said that Allen was really Carl Meredith Allen of New Kensington, Pennsylvania. Goerman described him as a creative, lonely man. Years later, Allen himself said he had written the notes in the margins of Jessup’s book to scare Jessup.

His letters felt convincing because they were so specific. He named a ship, a place, and a technology. He described what happened to the crew.

But when researchers checked, the evidence fell apart. The USS Eldridge was commissioned, or made ready for service, on August 27, 1943. Its deck log and war diary show that it was never in Philadelphia between August and December 1943.

In 1999, a reunion of Eldridge veterans said the ship never made port in Philadelphia. Other former crew members said they knew nothing about any experiment.

The USS Eldridge was a real ship. It was a Cannon-class destroyer escort. From January 1944 to May 1945, it guarded convoys on nine voyages to the Mediterranean. In 1945, it went to the Pacific.

In 1946, the ship was put in reserve. In 1951 it was given to Greece and renamed the Leon. It left the Greek Navy in 1992 and was sold for scrap in 1999.

The Navy says its records show no strange experiment. On the dates in the legend, they place the ship somewhere other than Philadelphia.

The story might have stayed a small matter between an odd letter writer and an astronomer. But then came one of the strangest events in the history of American intelligence.

Around the same time, the Office of Naval Research received a package. The office is known as the ONR. Inside was a copy of Jessup’s book, with notes written in the margins in three shades of blue ink.

The notes seemed to come from several people. One used the name “Jemi.” The notes talked about secret government plans. Jessup saw this copy at the ONR in 1957.

A company called Varo Manufacturing later made copies of the marked-up book. In the end, there were 127. This is known as the “Varo Edition.” Believers still call the Navy’s copy their strongest piece of “evidence.” The Navy says the ONR never studied radar invisibility.

The legend sounds believable partly because it uses real scientists and real science. Albert Einstein was a real and famous scientist. The legend uses his name.

Einstein also worked on a unified field theory. This was an attempt to join gravity and electromagnetism in a single set of equations. It is not a finished theory. There is no evidence that he ever worked on invisibility, teleportation, or anything like the Philadelphia Experiment.

The legend sometimes says that Nikola Tesla helped build the technology. This cannot be true. Tesla died on January 7, 1943, nearly ten months before the experiment was said to happen.

The physics in the story does not match what we know. The legend says strong electromagnetic fields bent light around the ship so it could not be seen. Today, scientists have ideas for optical cloaking using metamaterials, which are human-made materials with very tiny designed structures. But these must be built with great care at the nanoscale, which is far smaller than anything the eye can see. They look nothing like generators and cables.

Even though the experiment never happened, the Navy really did work on ships during the war. This may have given the legend its seed.

One example is degaussing. This means taking away the magnetism of a ship’s hull. A ship with less magnetism was safer from magnetic mines. Workers wrapped the hull in thick electric cables and ran currents through them. It does not change how a ship looks to the eye or to radar.

Some writers think an outsider could have mistaken this work for an “invisibility” test. This is only a guess.

The Navy also tested ship camouflage in both World Wars. One kind was dazzle camouflage, with bold patterns meant to confuse enemy range-finders. Wartime secrecy at the shipyard, plus degaussing and camouflage, gave rumors a good place to grow. A man like Carl Allen could easily have let his imagination do the rest.

For decades, the story was only talked about among UFO fans. That changed in 1984. A film called The Philadelphia Experiment came out in August. It starred Michael Paré. He played a sailor who is thrown through time to 1984.

The film changed the story a great deal. But it showed the legend to millions of people. A crank’s letter had become a pop culture hit.

The movie gave the story a vivid look. A sequel came out in 1993, and the Syfy channel made a new version in 2012. Each one added new details. In the end, people could not tell the original claims from the made-up parts.

The story also brought in new claimants. In 1989, a man named Alfred Bielek said he had been aboard the Eldridge during the experiment. He later said the 1984 film matched what he had seen.

Bielek added to his claims on radio shows and at conferences. Skeptics say that some people who made such claims were not veterans of the ships at all. No documents support the claims.

The researcher Robert Goerman worked to find out who Carl Allen really was. His work is why we know more about the man behind the letters.

By almost every measure of history and science, the Philadelphia Experiment is a myth. It began with one source, Carl Allen, and he later said he had made up the notes. The ship’s log shows it was not in Philadelphia on the date of the supposed experiment.

The science in the legend does not fit known physics. The Navy has denied that the experiment happened. No document has ever shown otherwise.

So why does the story live on? It speaks to something deeper than proof. Many people believe that governments hide amazing machines. Some think a denial proves a cover-up. Some feel that the truth is always stranger than what we are told.

It is a story about what we cannot know. It is also about how tempting a secret plot can be. And it shows how much we love the idea that somewhere, in a locked vault or hidden lab, the impossible is real. Even when it isn’t, we keep wondering.
