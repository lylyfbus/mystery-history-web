The Great Sphinx of Giza: Secrets of the World's Oldest and Largest Monument

It is dawn on the Giza Plateau in Egypt. The first light of the sun touches the face of the Great Sphinx. For a few minutes, the old limestone glows gold. It looks east, as it has done for about 4,500 years.

The Sphinx is huge. It is 73 meters (240 feet) long and 20 meters (66 feet) high. It was carved from one ridge of limestone bedrock. Bedrock is the solid rock under the ground. Most experts think a pharaoh, a king of ancient Egypt, gave the order. We do not know for certain which one.

The job was enormous. Workers had to dig a trench in the shape of a horseshoe around the rock. Only then was the body free from the stone around it. Since then the Sphinx has sat beside the Great Pyramids. It has lived through sandstorms, invasions, and time.

Yet this giant will not answer the simplest questions about itself. Who carved it? When? Why? And is anything hidden under those massive paws?

The usual answer is Pharaoh Khafre. He ruled from about 2558 to 2532 BCE, and he built a great pyramid at Giza. But a small group of researchers does not agree. For more than 30 years they have argued that the rock tells a very different story.

They say the Sphinx could be thousands of years older than the pyramids. This is one of the fiercest arguments in archaeology, the study of the human past.

The Great Sphinx is more than a statue. It is a riddle carved in stone. Travelers, scholars, and mystics have wondered about it for thousands of years.

The name Sphinx came from the ancient Greeks, about 2,000 years after the statue was made. It may come from a Greek word meaning “to squeeze.” In Greek myth, a sphinx was a monster that strangled people. The Egyptians used the word shesep-ankh, “living image,” for statues like this one. In the Middle Ages, Egyptians called it “father of terror.” And people say that Napoleon’s soldiers used it for target practice. But did they?

Every age brings its own fears and wonders to that strange face, and goes away without an answer. The Sphinx keeps its secrets.

The Great Sphinx sits in a shallow pit on the eastern edge of the Giza Plateau. It faces east, toward the rising sun. It shows a lion lying down, with a human head. The head wears the nemes, the striped royal headcloth of an Egyptian pharaoh.

The body was carved from the natural limestone of the Giza ridge. Builders added cut limestone blocks to the rock. Traces of red, blue, and yellow paint have been found. They suggest the Sphinx was once brightly colored. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder said its face was red.

The Sphinx is unusual in one important way. The pyramids were put together from separate blocks. The Sphinx was mostly carved where it stood, from a natural hill of rock.

Long ago the body was covered with a layer of fine limestone brought from another place. That layer has worn away.

The Sphinx is often called a monolith. A monolith is something made from one single piece of stone. But the Sphinx is not quite that, because builders added blocks to the carved rock.

To free the body from the bedrock, workers removed a great deal of stone. A royal beard once hung from the chin. Pieces of it were found between the paws, and some are now in the British Museum.

Most Egyptologists say Pharaoh Khafre built the Sphinx. Egyptologists are experts who study ancient Egypt. Khafre is also known as Chephren. He ruled in the Fourth Dynasty of the Old Kingdom, from about 2558 to 2532 BCE. He built a great pyramid at Giza.

The evidence for Khafre is indirect. The Sphinx sits next to a causeway, a raised road, that leads from Khafre’s pyramid complex. It is near his Valley Temple. Some experts think this fits one plan.

There is also the Sphinx Temple, which stands in front of the statue. Its blocks were cut from the pit around the Sphinx itself. But the temple was built over part of the wall of Khafre’s Valley Temple. So some scholars think the Sphinx came after Khafre’s buildings. They name other pharaohs, such as Khufu or Djedefre.

The idea has one serious weakness. No writing from Khafre’s own time says that he built the Sphinx. Nothing found so far settles the question.

The Inventory Stele is a carved stone slab that was found near the Sphinx in 1857. But it was made much later, in the 26th Dynasty, around 600 BCE. It says that Khufu found the Sphinx already buried in sand. Scholars do not trust this story.

So the case rests mostly on location and building style. The Sphinx seems to belong to Khafre’s pyramid complex because of where it sits. No ancient text from his time says so.

One of the best-known stories about the Sphinx is about its missing nose. People say that the soldiers of Napoleon Bonaparte shot it off with cannons. This was supposed to have happened during the French campaign in Egypt in 1798. The story is almost certainly false.

European travelers made careful drawings of the Sphinx long before that. One was a Danish naval captain, Frederic Louis Norden. He visited Giza in 1737, decades before Napoleon was born. His drawing already shows the Sphinx without its nose.

One account comes from al-Maqrizi, an Arab historian of the early 1400s. He blamed a Sufi Muslim named Muhammad Sa’im al-Dahr. According to al-Maqrizi, it happened in 1378 CE.

Local farmers were making offerings to the Sphinx. They hoped it would give them better harvests. Al-Dahr was furious, because he believed this was the worship of a false god. He climbed onto the Sphinx and cut off its nose with chisels.

Studies show that rods or chisels were used to pry the nose off. So it was broken on purpose. But the archaeologist Mark Lehner thinks this happened earlier, between the 3rd and 10th centuries CE. So the 1378 story may not be the whole truth.

In 1990, a geologist named Robert M. Schoch traveled to Egypt. A geologist is a scientist who studies rocks. The writer John Anthony West had invited him.

Schoch wanted to look at the Sphinx only as a geologist. He would read the worn marks in the limestone like the layers of a cliff. What he said he found started a long argument about the Sphinx.

He studied the body of the Sphinx. He also studied the walls of the Sphinx Enclosure, the horseshoe-shaped pit from which the statue was carved. He found deep cracks and wavy surfaces. He argued that only long periods of rain and running water could have made them.

This was not like the wind damage on other Old Kingdom buildings at Giza. Wind makes sharp, angular cuts and layered patterns. The walls of the pit had the rounded, rolling shapes that water leaves behind.

Schoch said the Giza region has been very dry for about 5,000 years. If rain wore down the Sphinx, the Sphinx must be older than the drying of the Sahara.

That would put its carving back to at least 5000 BCE, he said. Later he suggested the end of the last Ice Age, around 10,000 BCE. That is thousands of years before the usual date of Khafre’s reign. It is also thousands of years before writing, giant buildings, or the Egyptian state.

Egyptologists and other geologists pushed back hard. They pointed out that the Sphinx Temple was built over part of an older temple wall. They also said the temple was never finished. Both facts suggest a later date.

Tests that measure when stone was last in sunlight also fit the time of the Fourth Dynasty.

Critics also said the rain estimates were weak. One study says the Egyptian Sahara turned dry around 5300 BCE. Other evidence points to heavy rains until about 2200 BCE. No great flood from before history was needed.

The geologist James Harrell and others blamed haloclasty. That is damage from salt. Salt crystals grow inside the tiny holes in the limestone and break it. This leaves rounded shapes that look like water damage.

Most archaeologists and Egyptologists reject the idea of an earlier date. They call it a fringe theory, which means an idea far outside the main view.

Are there hidden rooms under the Great Sphinx? People have guessed about this for a long time. Much of the talk began with Edgar Cayce, an American who said in the 1900s that a Hall of Records lay near the Sphinx. Nobody has found one.

In 1991, Schoch led a survey of the Sphinx area. The geophysicist Thomas Dobecki said that the seismic readings showed a hollow space under the right paw. A seismic survey sends waves into the ground to show what lies below. He thought people might have made the space.

A group that follows Cayce’s ideas sent an observer. In 1996, a further survey looked at the same spot. Later, Egypt’s antiquities council allowed drilling at one spot near the Great Pyramid. It found only a natural hollow. The council refused more drilling.

In 1999, Egyptian archaeologist Zahi Hawass had a flooded shaft near the Sphinx emptied and explored. Nothing like Cayce’s Hall of Records was found. So far, no hidden room under the Sphinx has been proved.

Between the long front paws of the Sphinx stands a slab of granite. It is called the Dream Stele, or the Sphinx Stele. Pharaoh Thutmose IV set it up around 1400 BCE. By the usual dating, that was more than a thousand years after the Sphinx was built.

The stele tells the story of the young prince Thutmose. One day he was out hunting near Giza. He grew tired and lay down to rest in the shadow of the Sphinx. Sand had buried much of the statue.

As he slept, the Sphinx came to him in a dream and spoke. In the words of the stele, it promised him a kingdom if he cleared away the sand.

The stele says that he did this. And he became pharaoh, as the dream had promised.

The Dream Stele tells us a lot about the place of the Sphinx in Egyptian religion. By the time of Thutmose IV, the Sphinx was already very old. It had been buried in sand, and its first purpose may have been forgotten.

The stele shows the statue as a form of the sun god Horemakhet, “Horus in the Horizon.” Part of the text that may name Khafre is damaged, so it cannot settle the question.

The Great Sphinx has stared east across the desert for thousands of years. It has been buried in sand and dug out again. Its nose was broken off long ago, and its beard broke away.

It has been honored as a form of a god and ignored as an oddity. By the usual story, it has stood through 4,500 years of wind, sand, and human foolishness. Or perhaps it has stood far longer, as the marks on the walls of its pit may show.

The argument between the Egyptologists and the geologists who disagree will not end soon. But nobody argues about the power of the Sphinx as a symbol. It stands for the mystery of the ancient world, for the limits of what we know, and for the long time between us and its carvers.

Every theory about the Sphinx tells us as much about the thinker as about the statue. Most scholars see a work of the Old Kingdom. It is impressive, but it fits inside the known history of Egypt. Other researchers see signs of a lost chapter in the human story.

The Sphinx itself stays silent. It has kept that silence for thousands of years, and it will probably keep it for thousands more. That silence is the true mystery of the Great Sphinx of Giza. Who made it, and when?
