The Cadaver Synod: The Bizarre Trial Where a Dead Pope Was Dug Up and Condemned

Pope Stephen VI presiding over the Cadaver Synod of 897 — one of the most grotesque and surreal events in the history of the Catholic Church

It was January 897, in Rome. In the Basilica of St. John Lateran, the pope’s own church, a trial was about to begin. The judge was Pope Stephen VI, who is sometimes called Stephen VII. He wore the full robes of his office.

The man on trial was Pope Formosus. He had been pope from October 891 until his death on April 4, 896. There was one problem. Formosus had been dead for about nine months.

His body had been dug up from its tomb. It was carried into the church and dressed in the robes of a pope. Then it was propped up on a throne to face its accuser. A deacon, a junior church officer, was chosen to speak for the dead man and answer the charges.

The charges were perjury, which means lying after a promise to tell the truth. Formosus was also accused of wanting the papacy too much. And he was accused of breaking church law by moving from one bishop’s post to another. Stephen VI questioned the body, and the deacon replied for it.

When the trial ended, Formosus was found guilty on all counts. His time as pope was declared void, as if it had never happened. Three fingers of his right hand, the ones used to give blessings, were cut off. His robes were taken away.

Every ordination and appointment he had made was declared invalid. An ordination is the ceremony that makes someone a priest or bishop. This threw the Church into trouble. Then the body was carried out and buried in a cemetery for foreigners and strangers. Later it was dug up again and thrown into the Tiber River.

This event is called the Cadaver Synod. A synod is a church meeting. It is also known by a Latin name, the Synodus Horrenda, or Horrible Synod. It remains one of the strangest events in the whole history of the popes.

Why would a pope put a corpse on trial? To understand, we must look at Rome in the late ninth century. This time is sometimes called the Saeculum Obscurum, which means the Dark Age of the papacy. Popes came and went very fast. Some were removed, jailed, or killed.

Between 896 and 904, there was a new pope about every year.

The papacy was not really a spiritual office then. It was a political prize. Rival Roman noble families fought over it. So did the Holy Roman Emperor and other Italian noble houses. They saw the papacy as a way to gain power and wealth.

The strongest groups included the house of Spoleto and the family of Theophylact. They had great power over who became pope. They treated the Chair of St. Peter like a political football. In this world, the Cadaver Synod was not a strange accident. It was an extreme example of the political war that had taken over the Church.

Pope Formosus (about 816–896) was not a villain. Many accounts say he was a capable and respected churchman. He was unlucky to be pope in one of the most unstable times in Roman history. He was born in Rome around 816. He rose through the Church under Pope Nicholas I.

In 864, he became Bishop of Porto, a church district near Rome. Formosus was a skilled diplomat. He led a mission to Bulgaria in 866. He was also sent on missions to France in 869 and 872.

His skills made him one of the best-known churchmen in Rome. But they also made him a target for his enemies.

In 872, Formosus was named as a possible pope. But John VIII was chosen instead. John VIII became a bitter enemy.

In 876, John VIII drove Formosus out of Rome. Formosus refused to return and was excommunicated, which means thrown out of the Church. He found safety in Tours, in France.

Pope Marinus I forgave him in 883 and gave him back his post at Porto. Then, on October 6, 891, he was elected pope, when he was about 75 years old.

As pope, Formosus made a fateful choice. In February 896, he crowned Arnulf of Carinthia, King of East Francia, as emperor. This made the powerful house of Spoleto furious. They had imperial plans of their own. They saw Arnulf as a threat to their hold on Italy.

When Formosus died on April 4, 896, the Spoleto group saw a chance for revenge. They wanted to punish not just the dead pope but everything he had done. That meant everyone he had appointed and every alliance he had made.

The year 896 was one of the most chaotic in papal history. Three men held the office in that single year. Formosus died on April 4. He was followed by Pope Boniface VI, who ruled for about fifteen days. He died, reportedly of gout.

Next came Pope Stephen VI, the future judge of the Cadaver Synod. He had long been a critic of Formosus. He served until the summer of 897, when he was removed from office and killed. After him came Pope Romanus, who lasted only a few months.

The quick changes show how fierce the contest between Roman families was. Each one installed its own pope and threw out the last one’s choice.

The Cadaver Synod took place in January 897 at the Lateran Basilica, one of the holiest places in Rome. Stephen VI had Formosus’s body taken from its tomb. It had been in the tomb for about nine months.

The body was dressed in the robes Formosus had worn in life. It was then placed on a throne in the middle of the church, where a living accused man would stand.

The scene was strange and frightening. A dead pope sat in the finest robes of the Church. Around him were bishops, cardinals, and nobles. Stephen VI sat on his own throne, like a judge in a courtroom.

The charges came in three parts. First, Stephen VI said Formosus had lied under oath. He claimed Formosus had sworn never to return to Rome after John VIII sent him away.

Second, he said Formosus had wanted the papacy too much. Third, he said Formosus had broken church law. Formosus had been Bishop of Porto and then Bishop of Rome, which means pope. Moving from one bishop’s post to another was forbidden by the Church’s rules. This was called translation of sees.

The charges were not entirely baseless. Formosus really had been Bishop of Porto before he became pope. Whether he had properly given up that post was a real question of church law.

But the trial was not about law. It was about politics. If Formosus’s time as pope was invalid, Stephen VI could cancel all of his appointments. Some historians think the Spoleto family pushed for the trial, and that it could then undo Formosus’s friendship with Arnulf. It also let Stephen replace Formosus’s friends with his own.

Nobody doubted how the trial would end. Stephen VI was both the prosecutor and the judge. Formosus was found guilty on all counts.

The punishment was harsh and meant to shame him. First, the robes were stripped from the body. Then, according to Liutprand, Stephen VI ordered that three fingers of the right hand be cut off. They were the fingers used to give the papal blessing. This was a symbolic act. It was meant to destroy Formosus’s spiritual power.

The body was then buried in a graveyard for foreigners and strangers. That was an insult to a man who had led the Catholic Church.

The cruelty did not stop there. Stephen VI declared that all of Formosus’s ordinations and appointments were invalid. Every priest he had ordained, every bishop he had consecrated, and every official he had appointed lost their office.

The result was a disaster. Many clergy suddenly found their authority in doubt. Their sacraments, or holy ceremonies, were now in doubt.

Finally, the body was dug up again and thrown into the Tiber River. It was the last act of disrespect in a campaign of punishment against a dead man.

The body of Formosus did not stay in the river for long, according to later stories. One account says monks recovered it the next day and secretly buried it. Another says a hermit saved it. The sources do not agree.

Formosus’s supporters took this as a sign of divine favor. They saw it as proof that the wronged pope was innocent.

Public sympathy turned in his favor. People pictured the dead pope, abused by a living pope, and then rescued by loyal followers. It was a powerful image.

It turned Formosus into a symbol of suffering and injustice. It made Stephen VI look not just ruthless but monstrous. That view may have helped to cost Stephen his throne and his life within months.

In the narrowest sense, the Cadaver Synod was a success for Stephen VI. Formosus was declared guilty. His appointments were canceled.

But it was a terrible public relations disaster. A pope had put a corpse on trial. The dead body had been harmed and thrown in the Tiber. Many people in Rome were horrified.

The Cadaver Synod was seen as deeply offensive and sacrilegious, which means disrespectful to holy things. Public opinion turned against Stephen VI. The very act meant to strengthen his power may have destroyed it instead.

The backlash came quickly. In the summer of 897, within a year of the Cadaver Synod, Stephen VI was removed from power. A public uprising in Rome turned against him.

Stephen was thrown in prison. Soon afterward he was strangled in his cell. His reign began with the shocking sight of a corpse on trial. It ended with his own death at the hands of his enemies.

Pope Romanus followed him and served only a few months. Then Pope Theodore II was elected. He moved to undo the verdict of the Cadaver Synod.

In December 897, Theodore II held a synod that canceled the Cadaver Synod and restored Formosus’s good name. He ordered that the remains be reburied in St. Peter’s Basilica. His reign was very short.

But his successor, Pope John IX (898–900), also acted against the Cadaver Synod. In 898, John IX held another synod. It canceled the Cadaver Synod and banned any future trials of the dead.

The story does not end there. In 904, Pope Sergius III (904–911) took the throne. He revived the decrees of the Cadaver Synod. Once again he declared Formosus’s ordinations invalid.

The reversal of the reversal of the reversal made a tangle of law and religion. The political fights that led to the Cadaver Synod were far from over.

For years, popes and groups took turns condemning and honoring Formosus’s memory. The Cadaver Synod became a symbol of what was wrong with the medieval papacy. It stood for corruption, political tricks, violence, and the way spiritual power bowed to worldly power.

Where do we learn about the Cadaver Synod? Our main sources each have their own biases and limits. Liutprand of Cremona (about 920–972) was a Lombard historian and diplomat. He gives one of the fullest accounts in his book Antapodosis, which means “Book of Retribution.” He wrote it decades after the event.

Liutprand served an emperor and could be harsh toward the popes of his own time. That may have shaped how he showed the Cadaver Synod, as a terrible case of papal corruption.

Auxilius of Naples was a supporter of Formosus. He wrote about the synod too, and he is sympathetic to the dead pope.

Despite the biases, historians agree that the trial really happened. They also agree on the main facts: the digging up, the trial, the guilty verdict, and the dumping of the body in the Tiber. Details such as the cutting of the fingers come mostly from Liutprand.

The Cadaver Synod of 897 sounds like fiction, but historians agree that it happened. A dead pope was dug up, dressed in robes, propped on a throne, and shouted at by his successor. His body was harmed, his good name was attacked, and his remains were thrown into a river.

The trial was political power dressed up as church justice. One group tried to cancel the acts of a rival by destroying his memory and undoing every appointment he made. The backlash was fast. Stephen VI was removed and strangled within months, and later popes reversed the verdict.

But the damage was done. The Cadaver Synod showed how political the papacy had become in the late ninth century. It shows that even the most sacred institutions can be spoiled by political power.

It also shows how easily justice turns into revenge when the accused cannot speak in his own defense. Formosus had been dead for nine months. Could anything be fair about that trial?
