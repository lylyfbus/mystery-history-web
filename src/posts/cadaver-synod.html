The Cadaver Synod: The Bizarre Trial Where a Dead Pope Was Dug Up and Condemned

Pope Stephen VI presiding over the Cadaver Synod of 897 — one of the most grotesque and surreal events in the history of the Catholic Church

A trial with a silent defendant

It was January 897, in Rome. In the Basilica of St. John Lateran, the pope’s own church, a trial was about to begin. The judge was Pope Stephen VI, who is sometimes called Stephen VII. He wore the full robes of his office.

The man on trial was Pope Formosus. He had been pope from October 891 until his death on April 4, 896. There was one problem. Formosus had been dead for about nine months.

His body had been taken from its tomb. It was carried into the church and dressed in the robes of a pope. Then it was propped up on a throne to face its accuser. A deacon, a junior church officer, was chosen to speak for the dead man.

This event is called the Cadaver Synod. A synod is a church meeting. It also has a Latin name, the Synodus Horrenda, or Horrible Synod. Why would a pope put a corpse on trial? Let us start with the charges.

Three charges against the dead

The first charge was perjury, which means lying after a promise to tell the truth. Stephen VI said that Formosus had sworn never to return to Rome after Pope John VIII sent him away.

The second charge was that Formosus had wanted the papacy too much. The third was about church law. Formosus had been Bishop of Porto and then Bishop of Rome, which means pope. Moving from one bishop’s post to another was forbidden by the Church’s rules.

Stephen VI questioned the body, and the deacon replied for it. The charges were not entirely baseless. Formosus really had been Bishop of Porto before he became pope. Whether he had properly given up that post was a real question of church law.

But nobody doubted how the trial would end. Stephen VI was both the prosecutor and the judge. Some historians think the real reason for the trial was politics, not church law.

A prize for rival families

The late ninth century is sometimes called the Saeculum Obscurum, which means the Dark Age of the papacy. Popes came and went very fast. Between 896 and 904, there was a new pope about every year. Some were removed, jailed, or killed.

The papacy was not only a spiritual office then. It was also a political prize. Rival Roman noble families fought over it. So did the Holy Roman Emperor and other Italian noble houses. The strongest groups included the house of Spoleto and the family of Theophylact.

The year 896 shows how wild it was. Three men held the office. Formosus died on April 4. Boniface VI followed, and he ruled for about fifteen days. Then came Stephen VI, who had long been a critic of Formosus.

In this world, the Cadaver Synod was not a strange accident. It was an extreme case of a political war. So who was the man they put on trial?

The pope who made enemies

Many accounts say that Formosus was a capable and respected churchman. He was born in Rome around 816, and he rose through the Church under Pope Nicholas I. In 864, he became Bishop of Porto, a church district near Rome. He was a skilled diplomat. He led a mission to Bulgaria in 866, and he went on missions to France in 869 and 872.

His skills made him well known. They also made him a target. In 872, he was named as a possible pope, but John VIII was chosen instead. John VIII became a bitter enemy. In 876, he drove Formosus out of Rome. Formosus refused to return and was excommunicated, which means thrown out of the Church. He found safety in Tours, in France.

Pope Marinus I forgave him in 883 and gave him back his post at Porto. On October 6, 891, Formosus was elected pope, at about 75 years old.

Then he made a fateful choice. In February 896, he crowned Arnulf of Carinthia, King of East Francia, as emperor. Some historians think that this angered the powerful house of Spoleto, who saw Arnulf as a threat to their hold on Italy. When Formosus died two months later, they may have seen a chance for revenge.

The verdict and the river

The trial was a way to cancel everything that Formosus had done. If his time as pope was invalid, Stephen VI could undo his appointments. Some historians think that the Spoleto family pushed for the trial. It also let Stephen replace Formosus’s friends with his own.

Formosus was found guilty on all counts. His time as pope was declared void, as if it had never happened. The robes were taken from the body. According to Liutprand, Stephen VI ordered that three fingers of the right hand be cut off. They were the fingers used to give the papal blessing. It was a symbol, meant to destroy Formosus’s spiritual power.

The body was buried in a graveyard for foreigners and strangers. That was an insult to a man who had led the Church. Later it was dug up again and thrown into the Tiber River.

Stephen VI also declared that every ordination Formosus had made was invalid. An ordination is the ceremony that makes someone a priest or bishop. Many clergy suddenly found their authority in doubt, and so were their sacraments, or holy ceremonies.

The body did not stay in the river, according to later stories. One account says that monks recovered it the next day and buried it in secret. Another says that a hermit saved it. The sources do not agree.

When the people turned

Many people in Rome were horrified. A pope had put a corpse on trial. They saw the act as sacrilege, which means disrespect to holy things. Formosus’s supporters took the rescue stories as a sign that the wronged pope was innocent.

The backlash came fast. In the summer of 897, a public uprising in Rome turned against Stephen VI. He was removed from office and thrown into prison. Soon afterward he was killed in his cell. His reign began with a corpse on trial and ended with his own death.

Pope Romanus followed him and served only a few months. Then Pope Theodore II was elected. In December 897, he held a synod that canceled the Cadaver Synod and restored Formosus’s good name. He ordered that the remains be reburied in St. Peter’s Basilica. In 898, Pope John IX held another synod. It canceled the Cadaver Synod again and banned any future trials of the dead.

But the tale did not end there. In 904, Pope Sergius III took the throne. He revived the decrees of the Cadaver Synod and declared Formosus’s ordinations invalid once more. For years, popes and groups took turns condemning and honoring the memory of one man.

How do we know?

Our main sources have their own biases. Liutprand of Cremona (about 920–972) was a Lombard historian and diplomat. He gave one of the fullest accounts in his book Antapodosis, which means “Book of Retribution.” He wrote it decades after the event. He served an emperor and could be harsh toward the popes of his own time. That may have shaped how he showed the synod, as a case of papal corruption.

Auxilius of Naples was a supporter of Formosus. He wrote about the synod too, and he is kind to the dead pope.

Even so, historians agree that the trial really happened. They agree on the main facts: the digging up, the trial, the guilty verdict, and the dumping of the body in the Tiber. Details such as the cutting of the fingers come mostly from Liutprand.

A question for the dead

The Cadaver Synod sounds like fiction, but historians agree that it happened. A dead pope was dressed in robes, propped on a throne, and questioned by his successor. The trial was political power dressed up as church justice. Later popes reversed the verdict, and then one of them reversed it again.

What is open is why Stephen VI did it. Some historians think that the Spoleto family pushed him. Nobody can say for sure.

Think again of that church in January 897, and the silent figure on the throne. Formosus had been dead for nine months. The deacon spoke for him, but nobody could truly speak for him. Could anything be fair about that trial?
