Rosetta Stone: How One Broken Slab Unlocked 3,000 Years of Lost Egyptian History

At first glance, the Rosetta Stone is not much to look at. It is a broken slab of dark, speckled stone, about the size of a coffee table. Three bands of tiny, tightly packed writing cover its face. Most visitors to the British Museum cannot read any of it.

Yet this plain-looking object may be the most important find in the history of archaeology. It unlocked 3,000 years of lost Egyptian history. It opened a whole ancient world to us.

The Rosetta Stone is a piece of a larger stone slab called a stele. It is made of granodiorite, and it was carved in 196 BCE. It carries a decree from priests. A decree is an official order. This one backed the royal cult of the young pharaoh Ptolemy V Epiphanes.

What makes it special is that the decree is written in three kinds of writing. The first is ancient Egyptian hieroglyphics, the sacred picture writing of the priests. The second is Demotic, the everyday writing of the Egyptian people. The third is Ancient Greek, the language of the Ptolemaic rulers.

The last hieroglyphic writing was carved on the island of Philae in 394 CE. After that, for about 1,400 years, nobody could read the language of ancient Egypt. It was like a locked code that guarded the secrets of one of the great civilizations. The Rosetta Stone gave us the key.

The story begins with Napoleon Bonaparte’s Egyptian Campaign of 1798 to 1801. Napoleon invaded Egypt with a huge army. He also brought 167 scientists, engineers, and scholars. Their job was to study and record every part of Egyptian life.

In mid-July 1799, French soldiers were pulling down an old wall inside Fort Julien. The captain in charge was Pierre-François Bouchard. The fort stood near the town of Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta. It lay about 35 miles northeast of Alexandria.

As the wall came down, the soldiers saw a flat slab of dark stone. Writing covered its smooth face. Bouchard saw that the stone had three kinds of writing, and one was clearly Greek. He understood at once that it might be very important. He told his superiors.

The find was announced in the Courrier de l’Égypte, the official French newspaper in Cairo. An officer wrote that the stone “is of great interest for the study of hieroglyphic characters; maybe it will even prove to be the key to understanding them.” He was right.

The stone is only a fragment of a much larger stele. It is about 114.4 centimeters (45 inches) high, 72.3 centimeters wide, and 27.9 centimeters thick. It weighs about 760 kilograms (1,676 pounds). Granodiorite is a hard rock with coarse grains, similar to granite.

The top right and bottom right corners are broken off. The hieroglyphic part at the top is the most damaged. Only 14 lines survive, out of perhaps 29 or more. The Demotic part has 32 lines, and the Greek part has 54.

The writing records a decree passed by a council of Egyptian priests at Memphis in 196 BCE. The king then was the boy-king Ptolemy V. He had become king as a child, and now he was being accepted as an adult ruler. The decree lists the good things the king had done for the temples and the priests. It orders that the decree be carved in three kinds of writing and put up in temples across Egypt.

The stone’s road from a fort wall in Egypt to a glass case in the British Museum is a story of war, deals, and empire. In August 1798, the British Royal Navy destroyed the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile. Napoleon went back to France and left his army stranded.

In 1801, the British defeated the French at Alexandria. The Treaty of Alexandria said the French must hand over all the old objects and science collections they had gathered. General Jacques-François Menou had taken the stone for himself. At first he refused to give it up. He said it was his own property.

After talks, the stone was given to Colonel Tomkyns Hilgrove Turner of the British Army. He took it to England. It reached Portsmouth in February 1802. It was offered to King George III, who gave it to the British Museum.

It has stayed there ever since, with one notable exception. During World War I, it was moved to an underground station to keep it safe from German bombing.

The code was not broken by one genius in one moment. It took a long, hard struggle of more than twenty years. In 1801, a French scholar of eastern languages, Silvestre de Sacy, tried to read the Demotic by comparing it with the Greek. He found a few names.

A Swedish diplomat, Johan David Åkerblad, went further. He correctly found that some Demotic signs stood for single sounds, like letters. But he wrongly thought that all Demotic signs worked that way. In fact, the script also used signs for whole ideas, and signs that clarify meaning.

The big breakthroughs came from two men working on their own. The first was Thomas Young, who lived from 1773 to 1829. He was a British doctor and physicist with many talents. He also made key discoveries about light and about how things move.

In 1814, Young began to study the Rosetta Stone. His best insight was about cartouches. These are oval rings drawn around some groups of hieroglyphs. He saw that they held royal names.

He found the name “Ptolemy” in the hieroglyphic text by matching it with the Greek. Later he found the name “Berenice” as well. Young also showed, for the first time, that hieroglyphs were not only symbols or hidden meanings, as scholars had thought for centuries. They could also stand for sounds. He published his findings in 1819, in a supplement to the Encyclopaedia Britannica.

The man who finished the job was Jean-François Champollion, who lived from 1790 to 1832. He was a French language expert who had loved ancient Egypt since he was a child. He was a prodigy. By sixteen he had learned Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Chaldean, Chinese, Coptic, Ethiopic, Sanskrit, Persian, and Amharic.

His deep knowledge of Coptic was the deciding advantage. Coptic is the last stage of the Egyptian language, written in Greek letters.

Champollion built on Young’s work on cartouches and sound signs. He focused on royal names. He began with “Ptolemy.” Then he turned to a second cartouche, from an obelisk at Philae. He worked out that it held the name “Cleopatra.” That name shared several letters with “Ptolemy.”

By comparing the signs the two names shared, he found the sounds of many hieroglyphs.

His greatest insight went beyond matching sounds. He saw that hieroglyphs were a mixed system. It used signs for sounds, signs for ideas, and signs that make a meaning clear. The system was very complex, and very beautiful.

On September 27, 1822, Champollion is said to have burst into his brother’s office at the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres in Paris. He shouted “Je tiens mon affaire!”, which means “I’ve got it!” Then he fainted from exhaustion.

He presented his findings in the famous “Lettre à M. Dacier.” It explained the rules of the hieroglyphic system for the first time. In 1824, he published a full book, the “Précis du système hiéroglyphique des anciens Égyptiens.” It started Egyptology as a modern field of study.

Champollion’s achievement cannot be overstated. He opened a door to 3,000 years of human history. That door had been sealed since the fall of the Roman Empire.

The decipherment did more than translate one old text. It unlocked a whole civilization. Before Champollion, the temples, tombs, and monuments of Egypt were covered with beautiful symbols nobody could understand.

After him, the great body of Egyptian writing could be read for the first time in over a thousand years. It included religious texts such as the Book of the Dead, historical writings, medical papyri, math books, personal letters, and government records.

The Rosetta Stone is not one of a kind. It is one of several copies of the Memphis decree of 196 BCE. More copies have been found at Philae, Elephantine, and Damanhur. Some are in better shape than the Rosetta Stone. The stone is famous not because its words are unique. It is famous because it was the first three-language text found. It was the one that cracked the code.

The last known hieroglyphic writing was carved at Philae in southern Egypt on August 24, 394 CE. The script had first been developed about 3200 BCE, so it had lasted over 3,500 years. A priest named Esmet-Akhom carved it, and it is dedicated to the god Osiris.

After that date, the knowledge of how to read and write hieroglyphs was lost for more than 1,400 years, until Champollion’s breakthrough.

Only the Coptic Church kept the Egyptian language alive. It used the language, written in Greek letters, for its church services. And it was Champollion’s knowledge of Coptic that helped him see the sounds of the hieroglyphic signs.

Today, the stone is at the center of a debate about returning objects to their home countries. In 2003, Zahi Hawass was the head of Egypt’s Supreme Council of Antiquities. He formally asked for the stone to be returned to Egypt. He argued that it was taken during foreign occupation and belongs in the land where it was made.

The British Museum has said no. It points to the stone’s importance to all the world, and to the legal force of the 1801 treaty. A copy of the stone now stands in Rashid, where it was found. It is a reminder of what was lost, and of what remains abroad.

In the end, the Rosetta Stone is a broken slab of rock. It is a piece of an official order that praised the divine status of a teenage king. But its effect has been revolutionary. It gave us back 3,000 years of human history. We regained its writings, its religion, its science, its daily life, and the great works of one of the world’s greatest civilizations.

Today it stands in the British Museum. Visitors from every continent gather around it. It is a dark and plain stone that changed everything.
