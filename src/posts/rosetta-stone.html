Rosetta Stone: How One Broken Slab Unlocked 3,000 Years of Lost Egyptian History

Image: Hans Hillewaert, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

At first glance, the Rosetta Stone is not much to look at. It is a broken slab of dark, speckled stone. Three bands of tiny, tightly packed writing cover its face. Most visitors to the British Museum cannot read any of it.

Yet this plain-looking object may be the most famous find in the history of archaeology. It helped unlock thousands of years of lost Egyptian history. It opened a whole ancient world to us.

The Rosetta Stone is a piece of a larger stone slab called a stele. It is made of granodiorite, and it was carved in 196 BCE. It carries a decree from priests. A decree is an official order. This one honored the young pharaoh Ptolemy V Epiphanes.

What makes it special is that the decree is written in three kinds of writing. The first is ancient Egyptian hieroglyphics, the sacred picture writing of the priests. The second is Demotic, the everyday writing of the Egyptian people. The third is Ancient Greek, the language of the Ptolemaic rulers.

The last hieroglyphic writing was carved on the island of Philae in 394 CE. After that, for more than 1,400 years, nobody could read the writing of ancient Egypt. It was like a locked code that guarded the secrets of one of the great civilizations. The Rosetta Stone gave us the key.

The story begins with Napoleon Bonaparte’s Egyptian Campaign of 1798 to 1801. Napoleon invaded Egypt with a huge army. He also brought more than 150 scientists, engineers, and scholars. Their job was to study and record every part of Egyptian life.

In July 1799, French soldiers were working on an old wall inside Fort Julien. The captain in charge was Pierre-François Bouchard. The fort stood near the town of Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta. It lay a couple of miles from the town.

The soldiers found a flat slab of dark stone. Writing covered its smooth face. Bouchard saw that the stone had three kinds of writing, and one was Greek. He thought it might be very important. He told his superiors.

The find was announced in the Courrier de l’Égypte, the French newspaper in Cairo. The writer hoped the stone might help to read hieroglyphs. He was right.

The stone is only a fragment of a much larger stele. It is about 112 centimeters high, 76 centimeters wide, and 28 centimeters thick. It weighs about 760 kilograms (1,676 pounds). Granodiorite is a hard rock with coarse grains, similar to granite.

The top is broken off, and the hieroglyphic part is the most damaged. Only 14 lines can be seen. The Demotic part has 32 lines, and the Greek part has 54.

The writing records a decree passed by a council of Egyptian priests at Memphis in 196 BCE. The king then was Ptolemy V. He had become king as a child of about five and was crowned at Memphis at about twelve. The decree lists the good things the king had done for the temples and the priests. It orders that the decree be carved in three kinds of writing and put up in temples across Egypt.

The stone’s road from a fort wall in Egypt to a glass case in the British Museum is a story of war, deals, and empire. In 1801, the British defeated the French in Egypt. Napoleon had already gone back to France.

The British demanded the old objects that the French had gathered. General Jacques-François Menou claimed the stone as his own property. At first he refused to give it up.

After talks, the stone was given to Colonel Tomkyns Hilgrove Turner of the British Army. He took it to England. It reached Portsmouth in February 1802. Turner was told to present it to King George III, and it was placed in the British Museum.

It has stayed there ever since, with one notable exception. During World War I, it was moved to an underground postal railway station to keep it safe.

The code was not broken by one genius in one moment. It took a long, hard struggle of more than twenty years. Around 1802, a French scholar of eastern languages, Silvestre de Sacy, tried to read the Demotic by comparing it with the Greek. He found a few names.

A Swedish diplomat, Johan David Åkerblad, went further. He published an alphabet for some Demotic signs. But the script also used signs for whole ideas, and signs that clarify meaning.

The big breakthroughs came from two men working on their own. The first was Thomas Young, who lived from 1773 to 1829. He was a British doctor and physicist with many talents. He also made key discoveries about light and about how things move.

In 1814, Young began to study the Rosetta Stone. His best insight was about cartouches. These are oval rings drawn around some groups of hieroglyphs. He saw that they held royal names.

He found the name “Ptolemy” in the hieroglyphic text by matching it with the Greek. He also made some progress with the name “Berenice.” Young showed that hieroglyphs were not only symbols or hidden meanings, as many scholars had thought. They could also stand for sounds. He published his findings in an article for the Encyclopaedia Britannica.

The man who finished the job was Jean-François Champollion, who lived from 1790 to 1832. He was a French language expert. He could read Coptic, Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Arabic, among other languages.

His deep knowledge of Coptic was the deciding advantage. Coptic is the last stage of the Egyptian language, written in Greek letters.

Champollion built on Young’s work on cartouches and sound signs. He focused on royal names. He began with “Ptolemy.” Then he turned to a second cartouche, from an obelisk at Philae. He worked out that it held the name “Cleopatra.” That name shared several letters with “Ptolemy.”

By comparing the signs the two names shared, he found the sounds of many hieroglyphs.

His greatest insight went beyond matching sounds. He saw that hieroglyphs were a mixed system. It used signs for sounds, signs for ideas, and signs that make a meaning clear. The system was very complex, and very beautiful.

On September 14, 1822, Champollion is said to have burst into his brother’s office in Paris. The story says he shouted “Je tiens mon affaire!”, which means “I’ve got it!” and then fainted. This tale may be a legend.

On September 27, 1822, he presented his findings in the famous “Lettre à M. Dacier.” In 1824, he published a full book, the “Précis du système hiéroglyphique des anciens Égyptiens.” Many people say it started Egyptology as a modern field of study.

Champollion’s achievement was huge. He opened a door to thousands of years of human history. That door had been closed since the ancient Egyptian writing went out of use.

The decipherment did more than translate one old text. It unlocked a whole civilization. Before Champollion, the temples, tombs, and monuments of Egypt were covered with beautiful symbols nobody could understand.

After him, the great body of Egyptian writing could be read again after more than a thousand years. It included religious texts such as the Book of the Dead, historical writings, medical papyri, math books, personal letters, and government records.

The Rosetta Stone is not one of a kind. It is one of several copies of the Memphis decree of 196 BCE. Pieces of other copies have been found at Elephantine, Nubayrah, and Philae. The stone is famous not because its words are unique. It is famous because it was found early and was in good enough shape to help crack the code.

The last known hieroglyphic writing was carved at Philae in southern Egypt on August 24, 394 CE. The script had first been developed about 3200 BCE, so it had lasted over 3,500 years. A priest named Esmet-Akhom carved it.

After that date, the knowledge of how to read and write hieroglyphs was lost for more than 1,400 years, until Champollion’s breakthrough.

Only the Coptic Church kept the Egyptian language alive. It used the language, written in Greek letters, for its church services. And it was Champollion’s knowledge of Coptic that helped him see the sounds of the hieroglyphic signs.

Today, the stone is at the center of a debate about returning objects to their home countries. In 2003, Zahi Hawass, a leading Egyptian official for antiquities, called for the stone to be returned to Egypt. Others have argued that it was taken during foreign occupation and belongs in the land where it was made.

The stone is still in the British Museum. In 2005, the museum gave Egypt a copy of the stone. It is shown in Rashid, where the stone was found.

In the end, the Rosetta Stone is a broken slab of rock. It is a piece of an official order that honored a young king. But its effect has been revolutionary. It helped give us back thousands of years of human history. We regained its writings, its religion, its science, its daily life, and the great works of one of the world’s greatest civilizations.

Today it stands in the British Museum. Visitors from every continent gather around it. It is a dark and plain stone that changed everything.
