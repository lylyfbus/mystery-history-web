The Copper Scroll Treasure: The World's Oldest Treasure Map and Billions in Hidden Gold

The Copper Scroll, found in a desert cave in 1952, lists 64 treasure locations worth billions. Not a single cache has ever been found.

Two rolls of rusted copper

It was March 14, 1952. Archaeologists were digging in Cave 3, near the ancient ruins of Qumran on the shore of the Dead Sea. They hoped to find more of the Dead Sea Scrolls. Bedouin shepherds had found the first ones in 1947.

The team did not find parchment or papyrus. Instead, they found two rolls of rusted copper, about 2.4 meters long in all. Ancient Hebrew words were carved into the metal.

Nobody could read them yet. The rolls were far too brittle to open. What did the metal say? And why would anyone write on copper in a cave? To find out, we must first see why this scroll is so strange.

The scroll that was not like the others

The Dead Sea Scrolls are among the most important finds of the twentieth century. They came from eleven caves near Qumran. They include the oldest surviving copies of whole books of the Hebrew Bible, community rules, and texts about the end of the world. Many scholars link them to a Jewish group called the Essenes.

Most of the scrolls were written on parchment or papyrus. In the very dry desert, these lasted two thousand years. But the scroll in Cave 3 was copper mixed with about one percent tin. Over two thousand years, it had rusted into brittle crumbs.

Its Hebrew was different too. It is closer to Mishnaic Hebrew, a plainer, everyday kind of Hebrew, than to the book language of the other scrolls. And its content was not like anything else from Qumran. So how could scholars open it without ruining it?

Cut with a fine saw

For three years, the rolls sat in the Jordan Archaeological Museum in Amman. Scholars argued about what to do. In 1955–1956, the rolls were sent to the Manchester College of Technology in England. There Professor H. Wright Baker found a way. He carefully cut them into 23 strips with a fine saw.

This let people read the text, but it destroyed the original shape of the scroll. John Marco Allegro, a scholar of the Dead Sea Scrolls, helped copy it. He published the first edition in 1960.

The researchers were stunned. This was not a prayer, a Bible commentary, or a set of community rules. It was a treasure map.

Sixty-four hiding places

Scholars call the scroll 3Q15. It lists 64 places, and 63 of them name gold or silver. Each entry usually gives a place, a direction, and a measurement in cubits. A cubit is an old unit of length, about the distance from an elbow to a fingertip. Last comes the amount of treasure.

The first entry begins like this, in one English translation: “In the fortress which is in the Vale of Achor, forty cubits under the steps entering to the east.” Other entries name cisterns, which are tanks for storing water, as well as tombs, ruins, and underground passages. The directions read like a guide for someone who already knows the land.

The amounts are huge. The scroll uses an ancient weight called the talent, which scholars estimate at about 21 to 33 kilograms. One entry lists 900 talents of silver, about 30 tons by one estimate. A guess from 1960 put the value at about a million dollars. Other writers give far bigger numbers, and nobody agrees.

Who could own so much? Scholars have four answers.

Did a small community hide it?

The first idea is the Essene theory. It says the Essenes of Qumran hid their shared wealth before the Romans destroyed their settlement around 68 CE.

But many scholars find this hard to believe. Could a small group that lived simply have collected so much silver and gold? That is a big problem for this idea.

Scholars date the scroll anywhere from about 25 CE to 135 CE. Many say about 70 CE. That date points to a much larger place with much larger riches.

The treasure of the Temple

The second idea is the Temple treasure theory. The Temple in Jerusalem was the center of Jewish religious life. Over five centuries, it gathered gifts, taxes, holy vessels, and precious furnishings. In August 70 CE, the Romans under Titus destroyed it.

The Jewish historian Josephus was there during the war. He described the Temple treasures. He also wrote that the Romans carried off huge amounts of gold and silver after the city fell.

Some scholars say the priests saw the Romans coming. They may have hidden the most holy or most portable items in the Judean wilderness. The scroll even lists “consecrated” gifts, which were items given to the Temple. If so, the Copper Scroll is the last record of the lost treasures of the Second Temple. Nobody can prove it.

A third idea moves the date later.

Rebels, fiction, and a dig

The Bar Kokhba theory dates the scroll to the revolt of 132–136 CE. In that war, Jewish rebels fought the Romans. They may have hidden valuables across the wilderness. This would also explain the later kind of Hebrew.

The fourth idea is that the treasure is not real. The scroll might be a tale of the imagination, perhaps about spiritual treasures. Experts who say it is real answer that nobody carves a fantasy on tough copper. The measurements and landmarks look like a practical document. Experts who say it is not real point to the size of the wealth. And no other evidence of it has ever turned up.

Allegro thought the treasure might be real. In 1962, he led an expedition to dig at some of the listed places. His team found nothing. Others have tried too, and nobody has found proven treasure from the scroll.

An empty list?

Why did the searches fail? There are three possible reasons. The landmarks may have been lost to time. The treasure may have been found and taken away long ago. Or it may never have existed.

A middle view says the scroll records a real attempt to hide real treasure. But the owners took it back, or others found it. The Romans destroyed Jerusalem in 70 CE, and they had good reason to hunt for hidden Jewish wealth. If soldiers or later officials took the treasure, the scroll could still be genuine. The hiding places would just be empty now.

Here is what we know. The scroll is real, and it lists treasure in 64 places. It has survived for about two thousand years. Not one item from the list has been surely identified. Here is what is open. We do not know who wrote it, when, or whether the treasure was ever real.

Today the scroll sits in the Jordan Museum in Amman, in 23 copper strips. It is a message we can read but cannot fully understand. Where is the treasure, if it was ever there?
