The Copper Scroll Treasure: The World's Oldest Treasure Map and Billions in Hidden Gold

The Copper Scroll, found in a desert cave in 1952, lists 64 treasure locations worth billions. Not a single cache has ever been found.

It was March 14, 1952. Archaeologists were digging in Cave 3 near the ancient ruins of Qumran, on the shore of the Dead Sea. They were looking for more of the Dead Sea Scrolls, which had been found in 1947. These old manuscripts had been changing what we know about ancient Judaism.

Instead of parchment or papyrus, they found something unexpected. It was two rolls of rusted copper, about 2.4 meters long in all. Ancient Hebrew words were carved into the metal.

The rolls were too brittle to open. In 1955–1956, they were finally cut into strips and unrolled at the Manchester College of Technology. The researchers were stunned. This was not a prayer, a Bible commentary, or a set of community rules, like every other Dead Sea Scroll. It was a treasure map.

The Copper Scroll listed 64 places where huge amounts of gold, silver, coins, vessels, and precious objects had been hidden. It gave directions for finding each hiding place. Nobody agrees on what that treasure would be worth today.

More than seventy years later, not one item from the scroll has been found for certain. It is often called a treasure map. And it is one of the best-known unsolved puzzles in archaeology.

The Copper Scroll is different from every other document found at Qumran. It was written on metal, not parchment. Its Hebrew is closer to Mishnaic Hebrew than to the book language of the other scrolls. Mishnaic Hebrew was a plainer, everyday kind of Hebrew.

Its content is purely practical. It has no prayers, no prophecies, and no community rules. It is just a list of hiding places, with amounts and directions.

The scroll raises questions that have split scholars. Who wrote it? When? Was the treasure real? If it was, where did all that wealth come from, and where did it go?

The Dead Sea Scrolls were first found by Bedouin shepherds in 1947. They are among the most important archaeological finds of the twentieth century. They include the oldest surviving copies of whole books of the Hebrew Bible. They also hold community rules, texts about the end of the world, and commentaries. Many scholars link them to a Jewish group called the Essenes. They have changed what we understand about Judaism in the years around the time of Jesus.

The scrolls came from eleven caves near Qumran, a ruin on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea. Most were written on parchment or papyrus. These materials lasted two thousand years in the very dry climate of the Judean desert.

But the scroll in Cave 3 was different. It was written on copper mixed with about one percent tin. Over two thousand years, the copper had rusted so badly that it turned into brittle crumbs. The two rolls could not be opened by normal means.

For three years, the copper rolls sat in the Jordan Archaeological Museum in Amman. Scholars argued over how to open them without ruining them. In 1955–1956, they were sent to the Manchester College of Technology in England. There Professor H. Wright Baker found a way. He carefully cut the rolls into 23 strips with a fine saw.

This let the text be read. But it destroyed the original shape of the scroll. John Marco Allegro, a scholar of the Dead Sea Scrolls, helped copy the text and published the first edition in 1960. It was unlike anything else from Qumran.

The amounts of precious metal in the scroll are huge. It uses an ancient weight called the talent. Scholars estimate a talent at about 21 to 33 kilograms, so the total weight is uncertain. One entry lists 900 talents of silver, about 30 tons by one estimate. Other entries list vessels, holy gifts, and even other scrolls. A 1960 guess put the value at about a million dollars. Other writers give far bigger figures.

The sheer size of the wealth has led some scholars to say the treasure must be symbolic or from a legend. They ask how any real community could have owned so much. Others reply that the amounts, though vast, are possible for the Temple of Jerusalem. For centuries the Temple received taxes and gifts from Jewish communities across the ancient world.

Scholars call the Copper Scroll 3Q15. It has Hebrew text cut into the copper. It lists 64 places, and 63 of them name gold or silver.

Each entry usually has a place and a direction, such as “under the steps entering to the east.” It also gives a measurement in cubits. A cubit is an old unit of length, about the distance from an elbow to a fingertip. Last comes the amount of treasure.

A few entries are followed by Greek letters. Nobody is sure what they mean. They may be numbers or markers.

The places are spread across the Judean wilderness and the land around it. Many name landmarks such as cisterns, reservoirs, tombs, ruins, and underground passages. A cistern is a tank for storing water. Some places could be found today. Others are annoyingly vague or point to things that disappeared long ago. The directions read like a guide for someone who already knows the land.

The first entry begins like this in one English translation: “In the fortress which is in the Vale of Achor, forty cubits under the steps entering to the east.” It goes on to name a money chest with its contents. Another entry begins: “In the gutter which is in the bottom of the (rain-water) tank.”

Who wrote the scroll, and when? These are some of the most argued questions in Dead Sea Scrolls research. Its Hebrew is closer to Mishnaic Hebrew than to the book Hebrew of the other scrolls. Scholars date it anywhere from about 25 CE to 135 CE. Many say about 70 CE. There are four main theories.

The first is the Essene theory. It says the Essene community at Qumran hid their shared wealth before the Romans destroyed their settlement around 68 CE. But many scholars find it hard to believe that a small, simple-living group could have collected so much.

The second is the Temple treasure theory. It says the scroll records where priests hid treasures of the Second Temple of Jerusalem. They would have escaped with them when the Romans under Titus besieged the city in 70 CE. It would explain the huge amounts of gold and silver. It would also explain the “consecrated” gifts, which were items given to the Temple.

The third is the Bar Kokhba theory. It dates the scroll to the Bar Kokhba Revolt (132–136 CE). In that war, Jewish rebels fought the Romans and hid valuables across the Judean wilderness. That would explain the later kind of Hebrew and the care taken to record the hiding places.

The fourth is the symbolic theory. It says the treasure is not real. The scroll would be a text of the imagination, perhaps about spiritual treasures.

The Copper Scroll has sent many treasure hunters to the desert. John Marco Allegro, who published the first edition, thought the treasure might be real. In 1962, he led an expedition to dig at some of the listed places. His team found nothing.

Others have tried too. None has found proven treasure from the scroll. The failures suggest three things. The landmarks may have been lost to time. The treasure may have been found and taken away centuries ago. Or the treasure may never have existed.

The big question is whether the treasure was ever real. The experts are deeply split. On one side are the “realists.” They say someone took great care to carve the text on tough metal, not fragile parchment. That suggests the information was too important to lose. Nobody carves a fantasy on copper, they say. The directions, with measurements in cubits and named landmarks, look like a practical document.

On the other side are the “skeptics.” They point out that the amounts of gold and silver are enormous, perhaps too large to believe. And no other evidence of such wealth has ever turned up.

A middle view says the scroll describes a real attempt to hide real treasure. But the treasure was later taken back by its owners, or found by others long ago. The Romans destroyed Jerusalem in 70 CE. They had every reason to hunt for hidden Jewish wealth.

If Roman soldiers took the treasure, or later Byzantine or Islamic officials, or local treasure hunters, the scroll could still be genuine. The hiding places it names would simply be empty now.

One famous theory ties the scroll to the fate of the Second Temple. The Romans under Titus destroyed it in August 70 CE. The Temple was the center of Jewish religious life. It held vast wealth built up over five centuries: gifts, tithes, holy vessels, and precious furnishings.

The Jewish historian Josephus was there during the war. He described the Temple treasures. He also wrote that the Romans carried off huge amounts of gold and silver after the city fell.

But some scholars say the Temple priests saw the Romans coming. They may have hidden part of the treasure, the most holy or most portable items, in the Judean wilderness before the city fell. If so, the Copper Scroll may be the last record of the lost treasures of the Second Temple. That would matter a great deal to history. But nobody can prove it.

The Copper Scroll is both exciting and frustrating. It lists huge amounts of gold and silver in 64 places in the Holy Land. And it has survived for about two thousand years.

Yet after seven decades of searching, not one item on its list has been surely identified. It may record a real treasure hidden as Jerusalem fell, and never recovered. It may describe wealth that was found long ago by people who left no record. It may be a symbolic text that never matched the real world. Or it may be something we have not yet understood.

What is certain is that the Copper Scroll is one of the most puzzling objects ever found. It is a message from the ancient world that we can read but cannot fully understand. It sits in the Jordan Museum in Amman. Its 23 copper strips hold directions to wealth that may or may not exist. The places may or may not be findable. The writers are people nobody can name.

The scroll can be read, but it is still unsolved. Where is the treasure, if it was ever there?
