Cleopatra's Lost Tomb: Where Is Egypt's Last Queen Buried?

It was August, 30 BC, in Alexandria, Egypt. Cleopatra VII, the last pharaoh of Egypt, died. She was a clever queen, and ancient writers say she spoke many languages. She had charmed Julius Caesar and Mark Antony. She had ruled Egypt for twenty-one years and presented herself as the living form of the goddess Isis.

The ancient writers say she died by her own hand. How she did it is still debated. The Roman who had beaten her would soon rename himself Augustus. He later gave orders that she be buried with dignity.

The Greek historian Plutarch wrote a little over a century after her death. He said Octavian “gave orders that her body should be buried with that of Antony in splendid and regal fashion.” The Roman historian Suetonius agreed. He wrote that Augustus “allowed them both the honour of burial.” He also ordered that “the mausoleum which they had begun should be finished.”

So the queen and her love were laid to rest together, as they had wished. Their tomb was somewhere in or near Alexandria. This shining city on the Mediterranean Sea had been the center of the Greek-speaking world for three centuries.

Then, at some point in the centuries that followed, the tomb vanished. No trace of it has ever been found. No ancient writer tells us exactly where it stood.

The grave of two of the most famous people of the ancient world has been lost for more than two thousand years. One was a queen whose name stands for beauty, power, and sadness. The other was a Roman general who was one of the three most powerful men in Rome.

The mystery of Cleopatra’s tomb is more than a missing grave. It shows how a great city of the ancient world was lost. Alexandria was a marvel of Greek-style civilization. It had the famous Library of Alexandria and the tall Pharos Lighthouse, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. It also had a royal quarter of palaces, temples, and monuments.

But Alexandria was built on soft mud from the Nile delta. Over the centuries, earthquakes, tsunamis, and sinking land pulled whole parts of the city under the sea. Much of the royal quarter now lies under the sea, with sediment on top of it. That includes the island of Antirhodos, where ancient writers place Cleopatra’s palace.

These facts come from the underwater archaeologist Franck Goddio, who has surveyed the bay of Alexandria for decades. The same forces that destroyed the royal quarter may have destroyed or buried Cleopatra’s tomb. But the tomb may still be out there, waiting to be found.

The search for her final resting place has become one of the most passionate and disputed quests in archaeology. It has brought exciting finds under the sea and bitter arguments among scholars. It also brought a Dominican lawyer turned archaeologist named Kathleen Martinez. She has staked her career on a theory that many Egyptologists doubt. An Egyptologist is an expert on ancient Egypt.

To see why the tomb matters, we should know the woman who lies in it. Cleopatra VII Philopator (69–30 BC) was the last active ruler of the Ptolemaic Kingdom of Egypt. A general of Alexander the Great founded that dynasty after Alexander died in 323 BC.

The Ptolemies were Greek. For nearly three centuries, they ruled Egypt from Alexandria. The court spoke Greek, while the Egyptian people spoke their own language.

Cleopatra stood out even in this mixed-up royal family. She was the first Ptolemaic ruler to learn the Egyptian language. She presented herself as the new birth of the goddess Isis, the Egyptian mother goddess linked with magic, fertility, and rebirth.

She was, by all accounts, very clever. Ancient writers say she spoke many languages. She was also a sharp politician who moved through the dangerous world of Roman politics with great skill.

Her partnership with Mark Antony, one of the three most powerful men in Rome, was both a love story and a political team. It was a threat to the Roman Republic. Together they controlled the eastern half of the Mediterranean. Antony had his Roman legions, and Cleopatra had huge wealth and the riches of Egypt, the breadbasket of the ancient world.

Their plans put them in direct conflict with Octavian, Julius Caesar’s adopted heir. He controlled the western half of the Roman world. The final clash was the Battle of Actium on September 2, 31 BC. Octavian’s fleet, led by his general Agrippa, beat the combined fleets of Antony and Cleopatra off the west coast of Greece.

The defeat was decisive. Antony and Cleopatra fled to Alexandria. Over the next months, Octavian’s forces closed in, and their world fell apart. Antony heard a false report that Cleopatra was dead, and he fell on his sword.

In the most famous version of the story, Cleopatra let an asp, an Egyptian cobra, bite her. The cobra was a symbol of divine royalty. The ancient sources do not agree on how she died. Some say an asp bite, some say poison, and some say both. The ancient writers agree that she chose death over being paraded through Rome in Octavian’s victory march.

People have argued about her death for more than two thousand years. The most famous account is by Plutarch. It says the asp was brought to her hidden under figs and leaves. Plutarch writes only that “it is said” this happened. The cobra was sacred to the goddess Wadjet, so some scholars think her death was meant to show she was divine.

Some modern experts doubt this story. Cobra venom may cause a slow and painful death. That may not match the ancient reports that she died quickly.

The Roman historian Cassius Dio says no one knows for sure how she died. He writes that some people said an asp was brought in a water jar. Others said she scratched her arm with a poisoned hairpin. The truth may never be known. Plutarch says two small marks were seen on her arm, but no snake was seen in the room.

Asp or poison, her death was a carefully staged final act. She was a queen who understood the power of symbols and stories. She died as she had lived, on her own terms.

Our knowledge of the tomb comes almost entirely from a few ancient writers. None of them gives an exact place. The most important is Plutarch, in his Life of Antony. He wrote it around AD 100 or a little later, so more than a century after the events.

Plutarch says Cleopatra built a tomb that was very “lofty and beautiful” near a temple of Isis. In her last days, she went back and forth between her palace and the unfinished tomb. He says she brought the most valuable royal treasures there, such as gold, silver, emeralds, pearls, ebony, ivory, and cinnamon. He says Octavian let Cleopatra and Antony be buried together “in splendid and regal fashion.”

The Roman historian Suetonius agrees. In his Life of Augustus, he says Augustus allowed them both the honour of burial and ordered the tomb finished. Cassius Dio wrote in the early third century AD. He adds that the tomb’s “upper part next to the roof was not yet fully completed” when Cleopatra died. He says both were “embalmed in the same fashion and buried in the same tomb.”

The geographer Strabo wrote about Alexandria in the time of Augustus. He and, later, John, Bishop of Nikiu, are said to place the royal palace on the island of Antirhodos in the eastern harbor. Neither gives the exact place of the tomb.

Together these sources suggest the tomb stood in or near the royal quarter, close to the palace and the harbor. The “temple of Isis” is a strong clue. Cleopatra linked herself closely with Isis, so a tomb near an Isis temple would fit her religious image.

But none of the sources gives a map point or a street name. And the Alexandria they describe is gone. The royal quarter, the palace, the temples, and the harbors have all been destroyed, rebuilt, sunk, or buried. The modern city of Alexandria has more than five million people. It sits on top of the ancient city like a layer cake of history.

The most dramatic news about ancient Alexandria has come from Franck Goddio, a French underwater archaeologist. He has surveyed the eastern harbor since the 1990s. His team at the European Institute for Underwater Archaeology (IEASM) has mapped and dug up the sunken ruins of the royal quarter, including Antirhodos, where Cleopatra’s palace stood.

The finds are amazing. There are huge stone columns, pavements, statues, and building bases on the seabed. They lie under the water and mud, and many are well preserved.

Among the finds are the remains of what may be the Timonium. This was a retreat that Mark Antony built for himself near the harbor. It was named after Timon of Athens, a Greek who hated people. The find suggests parts of the royal quarter survive on the seabed. So the tomb, if it was in this area, may survive underwater too.

The French archaeologist Jean-Yves Empereur has worked on his own in Alexandria’s waters. He has found huge stone blocks that may come from the Pharos Lighthouse. But neither Goddio nor Empereur has found any sign of Cleopatra’s tomb in the underwater ruins.

The challenge is huge. The harbor is vast, and the layers of mud are deep. A tomb may lie deep, so it would be especially hard to detect under the seabed. On land, the problem is just as hard. The modern city sits right on top of the ancient one. Digging in the areas that have not sunk is extremely difficult. Much of the ancient city lies under streets and buildings, and under the rubbish of two thousand years of people living there.

The loss of ancient Alexandria is one of history’s best-known cases of a city going under water. The city stood on a narrow strip of land between the Mediterranean and Lake Mariout (Mareotis). The royal quarter lay along the eastern harbor.

Over the centuries, a series of earthquakes and tsunamis hit, including a terrible tsunami in AD 365. The land sank and the sea crept in. The island of Antirhodos, where Cleopatra’s palace stood, slipped beneath the waves.

When Goddio began his surveys in the 1990s, the royal quarter lay hidden under water and a layer of sediment. His team has found palace foundations, harbor works, and hundreds of objects from the Ptolemaic and Roman periods.

These finds confirm that Cleopatra’s Alexandria was not just destroyed. It was swallowed by the sea. The tomb may have shared that fate. If it lay in the royal quarter, it could now be under the Mediterranean, kept safe in the silt but invisible to ordinary digging.

The underwater search has made wonderful finds. But the most disputed search for the tomb has taken place on land. The site is Taposiris Magna, about 45 kilometers (30 miles) west of Alexandria.

The driving force is Dr. Kathleen Martinez, a Dominican archaeologist and criminal lawyer. She gave up a law career to search for the tomb. She began digging at Taposiris Magna in 2005, hoping to find Cleopatra’s tomb.

Her thinking was like a detective’s. She studied the ancient sources and Cleopatra’s personality and her way of planning. She decided that a careful queen would not have chosen the royal quarter of Alexandria. Martinez argues that priests may have buried the couple in secret, without Roman approval.

Taposiris Magna means “Great Tomb of Osiris.” It is a temple complex of the Ptolemaic age, linked to the god Osiris. The choice of gods matters. Osiris was the god of death and rebirth. Isis was his wife, and Cleopatra identified herself with her.

Plutarch wrote that Cleopatra built her tomb near a “temple of Isis.” Taposiris Magna is a temple of Osiris, and Isis was his wife. Martinez’s idea is that Cleopatra expected defeat. She thinks the body was carried in secret to a hidden burial room in or under the temple. There she and Antony would lie together forever, in a place holy to the gods she worshiped.

Her team has made a series of finds that keep the theory alive, even as many Egyptologists stay doubtful. In the late 2000s, they found tombs of Egyptian nobles with ten mummies. Some mummies were once covered in gold leaf. There were also coins with Cleopatra’s face.

In 2022, they announced a tunnel about 1,300 meters (4,300 feet) long. It was partly flooded and ran toward the sea, about 40 feet underground. It held jars and pottery from the Ptolemaic period.

In 2025, Martinez worked with Robert Ballard, the man who found the Titanic. With a team using sonar, she announced a sunken ancient port several miles offshore. Ballard said the tunnel pointed directly at it. Martinez thinks this link to the sea could have been used to move a queen’s body in secret. The tomb itself has not been found.

Still, many Egyptologists and archaeologists doubt that Cleopatra’s tomb is at the site. The best-known critic is Zahi Hawass, the former Egyptian Minister of State for Antiquities Affairs and one of the most influential archaeologists in the world. In 2019, he said he did not believe Martinez’s idea. He said the Egyptians never buried people inside a temple.

Critics say the coins prove little. Cleopatra’s coins were used in Egypt in her time. Finding them at a Ptolemaic site does not show that she was buried there.

The mummies are interesting, but they are not royal burials. They seem to belong to Egyptian nobles, not pharaohs or queens. So far, nothing found there is known to name Cleopatra or Antony.

The tunnels are fine engineering. Critics say they may have had other uses, not burial. Many archaeologists think Cleopatra was buried in Alexandria, in or near the royal quarter, as the ancient writers say. They think the tomb was destroyed or sunk by the same earthquakes that destroyed the royal palace and the lighthouse.

Cleopatra’s tomb is not the only great burial mystery in Alexandria. The city was also the final resting place of Alexander the Great. His body was brought to Egypt after he died in 323 BC. It was placed in a grand tomb that became one of the most famous places of pilgrimage in the ancient world. A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy or special place.

Roman emperors, including Augustus, Caligula, and Caracalla, are recorded as visiting Alexander’s tomb. Ptolemaic rulers were also buried in Alexandria.

Writers as late as the Middle Ages said they had seen it. The last claimed visit was in 1610, and the stories are hard to check. No one today knows where Alexander the Great is buried. The same forces that erased his tomb may have erased Cleopatra’s too.

The comparison is sobering. If the tomb of the most famous conqueror in history can vanish in Alexandria, it is no surprise that a queen’s tomb did the same.

The search for Cleopatra’s tomb is one of the most exciting quests in archaeology. It joins the grandeur of ancient history with the newest tools for exploring underwater. The ancient writers tell us that Cleopatra and Mark Antony were buried together in a tomb near a temple of Isis. It most likely stood in or near Alexandria.

But the Alexandria they knew has largely vanished. It sank into the Mediterranean, lies under the modern city, or was destroyed by earthquakes and tsunamis. Goddio’s finds in the harbor show a sunken world of palaces and temples. They show that parts of the royal quarter survive under the waves. Meanwhile, Martinez’s digs at Taposiris Magna have turned up tunnels, mummies, coins, and a sunken port that keep her disputed theory alive.

Neither search has yet found proof that would end the debate. The tomb may lie under the mud of Alexandria’s eastern harbor, out of reach of today’s tools. It may lie hidden in a secret room beneath the Osiris temple at Taposiris Magna. It may have been destroyed long ago, its stones carried off for new buildings, its treasures stolen, and its bones scattered.

Or it may be found tomorrow, by a team of divers or a radar survey that sees underground, or by pure luck. That would rank among the greatest finds in archaeology. Until then, Cleopatra’s tomb stays where it has been for more than two thousand years: lost and silent. Where did they lay the last queen of Egypt?
