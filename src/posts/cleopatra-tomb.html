Cleopatra's Lost Tomb: Where Is Egypt's Last Queen Buried?

An order given in Alexandria

It was August, 30 BC, in Alexandria, Egypt. Cleopatra VII, the last pharaoh of Egypt, had died. She had ruled for twenty-one years. Ancient writers say she spoke many languages, and she had charmed both Julius Caesar and Mark Antony. Antony was dead too.

The Roman who had beaten them was named Octavian. He would soon rename himself Augustus. The Greek writer Plutarch tells us what he ordered. Octavian “gave orders that her body should be buried with that of Antony in splendid and regal fashion.”

So the queen and her love were laid in a tomb together. And then, some time in the centuries that followed, that tomb vanished. Nobody has ever found it. Not one ancient writer says exactly where it stood. How can the grave of the most famous queen of Egypt be lost? Let us first meet the woman who was buried in it.

The queen who learned Egyptian

Cleopatra VII Philopator lived from 69 to 30 BC. She belonged to the Ptolemies, a Greek family that ruled Egypt for nearly three centuries. A general of Alexander the Great had founded the dynasty after Alexander died in 323 BC. The court in Alexandria spoke Greek, while the Egyptian people spoke their own language.

Cleopatra was different. She was the first Ptolemaic ruler to learn Egyptian. She also presented herself as the new birth of Isis, the Egyptian mother goddess of magic and rebirth.

Her partner, Mark Antony, was one of the three most powerful men in Rome. Together they ruled the eastern half of the Mediterranean. But Octavian, Julius Caesar’s adopted heir, held the west. In 31 BC, his fleet beat theirs at the Battle of Actium, off the coast of Greece. Octavian’s forces then closed in on Alexandria.

The ancient sources do not agree on how Cleopatra died. Plutarch says it is said that an asp, an Egyptian cobra, was brought to her under figs and leaves. Cassius Dio says no one knows for sure. Some people said an asp came in a water jar. Others said a poisoned hairpin was used. All the writers agree on one thing. She chose death over being paraded through Rome in Octavian’s victory march.

That is the woman. Now we must ask how we know about her grave.

Three writers and a sunken city

Our knowledge of the tomb comes from a few ancient writers. The most important is Plutarch, in his Life of Antony. He wrote it around AD 100 or a little later, more than a century after the events.

Plutarch says Cleopatra built a tomb that was “lofty and beautiful,” near a temple of Isis. In her last days, she moved the royal treasures there, such as gold, silver, pearls, and ivory. The Roman writer Suetonius adds that Augustus ordered the tomb to be finished. Cassius Dio says its upper part, next to the roof, was not yet complete when she died. He says both were buried in the same tomb.

These clues suggest a place near the royal palace and the harbor. The temple of Isis fits too, because Cleopatra linked herself so closely with that goddess. But no writer gives a map point or a street name. And the city they describe is gone. So where did Alexandria go?

Alexandria was built on soft mud from the Nile delta. Over the centuries, earthquakes and tsunamis hit it, including a terrible tsunami in AD 365. The land sank and the sea crept in. Whole parts of the royal quarter slid beneath the waves. That includes the island of Antirhodos, where ancient writers place Cleopatra’s palace.

Today the modern city has more than five million people. It sits on top of the ancient one like a layer cake of history. Digging on land is extremely hard, because the old city lies under streets, buildings, and two thousand years of rubbish.

So the tomb may have sunk, too. But if it sank, it may still be there. Someone had to go and look.

Divers in the harbor

That someone was Franck Goddio, a French underwater archaeologist. Since the 1990s, his team has surveyed the eastern harbor of Alexandria. They found huge stone columns, pavements, statues, and building bases on the seabed. Many are well preserved under the water and mud.

His team also found palace foundations, harbor works, and hundreds of objects from the Ptolemaic and Roman periods. Among the finds may be the Timonium, a retreat that Antony built near the harbor. Another French archaeologist, Jean-Yves Empereur, found huge blocks that may come from the Pharos Lighthouse. The lighthouse was one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

These finds show that Cleopatra’s Alexandria was not simply destroyed. The sea swallowed it, and parts survive. But neither Goddio nor Empereur has found any sign of the tomb. The harbor is vast, and the layers of mud are deep. A tomb could lie very deep and be hard to detect.

So one search goes on under the water. Another goes on, far from the sea, in the desert.

A lawyer with a theory

The driving force behind that search is Dr. Kathleen Martinez. She is a Dominican criminal lawyer turned archaeologist. She gave up law to look for the tomb. In 2005, she began to dig at Taposiris Magna, about 45 kilometers (30 miles) west of Alexandria.

She studied the sources and Cleopatra’s way of planning. She decided that a careful queen would not choose the royal quarter. Martinez argues that priests may have buried the couple in secret, without Roman approval.

The name Taposiris Magna means “Great Tomb of Osiris.” Osiris was the god of death and rebirth, and Isis was his wife. So Martinez thinks the body was carried to a hidden room in or under the temple.

Her team has made finds that keep the idea alive. In the late 2000s, they found tombs of nobles with ten mummies, some once covered in gold leaf. They also found coins with Cleopatra’s face. In 2022, they announced a tunnel about 1,300 meters (4,300 feet) long. It was partly flooded, ran toward the sea, and held jars from the Ptolemaic period. In 2025, Martinez worked with Robert Ballard, the man who found the Titanic. They announced a sunken ancient port several miles offshore. Ballard said the tunnel pointed directly at it.

But the tomb itself has not been found. And many experts are not convinced.

Why many experts say no

The best-known critic is Zahi Hawass, the former Egyptian Minister of State for Antiquities Affairs. In 2019, he said he did not believe the idea. He said the Egyptians never buried people inside a temple.

Critics add three more points. Cleopatra’s coins were used all over Egypt in her time, so finding them proves little. The mummies seem to belong to nobles, not to a queen. And the tunnels may have had other uses. So far, nothing found there is known to name Cleopatra or Antony.

Many archaeologists think she lies in Alexandria, as the ancient writers say. They think the same earthquakes that sank the palace destroyed her tomb. Is that so strange? Another famous tomb in the same city shows how it can happen.

Alexander the Great was also buried in Alexandria. His body was brought to Egypt after he died in 323 BC. His tomb became a place of pilgrimage, which is a journey to a holy or special place. Roman emperors, including Augustus, Caligula, and Caracalla, are recorded as visiting it.

Some writers as late as the Middle Ages said they had seen it. The last claimed visit was in 1610, but those stories are hard to check. Today no one knows where Alexander lies. If the tomb of a great conqueror can vanish in Alexandria, a queen’s tomb can vanish too.

Lost and silent

So what do we know? Ancient writers say that Cleopatra and Antony were buried together, in a tomb near a temple of Isis. It most likely stood in or near Alexandria. Goddio’s finds show a sunken world of palaces and temples. Martinez’s digs have turned up tunnels, mummies, coins, and a sunken port.

What is open? Nobody has found proof that ends the debate. The tomb may lie under the mud of the eastern harbor. It may lie in a secret room beneath the temple at Taposiris Magna. Or it may be gone for good. It could be found tomorrow, by divers or by a radar survey that sees underground.

Until then, the grave stays where it has been for more than two thousand years: lost and silent. Where did they lay the last queen of Egypt?
