The Princes in the Tower: England's Most Enduring Royal Mystery

It was the summer of 1483, in London. Two boys went into the Tower of London. One was twelve years old, and the other was only nine. They vanished from the best-guarded building in England.

The older boy was Edward V, the newly named king. His younger brother was Richard, Duke of York. Their uncle, Richard, Duke of Gloucester, had placed them in the Tower to get ready for Edward’s coronation, the crowning ceremony.

The coronation never happened. In June 1483, leading nobles and townsmen said the boys were illegitimate. That means they said the boys were not the lawful children of the king. Parliament later made this a law, called the Titulus Regius. Their uncle took the throne as King Richard III.

The boys were the sons of King Edward IV and Elizabeth Woodville. They were the rightful heirs to the English crown. And they were never seen in public again.

Their disappearance is one of the best-known unsolved mysteries in English history. For more than five hundred years, historians, novelists, playwrights, and amateur detectives have argued. Who killed the princes? Were they killed at all? What really happened inside the Tower walls that summer?

The Princes in the Tower is more than a whodunit. It shows how power worked in medieval England. The throne was the greatest prize. Rival families settled their fights with blood. And the fate of two children could change the history of a whole nation.

The story inspired William Shakespeare’s most chilling villain. It split historians into passionate camps. In 2012 it flared up again, when the remains of Richard III himself were found under a car park in Leicester. After five centuries, the mystery is still not solved.

The chain of events began on April 9, 1483. King Edward IV died suddenly at the age of forty, after twenty-two years on the throne. Nobody knows for sure why he died. Stroke and pneumonia are two guesses. His death caused an instant crisis.

His eldest son and heir, Edward V, was only twelve. He was old enough to be king in name, but far too young to rule. Edward IV had named his trusted brother, Richard, Duke of Gloucester, as Lord Protector. A Lord Protector is a man who runs the kingdom until the young king grows up.

The boy Edward V was at Ludlow Castle in the Welsh Marches, near the border of Wales. His mother’s brother, Anthony Woodville, Earl Rivers, was looking after him. Edward’s party set out for London for the coronation. Gloucester stopped them at Stony Stratford in Northamptonshire.

Gloucester acted quickly. He arrested Rivers and several of the young king’s closest friends. In this way he took control of the king himself. He escorted the boy to London and placed him in the Tower. At first Edward was not a prisoner. He was the future king, getting ready for his crowning.

But Gloucester’s plans soon became clear. On June 16, the king’s younger brother, Richard, Duke of York, was also brought to the Tower. The reason given was to keep his brother company.

Then a group of lords and commoners met. Parliament was not officially sitting. They said that Edward IV’s marriage to Elizabeth Woodville was not valid. The reason was that Edward had earlier promised to marry Eleanor Talbot. A bishop said he knew of this promise. Parliament made it law in January 1484 as the Titulus Regius.

By this reading, both princes were bastards, which means children of parents not legally married. They could not inherit the throne. The lawful Yorkist heirs were out of the way. The path was clear for Gloucester.

On June 26, 1483, he was proclaimed king. He accepted. He was crowned King Richard III on July 6, 1483.

The last time anyone was sure the princes were alive was in the summer of 1483. Sources say the boys were seen less and less in the Tower grounds. In the end they were not seen at all.

An Italian friar named Dominic Mancini was in London that summer. He wrote that the boys were seen less and less. He also heard rumors that they were dead. He is the only writer of that time to tell the story. The Croyland Chronicle was written about three years later.

What happened next is the heart of the mystery. No writer of the time described the princes’ deaths. There is no confession and no witness. No body has been proved to be theirs. The record simply stops. The princes were alive in the Tower in the summer of 1483. By autumn, nobody saw them again.

Who was to blame? This question has caused one of the most heated debates among English historians.

Richard III is the usual suspect. He was their uncle. He had declared them illegitimate and taken their throne. So he had the clearest reason. While the princes lived, rebels could rally around them. He also ran the Tower and could reach the boys at any time. Many historians, from Tudor times to today, have thought Richard the most likely culprit.

Other suspects have been named. One is Henry Stafford, Duke of Buckingham. He was Richard’s cousin and an old ally. Later he turned against Richard and led a rebellion. He may have killed the princes on Richard’s secret orders, or on his own.

Another is Henry VII, also called Henry Tudor. He was the Lancastrian claimant who beat Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485 and started the Tudor dynasty. Some “Ricardian” historians, who defend Richard, think he was the true culprit. But this idea needs the princes to have stayed alive in Richard’s care until 1485. Most historians find that unlikely.

Some researchers even say one or both princes may have lived. They point to later men who claimed to be the princes. One was Perkin Warbeck, who said he was Richard, Duke of York. In the 1490s, he led a serious rebellion against Henry VII.

In 1674, nearly two centuries later, workmen were pulling down a staircase in the Tower of London. They found a wooden box with the bones of two children. People thought they were the princes. King Charles II had the bones placed in Westminster Abbey. The Latin words on the grave say they are the remains of Edward V and Richard, Duke of York.

In 1933, two men studied the bones. They were an archivist, Lawrence Tanner, and an anatomist, William Wright. An anatomist studies how bodies are built. A dentist, George Northcroft, also helped. They decided the bones belonged to two children of about the right ages.

But the test used the limited methods of the 1930s. Many modern experts doubt the findings. Some bones were missing, and the men started by assuming the bones were the princes. DNA, the code inside every living cell, has not been tested on these bones. The Abbey has not allowed it.

The bones may be the princes. They may be other children. Without DNA testing, nobody can be sure.

On August 22, 1485, Richard III was killed at the Battle of Bosworth Field. He was the last English king to die in battle. His body was taken to Leicester. It was buried in the church of the Greyfriars monastery.

In the 1530s, King Henry VIII closed the monasteries. Greyfriars was pulled down, and Richard’s grave was lost. For more than five centuries, nobody knew where England’s most disputed king lay.

In August 2012, a team dug up a car park in central Leicester. The dig began with the writer Philippa Langley. The archaeologist Richard Buckley of the University of Leicester led the work. Old maps suggested the Greyfriars church had stood there. They soon found a skeleton.

The skeleton had a curved spine, called scoliosis. Experts think it was not bad enough to show under clothes. Old stories said Richard had a twisted body. It also showed about ten injuries, most of them to the skull. Radiocarbon dating and DNA tests showed that this was King Richard III. DNA matched two living relatives of his family.

The news spread around the world. In March 2015, Richard was buried again, in Leicester Cathedral.

This discovery changed the debate about the princes. Philippa Langley began the Missing Princes Project. It aimed to re-check the evidence using original records, not later Tudor stories.

Langley’s work challenges the old story. She argues that some records suggest Edward V may have lived beyond 1483. She says Richard’s enemies used their disappearance to blacken his name. Some people praise her work for finding overlooked evidence. Critics say she makes too much of small, broken pieces of evidence.

The Richard III Society is a group that defends Richard. It argues that he was the victim of Tudor propaganda. This was a campaign meant to make Henry VII’s seizure of the throne look right, by painting the last king as a monster.

The Society points out that the worst stories about Richard came from the Tudor period. They include Sir Thomas More’s history and Shakespeare’s play. Their writers lived decades after the events, and they had strong reasons to make the last Yorkist king look bad.

The Princes in the Tower is the coldest of cold cases. If it was a crime at all, it happened over five hundred years ago. No forensic evidence survives. No witness was ever produced. No confession was ever written down.

Many historians think the most likely answer is that Richard III ordered the deaths of his nephews. This would remove a threat to his throne. It was a cold and practical act in a cold and practical age. Fights over the throne were common in those years.

But “most likely” is not “certain.” The mystery lives in the gap between probable and proved. Perhaps one day DNA tests on the bones in Westminster Abbey will be allowed. Perhaps new papers will shed light on the dark summer of 1483. Or perhaps the princes will remain what they have been for five hundred years. They are a question mark at the heart of English history.

Two boys walked into the Tower of London and became ghosts. Five hundred years later, their ghosts still haunt us.
