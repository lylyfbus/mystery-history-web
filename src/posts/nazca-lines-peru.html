The Nazca Lines: Ancient Desert Drawings That Are Best Seen From the Sky

A line that pointed at the sun

It was June 22, 1941, in the dry desert of southern Peru. An American professor named Paul Kosok was standing at the end of a long, straight line in the ground. He was there to study ancient irrigation systems. Those are channels that bring water to crops.

The day before had been the winter solstice. That is the shortest day of the year in that part of the world. Now the sun began to set. Kosok looked along the line and saw that it pointed straight at the sun.

He later called the desert “the largest astronomy book in the world.” What else was written in this book? To find out, we must first walk out into the Pampa de Jumana.

A desert full of pictures

Imagine that you stand on this flat, dry land. You see pebbles and dust, and not much more. Yet all around you, over a huge area, lies one of the great works of the ancient world. You cannot see it. The pictures are too big, and the ground is too flat.

There are over 800 straight lines. Some of them stretch as far as 30 miles across the desert. There are also over 300 shapes, such as triangles, rectangles, and spirals. And there are over 70 huge figures of animals and plants, among them a hummingbird, a spider, a monkey, and a condor.

The largest figures are more than 1,200 feet across. That is about as long as the Empire State Building is tall. Today we call the whole collection the Nazca Lines. The people who made them lived in the Americas before the Europeans arrived. We call such a people pre-Columbian. They worked between 500 BCE and 500 CE, long before the airplane was invented.

So how did anyone find out that the pictures were there?

Seen first from the air

The lines were in plain sight for about 2,000 years. But from the ground, the pictures were hard to see. One of the first scholars to study them was a Peruvian archaeologist named Toribio Mejía Xesspe. He saw them in the 1920s.

Then, in the 1930s and 1940s, air travel grew. Pilots flying over southern Peru began to report huge patterns and animal shapes in the desert below. Only from the sky could people see the true size of the pictures. After years of looking, Kosok stood at his line in 1941.

His work caught the attention of a German mathematician named Maria Reiche. She would spend more than 50 years studying, mapping, and protecting the lines. But before we ask why the lines were made, let us ask how.

Moving the dark stones

The way the lines were made is very simple. The ground of the Pampa de Jumana is covered with dark reddish-brown pebbles. Under them lies lighter, yellowish-white earth. The Nazca people moved the dark pebbles aside. That showed the pale soil below, and the lines appeared. They are only a few inches deep.

The lines also survived because of the climate. The Nazca desert is one of the driest places on Earth. It gets less than an inch of rain in a year. There is very little wind to wear things away. So the lines stayed in good shape for about 2,000 years.

The harder question is how the Nazca people got such exact results without seeing the designs from above. Wooden stakes have been found at the ends of some lines. Some experts think that the Nazca people used stakes and long ropes to keep the lines straight and the curves even. The writer Joe Nickell made figures with simple tools like those of the ancient people. It worked.

Now let us meet the answers to the biggest question of all: why?

Landing strips for spaceships?

In 1968, a Swiss author named Erich von Däniken published a book called Chariots of the Gods?. It sold very well. He said that the Nazca Lines were landing strips and signals for spacecraft from other planets.

Archaeologists have rejected this idea, for several reasons. The lines are shallow, and the “runways” are not smoothed for planes. There is no sign of any technology beyond what the Nazca people had. And the idea wrongly assumes that native peoples could not do clever work without help from outside.

No aliens were needed. It took careful planning, simple tools, and a lot of hard work. If we leave the spaceships behind, the older idea is the one that Kosok began.

A calendar in the sand?

The earliest and most famous theory is the calendar. It says that the lines matched the sun, moon, and stars at certain times of the year. They would have worked as a huge calendar for tracking the seasons. The solstices were part of this. An equinox, too, is a day when day and night are equal in length.

Maria Reiche gave most of her life to this idea. She lived in Nazca, near the lines, to look after them. She began mapping the figures around 1946, and she published her findings in books and papers. Her theory was that the lines matched the positions of the sun, moon, and stars.

Later researchers, such as Gerald Hawkins and Anthony Aveni, said that the evidence was not strong enough. Not all of the lines match an event in the sky. Still, her work to record and protect the lines is very important. She pushed officials to guard the site. Her efforts helped the lines win UNESCO World Heritage status in 1994.

If the lines are not a calendar, perhaps they were for walking.

Paths and prayers for rain

Another theory was supported by scholars such as Johan Reinhard and Anthony Aveni. It says that the lines were sacred paths for religious ceremonies. People may have walked the straight lines in processions. The animal figures may have been symbols or markers along the way.

A third idea comes from the place itself. This is one of the driest deserts on Earth. So perhaps the lines were part of a water cult. That means a religion that asked the gods for rain and good crops. Reinhard thought that most lines led to places where people held rituals for water. He linked the spider with rain and the hummingbird with fertility.

In this view, the lines were not tools. They were prayers, huge pleas for the most precious thing in a harsh desert. Nobody can prove it, because the Nazca people left no written records.

The mystery is not finished, and the desert is still giving us surprises.

Still speaking, still in danger

New figures keep turning up. Researchers from Yamagata University in Japan announced 143 new geoglyphs in 2019. A geoglyph is a large design made on the ground. In 2024, the same university and IBM announced 303 more figures. They were found with the help of artificial intelligence, and human researchers then checked them on the ground. They include birds, cats, monkeys, and killer whales.

The lines face dangers, even with UNESCO protection. The Pan-American Highway runs close by, and floods have damaged some places. In 2014, activists from Greenpeace put big yellow letters near the hummingbird. They left footprints and disturbed the delicate desert surface. Peru’s officials were angry. Greenpeace said sorry, and one activist was fined.

So what do we know? People made these pictures by moving dark stones, about 2,000 years ago. We do not know why. A calendar, a path, and a prayer for rain are all ideas, and none of them explains everything. Think of Kosok, standing at the end of his line as the sun went down. The line did point at the sun. But the Nazca people left us no words, and the lines themselves stay silent.
