The Nazca Lines: Ancient Desert Geoglyphs That Can Only Be Fully Seen From the Sky

Imagine standing in the dry, flat desert of southern Peru. This land is called the Pampa de Jumana. All around you, spread over a huge area, lies one of the great achievements of the ancient world. You cannot see it. The ground is too flat, and the pictures are too big.

There are over 800 straight lines, and some stretch as far as 30 miles across the desert floor. There are over 300 shapes, such as triangles, trapezoids, rectangles, and spirals. And there are over 70 huge figures of animals and plants. They include a hummingbird, a spider, a monkey, and a condor.

The largest figures are more than 1,200 feet across. That is about as long as the Empire State Building is tall. Some of the straight lines run all the way to the horizon and beyond. They are very straight for lines drawn without modern tools.

A pre-Columbian culture made these designs. That means a people who lived in the Americas before the Europeans arrived. They had no way to see their work from above. They had no machines and no written language. They made the lines between 500 BCE and 500 CE, more than 1,500 years before the airplane was invented.

For centuries, nobody on the ground could see the full pictures. Only in the 20th century, when passenger flights began, did people see the true size of what the Nazca people had made.

The Nazca Lines have inspired wonder, obsession, and plenty of wild guesses. Were they an astronomical calendar? The German mathematician Maria Reiche spent most of her life working on this idea. Were they sacred paths for religious processions, as many archaeologists now believe? Were they messages to the gods, a desperate plea for rain in one of the driest deserts on Earth?

Or, as the bestselling author Erich von Däniken famously claimed, were they landing strips for spacecraft from other planets? The lines have attracted astronauts, mathematicians, archaeologists, and pseudoscientists, which means people who claim to be scientists but do not follow real scientific methods.

The lines became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994. New figures are still being found, most recently with the help of artificial intelligence. Yet after decades of research, no single theory fully explains why the Nazca people put so much effort into designs they could never see from the ground.

The Nazca Lines were in plain sight for about 2,000 years. But the desert is so big and so flat that the patterns were almost invisible from the ground. One of the first scholars to study them was the Peruvian archaeologist Toribio Mejía Xesspe. He saw them in the 1920s.

From the ground, they looked like faint trails across the desert floor. They were curious, but they did not seem important. Then, in the 1930s and 1940s, air travel grew. Pilots flying over southern Peru started to report huge geometric patterns and animal shapes in the desert below.

The breakthrough came in 1941. An American professor named Paul Kosok, from Long Island University, studied the Nazca desert. He wanted to study ancient irrigation systems, the channels that bring water to crops.

On June 22, one day after the winter solstice, Kosok was standing at the end of one of the long straight lines. The winter solstice is the shortest day of the year in that part of the world. The sun began to set. He saw that the line pointed at the setting sun. To him, the match seemed too good to be an accident.

Kosok was excited. He called the desert “the largest astronomy book in the world.” He devoted himself to studying how the lines lined up with the sky.

His work caught the attention of a German mathematician named Maria Reiche. She would spend the rest of her life, more than 50 years, studying, mapping, and protecting the Nazca Lines. Reiche lived in Nazca, near the lines, to look after them.

She began mapping the figures around 1946. She published her findings in books and papers.

Her theory was that the lines matched the positions of the sun, moon, and stars at certain times of the year. Later researchers, such as Gerald Hawkins and Anthony Aveni, said the evidence was not strong enough. But her work to preserve and record the lines is very important. She pushed officials to protect the site. Her efforts helped the lines win UNESCO World Heritage status in 1994.

The way the Nazca Lines were made is very simple. That makes it remarkable that they lasted about 2,000 years. The surface of the Pampa de Jumana is covered with dark reddish-brown pebbles, coated with iron oxide. Below this layer lies lighter, yellowish-white earth.

The Nazca people made their designs by removing the dark pebbles from the surface. This showed the lighter soil underneath. The contrast between the dark top layer and the light layer below makes the lines visible. The lines are only a few inches deep.

The lines survive because of one of the most extreme climates on Earth. The Nazca Desert is one of the driest places on the planet. It gets very little rain, less than an inch in a year. There is also very little wind to wear things away. Together these kept the lines in good shape for about two thousand years.

Researchers have been fascinated for decades by one question. It is not how the lines were made, since the method is simple. It is how the Nazca people got such exact results on such a huge scale without seeing the designs from above.

Experiments have given a likely answer. Wooden stakes have been found at the ends of some lines. Some experts think the Nazca people used stakes and long ropes or strings to keep lines straight and curves even. A team could then copy a design onto the desert floor with good accuracy.

This method has been tested. The writer Joe Nickell made figures with simple tools like those of the ancient people. No aliens were needed. It took human cleverness, careful planning, and a lot of hard work.

The biggest debate is not how the lines were made but why. What purpose could be worth the huge effort of carving hundreds of figures and thousands of lines into one of the harshest deserts on Earth?

The earliest and most famous theory is the calendar. It says the lines matched the sun, moon, and stars at certain times of the year. They would have worked as a huge calendar for tracking solstices, equinoxes, and the seasons. An equinox is a day when day and night are equal in length. Paul Kosok saw one line point at the solstice sunset, and that started the theory. Maria Reiche spent decades mapping such alignments. But later researchers, such as Gerald Hawkins and Anthony Aveni, found the evidence too weak. Not all the lines match an event in the sky.

Another theory was championed by scholars like Johan Reinhard and Anthony Aveni. It says the lines were sacred paths used in religious ceremonies. People may have walked the straight lines in processions. The animal figures may have been symbols or markers along the way.

For a people living in one of the driest deserts, another idea is a water cult. This means the lines were part of a religion that asked the gods for rain and good crops. Reinhard thought most lines led to places where people held rituals for water. He linked the spider with rain and the hummingbird with fertility.

In this view, the lines were not practical tools. They were desperate prayers, huge pleas for the most precious thing in a harsh desert.

In 1968, the Swiss author Erich von Däniken published Chariots of the Gods?. It sold very well. Most professional archaeologists did not accept it. Von Däniken said the Nazca Lines were landing strips and signals for spacecraft from other planets.

Archaeologists have rejected this idea, for several reasons. The lines are shallow, and the “runways” are not smoothed for planes. There is no sign of any technology beyond what the Nazca people had. And the theory wrongly assumes that native peoples could not do clever engineering without outside help.

The real achievement is more remarkable than von Däniken’s fantasy. Ordinary people with simple tools and great organization made works of art so huge that they are best seen from the air.

The Nazca Lines are not a closed chapter. New figures keep turning up, often found with new technology that Maria Reiche could never have imagined. Researchers from Yamagata University in Japan announced 143 new geoglyphs in 2019. A geoglyph is a large design made on the ground.

In 2024, the same university and IBM announced 303 more figures. They were found with the help of artificial intelligence. They include birds, cats, monkeys, and killer whales. This greatly widened the known number of Nazca figures.

The AI system was trained to spot possible geoglyphs in aerial images. Then human researchers checked the findings on the ground.

Nearby are the Palpa Lines, on the hillsides next to the Nazca desert. They are less well known. In 2018, drones revealed 25 more figures there. They are linked to the Paracas culture, which came about a thousand years before the Nazca.

Even with UNESCO protection, the lines face threats. The Pan-American Highway runs close by, and floods have damaged some places. Tourism adds pressure.

In 2014, a Greenpeace protest caused anger around the world. Activists put big yellow letters near the hummingbird figure. They spelled a message about climate change. They left footprints and disturbed the delicate desert surface. Peru’s officials were angry. Greenpeace said sorry, and one activist was fined. The event showed how easily the lines can be harmed. It also showed the tension between protecting the site and raising public awareness.

The Nazca Lines are a puzzle for archaeologists. They are a message so large that it can only be seen from the sky. Yet the people who made it had no way to see it from the sky.

They are both simple and clever. They were made by moving rocks. Yet they were made with a precision and size that still fills people with awe 2,000 years later.

Were they a calendar? A temple? A prayer for rain? A vision of a shaman, a healer who speaks with spirits? A shared art project on the grandest scale imaginable? The answer may be all of these, or none of them. The Nazca people left no written records to explain their purpose. The lines themselves stay silent.

What is certain is that they are a great achievement of human civilization. Many people worked over centuries in one of the harshest places on Earth. They made something beautiful and mysterious. The Nazca Lines are still speaking. We just have not learned their language yet.
