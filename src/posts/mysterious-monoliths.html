The Mysterious Monoliths: Metal Pillars from Nowhere

It was November 18, 2020. A helicopter crew from the Utah Department of Public Safety was flying over the remote Red Rock Desert in southeastern Utah. They were on a routine job, counting wild bighorn sheep. Pilot Bret Hutchings was at the controls.

Suddenly one of the biologists on board shouted, “Whoa, whoa, whoa, turn around, turn around!” Below them, wedged into a narrow sandstone slot canyon in the Lockhart Basin, stood something that had no business being there.

It was a shining metal pillar, about 10 to 12 feet tall. It was shaped like a perfect triangular prism, with three flat sides. Its smooth stainless-steel surface reflected the Utah sun like a mirror. It was firmly planted in the red earth. There were no tracks, no building machines, and no clue how it had got there.

The crew landed and took a look. The pillar was hollow and made of metal sheets held together with rivets. It seemed to have been set up with great effort. There was no plaque and no signature. There were no marks at all to show who made it.

Within days, the discovery went viral, which means it spread across the internet very fast. Within weeks, the monolith vanished. And within months, over 200 similar metal columns had appeared around the world. They showed up on every continent except Antarctica. The world had caught monolith fever.

The monolith was found in San Juan County, Utah, about 17 miles southwest of Moab. This is the heart of the state’s famous Red Rock country. It is some of the roughest and most remote land in the continental United States. It is a maze of sandstone canyons, flat-topped hills, and desert valleys. You can reach it only by four-wheel-drive vehicle or by helicopter.

The crew looked at the monolith up close. It was a carefully made triangular prism of stainless steel or aluminum sheets, riveted along the edges. It was about 9.5 feet tall. Its surface was smooth and highly reflective, polished like a mirror. It looked very different from the red sandstone around it.

It seemed that someone had cut into the sandstone floor and set the base into the rock with cement. There were no tool marks, no litter, and no tracks. Nothing showed who had put it there or when.

Later, the Bureau of Land Management studied satellite pictures. It decided that the monolith was put up sometime between July and October 2016. That means it had stood in the desert, unnoticed, for over four years before anyone found it.

The story took a dramatic turn just nine days after the monolith became public knowledge. On November 27, 2020, a group of hikers planned to make the trip to see it the next day. That night the monolith vanished. All that was left was a small triangular mark in the sandstone and a few scattered rivets.

The Bureau of Land Management said it had not taken the structure away. Someone unknown had removed it in the dark, with tools and vehicles that left almost no trace. It looked like a well-planned operation. Someone had hauled a heavy metal pillar out of a remote slot canyon at night. They had crossed miles of rough, four-wheel-drive land to do it.

Later, two men said they did it. They were Andy Lewis and Sylvan Christensen, adventure sports fans who lived in Utah. They posted a video of the job on social media. Lewis is a professional slackliner, who walks on a thin rope, and a BASE jumper, who jumps from high places with a parachute.

He said the group took the monolith away because of the “Leave No Trace” rule of caring for nature. They argued that the viral attention was bringing hundreds of people to a fragile desert. These visitors were making roads and trails. They were also damaging the delicate living crust on the desert soil, which takes decades to grow back.

The removal was controversial. Some people praised the group for protecting nature. Others said they had destroyed a work of art. They said the group had stolen a moment that did not belong to them. The argument shows a tension at the heart of the whole monolith story. People are fascinated by a mystery, but nature needs protection.

The Utah monolith’s disappearance did not end the story. It gave it new energy. Within days, copycat monoliths began to appear around the world. Each one brought its own wave of news stories and online guessing.

The first confirmed copycat appeared on November 26, 2020, in the Neamț area of Romania. It stood on the hilltop of Batca Doamnei near the city of Piatra Neamț. It was similar in shape, but it was covered in looping, scribbled patterns. This was a deliberate change from the Utah original. It vanished after only four days, just like the short public life of the Utah monolith.

The next big appearance came in Atascadero, California, on December 2, 2020. A monolith appeared on top of Pine Mountain in a public park. This one was crudely made. It was a hollow stainless steel prism held together with rivets and silicone. It was so wobbly that visitors could push it back and forth. A group of young men toppled it and put up a wooden cross in its place. This was a strange ending, and it showed how the monoliths had stumbled into cultural arguments.

Other notable monoliths appeared in many places. There was one on the Isle of Wight in England and one on a frozen lake in Uttarakhand, India. Others stood in the streets of downtown Rotterdam in the Netherlands, in Morocco’s Sahara Desert, and on a beach in Warsaw, Poland. One even appeared on the front lawn of a Las Vegas pawn shop.

By the end of 2020, over 200 monoliths had been recorded on six continents. New ones kept appearing in 2021 and after. The monolith had grown beyond a single mystery object in the Utah desert. It had become a worldwide art movement that anyone could join.

From the moment the Utah monolith was first photographed, art critics and fans noticed something. It looked like the work of John McCracken (1934–2011). McCracken was an American sculptor in the minimalist style, which uses very simple shapes. He was known for his “plank” sculptures. These were tall, narrow, brightly colored rectangles that leaned against gallery walls or stood alone.

McCracken lived and worked in Santa Fe, New Mexico, and later in Mendocino, California. He belonged to the Light and Space movement of the 1960s and 70s. This was a West Coast cousin of East Coast minimalism. It stressed polished surfaces, factory materials, and how the viewer experiences a work.

The David Zwirner Gallery looked after McCracken’s estate, the works he left behind. At first it issued a statement that neither confirmed nor denied that the monolith was his. It said only that the artist had “dialed into the unknown” and that “he would have loved the mystery.” Later the gallery made clear that the monolith was not a verified McCracken work. But it agreed that the style was very close.

McCracken himself had written and spoken about wanting his sculptures to stand in the landscape, not just in a gallery. He once said his ideal was a plank standing in the desert, interacting with the light and space of the natural world.

The monolith also reminds many people of the film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), by Stanley Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke. In the film, a smooth black monolith appears at key moments in human evolution. It first appears to a group of apes on the African savanna. Then it is on the Moon. Finally it is in orbit around Jupiter. Each time, it marks a leap forward in intelligence or awareness.

The Utah monolith had a triangular shape, a metal surface, and a remote location. All of these echoed the famous prop from the film. Nearly every news story and social media post about the discovery made the comparison. The creator may or may not have meant the reference. Either way, the link was instant and powerful.

Who put up the Utah monolith, and why? There are many theories. The most widely accepted one is that it was a guerrilla art installation. That means secret art set up without permission. It was made by an artist or a group of artists who chose to stay anonymous.

The careful building, the clever placement, and the polished look all point to someone with art training and skill in making things. It stood unfound for over four years. That suggests patience and real commitment to the idea. It was not just a simple prank.

Putting it on public land without permission puts it in the tradition of land art. That is art made in the landscape itself. It includes Robert Smithson’s Spiral Jetty (1970), a huge earthwork sculpture on the shore of the Great Salt Lake in Utah. It also includes Nancy Holt’s Sun Tunnels (1976), also in the Utah desert.

Another theory is viral marketing. It says that the monolith was a planned publicity stunt to win attention online. It might have been for a coming film, a new product, or an art show. The timing was convenient. It came during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the world badly wanted positive news to share.

But no company, artist, or brand ever claimed the original monolith. That is unusual for a marketing campaign. Such campaigns usually aim to turn attention into brand awareness.

A more interesting idea was that the monolith was a kind of participatory performance art. In this view, the real point was not the object itself. It was the reaction it caused. People wrote news stories, searched the internet for clues, and built copycat monoliths. They argued about art, vandalism, and public land. On this reading, the monolith was not a sculpture but a mirror. It reflected the worries, wishes, and creative urges of a society in crisis.

The monolith story is about what happens when a mystery meets the internet age. The year 2020 was marked by a global pandemic, political turmoil, and people being cut off from each other. Then a shiny metal pillar appeared in the Utah desert. It offered something that was in short supply: a mystery that was pure, harmless, and fun.

It was not a tragedy, a crisis, or a political scandal. It was a simple question: who put that there? The world found the question impossible to resist.

The copycats that followed were a remarkable sight. There were over 200 monoliths on six continents. They showed how one anonymous act of creativity can inspire a worldwide wave of imitation. Each copycat honored the original, and each also made its own statement. The materials, places, and settings were all different. Some were rough and playful. Others were polished and carefully placed.

Together they made one of the largest unplanned public art shows in history. No single person planned or controlled it. It was spread out, anonymous, and organized by itself.

The Utah monolith is still the most famous of them all. It was the spark that started a global phenomenon. And its maker is still unknown. It reminds us that even in the most chaotic times, people cannot help creating and wondering.
