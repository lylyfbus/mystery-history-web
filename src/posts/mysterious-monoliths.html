The Mysterious Monoliths: Metal Pillars from Nowhere

It was November 18, 2020. A helicopter crew from the Utah Department of Public Safety was flying over the remote Red Rock Desert in southeastern Utah. They were on a routine job, counting wild bighorn sheep. Pilot Bret Hutchings was at the controls.

Suddenly one of the biologists on board shouted, “Whoa, whoa, whoa, turn around, turn around!” Below them, wedged into a narrow sandstone slot canyon in the Lockhart Basin, stood something that had no business being there.

It was a shining metal pillar, about 10 feet tall. It was shaped like a perfect triangular prism, with three flat sides. Its smooth metal surface reflected the Utah sun like a mirror. It was firmly planted in the red earth. There were no tracks, no building machines, and no clue how it had got there.

The crew landed and took a look. The pillar was hollow and made of metal sheets held together with rivets. There was no plaque and no signature. There were no marks at all to show who made it.

Within days, the discovery went viral, which means it spread across the internet very fast. Within weeks, the monolith vanished. And within months, more than 200 similar metal columns had been reported around the world. The world had caught monolith fever.

The monolith was found in San Juan County, Utah, about 17 miles southwest of Moab. This is the heart of the state’s famous Red Rock country. It is a remote and rough land, a maze of sandstone canyons, flat-topped hills, and desert valleys. Few people go there, and the way is hard.

Later reports said it was a triangular prism of stainless steel or aluminum sheets, riveted along the edges. It was about 9.5 feet tall. Its surface was smooth and highly reflective, polished like a mirror. It looked very different from the red sandstone around it.

It stood on bare rock, and its base seemed to be set into cuts made in the stone. Nothing showed who had put it there or when.

Later, people studied satellite pictures of the spot. They found that the monolith was put up sometime between 2015 and late 2016. Different studies give slightly different dates. That means it had stood in the desert, unnoticed, for about four years or more before anyone found it.

The story took a dramatic turn nine days after the monolith was found. On the night of November 27, 2020, it vanished. Only a small mark in the rock and a few bits of leftover material were left, and some rocks that seemed to mark the spot.

The Bureau of Land Management said it had not taken the structure away. Someone unknown had removed it in the dark. A photographer later said he saw four men take it apart that night and carry the pieces away.

Later, two of the men said they did it. They were Andy Lewis and Sylvan Christensen, adventure sports fans who lived in Utah. Lewis posted a video of the job online. Lewis is a professional slackliner, who walks on a thin rope, and a BASE jumper, who jumps from high places with a parachute.

They said the group took the monolith away because of the “Leave No Trace” rule of caring for nature. They argued that the viral attention was bringing crowds to a fragile desert. The Bureau of Land Management did find tire tracks where no roads exist, trampled plants, and litter left by visitors.

The removal was controversial. Some people praised the group for protecting nature. Others said they had destroyed a work of art. They said the group had stolen a moment that did not belong to them. The argument shows a tension at the heart of the whole monolith story. People are fascinated by a mystery, but nature needs protection.

The Utah monolith’s disappearance did not end the story. It gave it new energy. Within days, copycat monoliths began to appear around the world. Each one brought its own wave of news stories and online guessing.

An early copycat appeared in late November 2020 in Romania. It stood on Batca Doamnei hill near the city of Piatra Neamț. It was gone within days, just like the Utah monolith. Nobody is known to have claimed it.

The next big appearance came in Atascadero, California, on December 2, 2020. A monolith appeared on Pine Mountain. It was about 10 feet tall and looked like stainless steel. Within days, a group of men knocked it down and put up a wooden cross in its place. Video showed them shouting a religious slogan. The mayor said the town was upset. The monoliths had stumbled into cultural arguments.

Other monoliths appeared in many places, such as the Isle of Wight in England.

Over 200 monoliths have been reported around the world, and new ones kept appearing in 2021 and after. The monolith had grown beyond a single mystery object in the Utah desert. It had become a worldwide art movement that anyone could join.

From the moment the Utah monolith was first photographed, art critics and fans noticed something. It looked a lot like the work of John McCracken (1934–2011). McCracken was an American sculptor in the minimalist style, which uses very simple shapes. He was known for his “plank” sculptures. These were tall, narrow, brightly colored rectangles that leaned against gallery walls or stood alone.

His work is often linked with the West Coast art of the 1960s and 70s. It stressed polished surfaces, factory materials, and how the viewer experiences a work.

The gallerist David Zwirner, who represents McCracken’s estate, first said the monolith looked nearly identical to one of the artist’s works. Later he changed his mind. He pointed to rivets and screws that McCracken would not have used. He said it might be another artist’s tribute.

The monolith also reminds many people of the film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), by Stanley Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke. In the film, a smooth black monolith appears at key moments in human evolution. It first appears to a group of apes on the African savanna. Then it is on the Moon. Finally it is in orbit around Jupiter. Each time, it marks a leap forward in intelligence or awareness.

The film’s slab was black and flat, not a triangle. But the Utah monolith was tall, strange, and in a remote place. The pilot who found it said it felt like a scene from the film. Many news stories made the same comparison. The creator may or may not have meant the reference.

Who put up the Utah monolith, and why? There are many theories. Many people think it was a guerrilla art installation. That means secret art set up without permission. It was made by an artist or a group of artists who chose to stay anonymous.

The careful building and the polished look suggest someone with skill in making things. It stood unfound for years. That is only a guess, but it was probably not a simple prank.

Putting it on public land without permission puts it in the tradition of land art. That is art made in the landscape itself. It includes Robert Smithson’s Spiral Jetty (1970), a huge earthwork sculpture on the shore of the Great Salt Lake in Utah. It also includes Nancy Holt’s Sun Tunnels (finished in 1976), also in the Utah desert.

Another theory is viral marketing. It says that the monolith was a planned publicity stunt to win attention online. It might have been for a coming film, a new product, or an art show. The timing was convenient. It came during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the world badly wanted positive news to share.

But no company, artist, or brand has claimed the original monolith. That is unusual for a marketing campaign.

Another idea is that the monolith was a kind of participatory performance art. In this view, the real point was not the object itself. It was the reaction it caused. People wrote news stories, searched the internet for clues, and built copycat monoliths. They argued about art, vandalism, and public land. On this reading, the monolith was not a sculpture but a mirror. It reflected the worries, wishes, and creative urges of a society in crisis.

The monolith story is about what happens when a mystery meets the internet age. The year 2020 was marked by a global pandemic, political turmoil, and people being cut off from each other. Then a shiny metal pillar appeared in the Utah desert. It offered something that was in short supply: a mystery that was pure, harmless, and fun.

It was not a tragedy, a crisis, or a political scandal. It was a simple question: who put that there? The world found the question impossible to resist.

The copycats that followed were a remarkable sight. There were more than 200 monoliths around the world. They showed how one anonymous act of creativity can inspire a worldwide wave of imitation. Each copycat honored the original, and each also made its own statement. The materials, places, and settings were all different. Some were rough and playful. Others were polished and carefully placed.

Together they made a huge, unplanned public art show. No single person planned or controlled it. It was spread out, anonymous, and organized by itself.

The Utah monolith is still the most famous of them all. It was the spark that started a global phenomenon. And its maker is still unknown. It reminds us that even in the most chaotic times, people cannot help creating and wondering.
