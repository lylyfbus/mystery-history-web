The Mysterious Monoliths: Metal Pillars from Nowhere

“Turn around, turn around!”

It was November 18, 2020. A helicopter crew from the Utah Department of Public Safety was flying over the remote Red Rock Desert in southeastern Utah. They were on a routine job, counting wild bighorn sheep. Pilot Bret Hutchings was at the controls.

Suddenly one of the biologists on board shouted, “Whoa, whoa, whoa, turn around, turn around!” Below them, wedged into a narrow sandstone slot canyon in the Lockhart Basin, stood something that had no business being there.

It was a shining metal pillar, about 10 feet tall. Its smooth surface reflected the Utah sun like a mirror. There were no tracks, no building machines, and no clue how it had got there.

Within days, the discovery went viral, which means it spread across the internet very fast. Within weeks, the pillar vanished. Let us look at the pillar first.

A mirror in the red rock

The monolith was found in San Juan County, Utah, about 17 miles southwest of Moab. This is the heart of the state’s famous Red Rock country. It is a remote and rough land, a maze of sandstone canyons, flat-topped hills, and desert valleys. Few people go there, and the way is hard.

The crew landed and took a look. The pillar was hollow. It was a triangular prism, which means it had three flat sides. It was made of metal sheets held together with rivets, and it was about 9.5 feet tall. There was no plaque and no signature. Nothing showed who had made it.

It stood on bare rock, and its base seemed to be set into cuts made in the stone. It looked very different from the red sandstone around it.

Later, people studied satellite pictures of the spot. They found that the monolith was put up sometime between 2015 and late 2016. Different studies give slightly different dates. That means it had stood in the desert, unnoticed, for about four years or more. And then, nine days after the crew found it, it was gone.

The night it vanished

On the night of November 27, 2020, the monolith disappeared. Only a small mark in the rock and a few bits of leftover material were left. The Bureau of Land Management said it had not taken the structure away. A photographer later said he saw four men take it apart that night and carry the pieces away.

Later, two of the men said they did it. They were Andy Lewis and Sylvan Christensen, adventure sports fans who lived in Utah. Lewis posted a video of the job online.

They said they took the monolith away because of the “Leave No Trace” rule of caring for nature. They argued that the viral attention was bringing crowds to a fragile desert. The Bureau of Land Management did find tire tracks where no roads exist, trampled plants, and litter left by visitors.

The removal was controversial. Some people praised the group for protecting nature. Others said they had destroyed a work of art. People are fascinated by a mystery, but nature needs protection. That is the tension at the heart of the story. And the vanishing did not end it.

Copies around the world

The Utah monolith’s disappearance gave the story new energy. Within days, copycat monoliths began to appear around the world. Each one brought its own wave of news stories and online guessing.

An early copycat appeared in late November 2020 in Romania. It stood on Batca Doamnei hill near the city of Piatra Neamț. It was gone within days, just like the Utah monolith. Nobody is known to have claimed it.

The next big appearance came in Atascadero, California, on December 2, 2020. A monolith appeared on Pine Mountain. It was about 10 feet tall and looked like stainless steel. Within days, a group of men knocked it down and put up a wooden cross in its place. The mayor said the town was upset.

Other monoliths appeared in many places, such as the Isle of Wight in England. Over 200 have been reported around the world. So who made the first one? Perhaps its shape holds a clue.

A film, or a sculptor?

From the first photographs, art fans noticed something. The monolith looked a lot like the work of John McCracken (1934–2011). McCracken was an American sculptor in the minimalist style, which uses very simple shapes. He was known for his “plank” sculptures. These were tall, narrow, brightly colored rectangles that leaned against gallery walls or stood alone.

The gallerist David Zwirner, who represents McCracken’s estate, first said the monolith looked nearly identical to one of the artist’s works. Later he changed his mind. He pointed to rivets and screws that McCracken would not have used. He said it might be another artist’s tribute.

The monolith also reminds many people of the film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), by Stanley Kubrick and Arthur C. Clarke. In the film, a smooth black monolith appears at key moments in human evolution. The film’s slab was black and flat, not a triangle. But the pilot who found the Utah monolith said it felt like a scene from the film. The creator may or may not have meant the reference.

These are clues about the style, but they do not name a maker. What about a reason?

Was it an advertisement?

One theory is viral marketing. It says that the monolith was a planned publicity stunt to win attention online. It might have been for a coming film, a new product, or an art show. The timing was convenient. It came during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the world badly wanted positive news to share.

But the theory has a problem. No company, artist, or brand has claimed the original monolith. That is unusual for a marketing campaign. A stunt that nobody takes credit for does not sell anything.

It also stood unfound for years. If it was an advertisement, it waited a long time for its audience. So many people look for a different answer.

A secret gift of art?

Many people think it was a guerrilla art installation. That means secret art set up without permission. They think it was made by an artist or a group of artists who chose to stay anonymous.

The careful building and the polished look suggest someone with skill in making things. That is only a guess, but it was probably not a simple prank.

Putting it on public land without permission puts it in the tradition of land art. That is art made in the landscape itself. It includes Robert Smithson’s Spiral Jetty (1970), a huge earthwork sculpture on the shore of the Great Salt Lake in Utah. It also includes Nancy Holt’s Sun Tunnels (finished in 1976), also in the Utah desert.

Another idea is that the real point was not the object itself. It was the reaction it caused. People wrote news stories, searched the internet for clues, and built copycat monoliths. They argued about art, vandalism, and public land. On this reading, the monolith was a mirror for a society in crisis.

The pillar that nobody owns

The year 2020 was marked by a global pandemic, political turmoil, and people being cut off from each other. Then a shiny metal pillar appeared in the Utah desert. It offered something that was in short supply: a mystery that was harmless. It was a simple question: who put that there?

Here is what we know. A metal prism stood in a Utah canyon for years before a helicopter crew saw it. Two men said they took it away. More than 200 copies were reported around the world. Here is what is open. The creator never claimed it, and the identity is unknown.

Think again of the helicopter turning back over the red rock. The crew saw a mirror in the desert, and the world still wonders who left it there.
