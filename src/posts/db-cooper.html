D.B. Cooper: The Only Unsolved Skyjacking in American History

It was the afternoon of Wednesday, November 24, 1971, in Portland, Oregon. It was the day before Thanksgiving, one of the busiest travel days of the year. A quiet man in a dark business suit walked up to the Northwest Orient Airlines counter at Portland International Airport. He paid cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle, Washington. He gave his name as Dan Cooper.

A few hours later, this man would carry out one of the most puzzling crimes in American aviation history. It was a skyjacking, which means taking over a plane by force. The FBI says it is still the only unsolved case of air piracy in the history of commercial flying.

Cooper boarded Flight 305. It was a Boeing 727 carrying 36 passengers, counting Cooper, and 6 crew members. Cooper handed a note to flight attendant Florence Schaffner. It read: “Miss, I have a bomb in my briefcase and want you to sit by me.”

He showed her what looked like a bomb: red sticks, wires, and a battery. He demanded $200,000 in negotiable U.S. currency and four parachutes. He got exactly what he asked for.

Then, somewhere over the dark, rainy forests of the Pacific Northwest, he opened the rear stairway of the plane. The plane was flying at 10,000 feet, in freezing air. He stepped out into the night and vanished.

No body was ever found. In the first search, nobody found any sure trace of the man or the money.

The early 1970s were a very different time for air travel. Airport security hardly existed. There were no metal detectors, no baggage X-rays, and no real identity checks. A person could walk onto an airliner with almost anything hidden on their body or in their bags.

Hijackings were shockingly common. In 1972 alone, there were 31 hijackings in U.S. airspace. Airlines usually chose to cooperate with hijackers and keep things calm.

In this setting, Dan Cooper boarded Flight 305 on that rainy November afternoon. The Boeing 727 was an ideal plane for a skyjacking. It was one of the few jets of that time with a rear stairway, called an “aft airstair,” that could be lowered in flight.

The stairway was made for emergency exits and for boarding at airports without jet bridges. It would prove vital to Cooper’s plan.

Cooper took seat 18E, a window seat in the last row at the back. He was described as a white man in his mid-40s. He was between 5 feet 10 inches and 6 feet tall, with an average build. He wore a dark business suit, a white shirt, a black tie with a mother-of-pearl tie clip, and a raincoat.

After takeoff, he ordered a drink and lit a cigarette. Soon after, he handed a note to Florence Schaffner. At first she ignored it. She thought it was a lonely businessman’s phone number. Cooper leaned in and told her to read it.

The note, in capital letters, said he had a bomb. Schaffner showed it to another flight attendant, Tina Mucklow.

Cooper then opened his briefcase. Inside were red tubes that looked like dynamite, wires, and a battery. He told Schaffner to write down his demands and take them to the captain.

His demands were precise and businesslike. He wanted $200,000 in negotiable U.S. currency. He wanted four parachutes. And he wanted a fuel truck waiting in Seattle to refuel the plane.

The message went to the cockpit. Captain William Scott passed the demands to the airline. The airline agreed to pay, as airlines then usually did, to protect passengers and crew. The FBI was told while the plane was still in the air.

Flight 305 landed at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport at about 5:45 PM. Cooper let the passengers go, and two flight attendants too. The money was delivered in a bag. It was 10,000 twenty-dollar bills, and the FBI had written down the serial numbers. The four parachutes were handed over as well.

Cooper kept four crew members. They were Captain Scott, First Officer Bob Rataczak, Flight Engineer H.E. Anderson, and flight attendant Tina Mucklow. Mucklow stayed with Cooper through the ordeal.

The money came from a Seattle bank. Before it was delivered, the FBI recorded the serial numbers. Most of the serial numbers began with the letter “L,” which showed they came from the San Francisco Federal Reserve Bank.

Cooper also got four parachutes, two main and two reserve. One of the reserve parachutes was a training chute and could not work.

After refueling in Seattle, Flight 305 took off again at about 7:40 PM. It headed southeast with a flight plan for Mexico City. The plane flew at no more than 10,000 feet, much lower than the usual 30,000 feet or more. The wing flaps were set at 15 degrees, and the landing gear stayed down. That kept the speed low, at about 100 knots (115 mph). The cabin was not pressurized. The plan was to stop in Reno to refuel on the way to Mexico City.

Cooper seemed to know a lot about the 727 and how the stairway worked. He also seemed to know the land below. He reportedly said, “Looks like Tacoma down there.” This suggested he knew the geography of the Pacific Northwest.

At about 8:13 PM, the crew felt a sudden change in cabin pressure and the tail of the plane tipped upward. These were signs that the rear stairway had been opened. No one saw Cooper jump. He went out into the dark, rainy, freezing night.

The weather was harsh. Below lay thick forest and rough mountains.

Investigators thought he jumped over southwest Washington, in the area of the Lewis River. The plane flew on to Reno, Nevada, and landed safely. Cooper was gone.

The FBI began a huge manhunt. Its code name was NORJAK, short for Northwest Hijacking. Ground teams, helicopters, and small planes searched the wilderness for weeks. They found nothing. There was no body, no parachute, no money, no clothing, and no trace of Dan Cooper.

The hijacking changed aviation security for good. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ordered that Boeing 727 planes get a new device. It became known as the “Cooper vane.” It is a spring-loaded part that stops the rear stairway from being lowered in flight.

The hijacking also helped change airport security across the United States. In early 1973, the FAA began requiring airlines to search all passengers and bags. This was an answer to the wave of hijackings in those years. The days of walking onto a plane with no checks were over.

The FBI’s NORJAK investigation was one of its longest. The case file grew to 66 volumes. Over 45 years of work, the FBI looked at more than 1,000 suspects.

The FBI studied the black clip-on necktie that Cooper left behind. It was sold only at J.C. Penney stores. Investigators tested it for DNA. DNA is the code in every person’s cells that is different for each person. It has never been matched to a suspect. The cigarette butts Cooper smoked on the flight were also collected.

Case agent Larry Carr believed that Cooper probably did not survive the jump. The weather was extreme, and the land below was rough and thickly wooded. Some former agents thought he lived. In July 2016, the FBI announced that it was moving its resources to other cases. That ended the active investigation after 45 years. The FBI still accepts tips about the parachutes and the money.

The most important physical evidence was found not by the FBI but by an eight-year-old boy. In February 1980, Brian Ingram was playing on the sandy bank of the Columbia River at Tena Bar, west of Vancouver, Washington. He found three bundles of rotting twenty-dollar bills.

It was reported to the authorities. The FBI confirmed that the serial numbers matched the ransom money. The bills totaled about $5,800, which was 290 bills. They were weathered and partly rotted, but still identifiable.

The find thrilled the investigators. The FBI searched the Tena Bar area. No more money was found.

The discovery raised more questions than it answered. How did the money end up on a sandbar on the Columbia River, miles from the guessed jump zone?

Some investigators thought river currents had carried the money downstream over the nine years since the hijacking. Others thought Cooper had survived the jump and lost the money there. The exact timeline could not be worked out.

In November 1978, a placard with instructions for lowering the rear stairs of a Boeing 727 was found near a logging road east of Castle Rock, Washington. It may have come from Cooper’s plane.

In 1986, Ingram was allowed to keep $2,760 of the found money. In 2008, he sold 15 of the bills at auction. Together they sold for more than $37,000, many times their face value.

Over the decades, the FBI looked at more than 1,000 suspects. None was ever proven to be Cooper. One of the best known was Richard Floyd McCoy Jr. In April 1972, just five months after Cooper, he did almost the same thing. He hijacked a Boeing 727 and used the same rear-stairway method. McCoy was caught, convicted, and sentenced to 45 years in federal prison. He escaped in 1974 and was killed in a gunfight with FBI agents. Despite the likeness, the FBI does not consider McCoy a suspect.

Duane Weber was a man who had spent time in many prisons. He died in 1995. His wife, Jo, said he had confessed on his deathbed. The FBI looked into Weber, but his fingerprints did not match. His DNA did not match samples from the tie.

Kenneth Christiansen was a former Northwest Airlines worker. He bought a house with cash the year after the hijacking. The FBI says he is not a prime suspect. Robert Rackstraw was a pilot and former paratrooper. A 2016 book named him as the hijacker. The FBI had cleared him in 1979, and he died in 2019.

Other suspects included William Gossett and Sheridan Peterson, and many more. In every case, the evidence was weak or unclear. The man who called himself Dan Cooper has never been identified.

The hijacker never actually called himself “D.B. Cooper.” He gave his name as “Dan Cooper” when he bought his ticket. The “D.B.” came from a media mistake. A reporter wrote the wrong initials, and the name stuck. “D.B. Cooper” became one of the most famous false names in American crime history.

The real name “Dan Cooper” may itself have come from a Belgian comic book hero of the same name. The FBI has said this might be the source. Did the hijacker choose the name on purpose, as a nod to the comic? Or did he simply pick a plain, common name? Nobody knows.

The crew described Cooper as calm, polite, and well-spoken. He showed no nervousness, even while handling what he said was a bomb.

His careful planning, calm manner, and total disappearance have made D.B. Cooper a lasting American folk hero. To many people, he is a criminal who outsmarted the system and got away clean.

D.B. Cooper remains one of the most fascinating mysteries in American history. It has the daring of a Hollywood thriller and the frustration of a cold case that will not be solved. The evidence is mixed and tempting. The ransom money found on the Columbia River sandbar proves that at least some of the cash survived. But where it was found raises more questions than it answers.

There was no body. So either Cooper survived the jump and escaped, or his remains were lost in the huge, rugged wilderness of the Pacific Northwest. It is so thickly forested and remote that bodies can go unfound for decades.

Some investigators think Cooper probably did not survive, given the harsh conditions. But this is only a theory, not a conclusion. The U.S. Attorney’s case file is held at the National Archives in Seattle.

Did Cooper die in the wilderness that November night? Did he live out his days under a false name? Or did he simply disappear into the legend that carries his false name? We may never know. And perhaps that is just how he wanted it.
