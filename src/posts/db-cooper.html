D.B. Cooper: The Only Unsolved Skyjacking in American History

A ticket to Seattle

It was the afternoon of Wednesday, November 24, 1971, in Portland, Oregon. It was the day before Thanksgiving, one of the busiest travel days of the year. A man in a dark business suit walked up to the Northwest Orient Airlines counter at Portland International Airport. He paid cash for a one-way ticket to Seattle, Washington. He gave his name as Dan Cooper.

A few hours later, this man would carry out a skyjacking, which means taking over a plane by force. The FBI says that it is still the only unsolved case of air piracy in the history of commercial flying.

To understand how he could do it, we must look at the time. In the early 1970s, airport security hardly existed. There were no metal detectors and no baggage X-rays. In 1972 alone, there were 31 hijackings in U.S. airspace. Airlines usually chose to cooperate with hijackers and keep things calm.

The note in seat 18E

Cooper boarded Flight 305, a Boeing 727. It carried 36 passengers, counting Cooper, and 6 crew members. He took seat 18E, a window seat in the last row. Later he was described as a white man in his mid-40s, of average build, between 5 feet 10 inches and 6 feet tall. He wore a dark suit, a white shirt, a black tie, and a raincoat.

After takeoff, he ordered a drink. Then he handed a note to flight attendant Florence Schaffner. It read: “Miss, I have a bomb in my briefcase and want you to sit by me.” At first she ignored it. Cooper leaned in and told her to read it.

He opened his briefcase. Inside were red tubes that looked like dynamite, wires, and a battery. He asked for $200,000 in negotiable U.S. currency and four parachutes. He also wanted a fuel truck waiting in Seattle.

The airline agreed, as airlines then usually did, to protect the passengers and crew. The FBI was told while the plane was still in the air. And what happened on the ground in Seattle?

The plane that flew low

Flight 305 landed at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport at about 5:45 PM. Cooper let the passengers go, and two flight attendants too. The money came in a bag. It was 10,000 twenty-dollar bills, and the FBI had written down the serial numbers. Most began with the letter “L,” which showed that they came from the San Francisco Federal Reserve Bank. The four parachutes arrived too, two main and two reserve. One reserve was a training chute that could not work.

Cooper kept four crew members: Captain William Scott, First Officer Bob Rataczak, Flight Engineer H.E. Anderson, and flight attendant Tina Mucklow.

The plane took off again at about 7:40 PM, with a flight plan for Mexico City. It was to stop in Reno to refuel on the way. The plane flew at no more than 10,000 feet, much lower than the usual 30,000 feet or more. The wing flaps were set at 15 degrees, the landing gear stayed down, and the cabin was not pressurized. That kept the speed low, at about 100 knots (115 mph).

The 727 was ideal for his plan. It had a rear stairway, called an “aft airstair,” that could be lowered in flight. At about 8:13 PM, the crew felt a sudden change in cabin pressure, and the tail tipped upward. These were signs that the stairway had been opened. Nobody saw Cooper jump.

A hunt that found nothing

Investigators think that he jumped over southwest Washington, in the area of the Lewis River. Below lay thick forest and rough mountains. The plane flew on to Reno and landed safely.

The FBI began a huge manhunt with the code name NORJAK, short for Northwest Hijacking. Ground teams, helicopters, and small planes searched for weeks. There was no body, no parachute, no money, and no trace of Dan Cooper.

The case file grew to 66 volumes. Over 45 years, the FBI looked at more than 1,000 suspects. Cooper had left behind a black clip-on necktie, sold only at J.C. Penney stores. Investigators tested it for DNA. DNA is the code in every person’s cells, and it is different for each person. It has never been matched to a suspect.

Case agent Larry Carr believed that Cooper probably did not survive the jump. Some former agents thought that he lived. In July 2016, the FBI moved its resources to other cases, ending the active investigation. But one clue had turned up long before that.

Money on a sandbar

In February 1980, an eight-year-old boy named Brian Ingram was playing on the sandy bank of the Columbia River at Tena Bar. He found three bundles of rotting twenty-dollar bills. The FBI confirmed that the serial numbers matched the ransom money. The bills totaled about $5,800, which was 290 bills.

The FBI searched the area, but no more money was found. How did the cash end up on a sandbar, miles from the guessed jump zone? Some investigators thought river currents had carried it downstream over nine years. Others thought Cooper had survived the jump and lost the money there. The exact timeline could not be worked out.

In 1986, Ingram was allowed to keep $2,760 of the money. In 2008, he sold 15 of the bills at auction for more than $37,000. A placard with instructions for lowering the rear stairs of a 727 was also found, in November 1978, near a logging road east of Castle Rock, Washington. It may have come from Cooper’s plane.

Suspects who do not fit

None of the 1,000 suspects was ever proven to be Cooper. Let us look at four.

Richard Floyd McCoy Jr. did almost the same thing in April 1972. He hijacked a Boeing 727 and used the rear stairway. He was caught and sentenced to 45 years in prison. He escaped in 1974 and was killed in a gunfight with FBI agents. Despite the likeness, the FBI does not consider him a suspect.

Duane Weber died in 1995. His wife, Jo, said that he had confessed. But his fingerprints and DNA did not match. Kenneth Christiansen was a former Northwest Airlines worker who bought a house with cash the year after the hijacking. The FBI says that he is not a prime suspect. Robert Rackstraw was a pilot and former paratrooper. A 2016 book named him, but the FBI had cleared him in 1979. He died in 2019.

In every case, the evidence was weak or unclear. And even the name that we use for the man is not quite right.

A name by mistake

The hijacker never called himself “D.B. Cooper.” He gave his name as “Dan Cooper” when he bought his ticket. The “D.B.” came from a media mistake. A reporter wrote the wrong initials, and the name stuck.

“Dan Cooper” may itself have come from a Belgian comic book hero of the same name. The FBI has said that this might be the source. Did he choose the name as a nod to the comic? Or did he just pick a common name? Nobody knows.

The hijacking changed aviation security. The FAA ordered a spring-loaded part, known as the “Cooper vane,” that stops the rear stairway from being lowered in flight. In early 1973, it began requiring airlines to search all passengers and bags.

The man who stepped out

So what do we know? A man who called himself Dan Cooper took $200,000 and stepped out of a plane at night. Part of the money was found in 1980. The FBI never identified him.

What is open? There was no body. Either Cooper survived and escaped, or the huge, rugged forest of the Pacific Northwest hid his remains. Some investigators think that he probably did not survive. But that is only a theory. The U.S. Attorney’s case file is held at the National Archives in Seattle.

Did Cooper die in the wilderness that night? Did he live out his days under a false name? We may never know. The crew described a calm, polite man who showed no nervousness. Where did he go?
