Salem Witch Trials: How Mass Hysteria and Fear Executed 20 People in Colonial America

It was the winter of 1692. In the strict, religious settlement of Salem Village, two young girls began to scream. Salem Village is now the town of Danvers, Massachusetts. Its people were Puritans, Christians who followed very strict rules.

The girls were Betty Parris, age nine, and her cousin Abigail Williams, age eleven. Betty was the daughter of the local minister, Reverend Samuel Parris, and Abigail was his niece. The girls twisted their bodies into shapes that seemed impossible. They said that invisible hands were pinching and biting them.

They babbled words that made no sense. They threw furniture around and crawled under tables, making sounds that frightened the adults. The village doctor, William Griggs, could find nothing wrong with their bodies. His answer was simple, and it led to disaster. He said the girls were bewitched.

Within weeks, fear had taken hold of the whole community. This was the start of the Salem Witch Trials. More than two hundred people were arrested. Thirty were found guilty, and twenty were put to death. Nineteen were hanged, and one was crushed under stones.

It is still the deadliest witch hunt in American history. It shows what can happen when fear, religion, and the courts join together and turn on people.

The trials were not one single event. They were a chain of accusation, confession, and judgment that lasted nine months. Each case led to more, and each round was fiercer than the last.

To the Puritans of Salem Village, the Devil was as real as the soil under their feet. Any bad luck could be a sign of evil magic. It might be a failed crop, a sick child, or a dead cow.

The village was also split by quarrels. Rich traders and poor farmers fought over land. Rival church groups fought for power. And the village stood at the edge of a wild forest, where the Puritans truly believed that demons lived.

So when the girls began to act in this frightening way, the village did not ask why. It asked who. And once people started to ask, they could not stop.

The first accusations came from a small group of girls and young women, mostly between eleven and twenty years old. After Betty Parris and Abigail Williams, the best-known accuser was Ann Putnam Jr. She was twelve, and she came from one of the most powerful families in the village. Mercy Lewis, Mary Walcott, and Elizabeth Hubbard joined her.

Their shaking and visions, and their claims of being bitten by unseen spirits, were treated as evidence, not illness. The judges pushed the girls for answers, and the girls gave names. Those names showed something about Salem that the village did not want to see.

The first three people accused were Tituba, Sarah Good, and Sarah Osborne. They were among the weakest people in the village. Tituba was an enslaved woman in the house of Reverend Parris, from the Caribbean or from Africa. Sarah Good was a poor beggar who went around the village asking for food and shelter. Sarah Osborne was an old woman who had shocked her neighbors. She had married her servant and had tried to take the land meant for her sons.

They were not chosen by chance. Puritan society already distrusted the poor, the stranger, and anyone who did not follow the rules.

Tituba was questioned very hard, and it is almost certain that she was threatened with violence. She confessed to witchcraft and named other women in the village as witches working with her. Her confession was vivid and detailed, and it was almost certainly forced out of her. It became the pattern for every accusation that followed.

In May 1692, the governor of Massachusetts, Sir William Phips, set up a special court. It was called the Court of Oyer and Terminer. Its head was Chief Justice William Stoughton. He was completely sure that witchcraft was real and that the accused were guilty. Under Stoughton, the court worked with frightening speed.

The most argued rule was called spectral evidence. A specter is a ghost or spirit. A witness could say that the spirit of an accused person had come to them in a dream, a vision, or a waking hallucination. The witness could say that the spirit had hurt them, pinched them, bitten them, or choked them.

A girl could say she dreamed that the ghost of Rebecca Nurse was choking her. The court then treated that dream as evidence that Nurse was a witch. The problem was clear even at the time. Nobody could check this kind of evidence, or prove it false. Without it, most of the guilty verdicts would have been impossible.

The executions began on June 10, 1692. The first to be hanged was Bridget Bishop, a tavern owner in her early sixties. She was known for her independent spirit and her bright, showy clothes.

On July 19, five more women were hanged. They were Sarah Good, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin, Rebecca Nurse, and Sarah Wildes. Sarah Good went to her death with a curse. She told Reverend Nicholas Noyes: “I am no more a witch than you are a wizard, and if you take away my life, God will give you blood to drink.”

Rebecca Nurse was a grandmother of 71. She was so devout that even some of her accusers doubted she was guilty. The verdict against her shocked the community. It began to turn opinion against the trials.

On August 19, six more people were put to death. One was George Burroughs, a former minister. On the gallows, the wooden frame for hangings, he said the Lord’s Prayer perfectly. Most people believed that a witch could not do that.

The crowd was stunned for a moment. Then Cotton Mather, a powerful Puritan minister, spoke to them. He reminded them that the Devil could sometimes let a witch seem holy. Burroughs was hanged.

The most terrible death was not a hanging at all. Giles Corey was an 81-year-old farmer, accused of witchcraft together with his wife Martha. In court, he refused to say either “guilty” or “not guilty.” So he was given an ancient English punishment with a French name, peine forte et dure. He was laid on the ground with a board across his chest. Stones were piled on the board, one by one, for two days.

Corey had a reason to stay silent. If he answered the court, the government would take his property. If he refused, his farm would pass to his family. Several accounts say that his last words to the men pressing him were simply: “More weight.” He died on September 19, 1692. His wife Martha was hanged three days later.

On September 22, 1692, the last group of condemned prisoners was hanged. They were Martha Corey, Mary Easty, Alice Parker, Mary Parker, Ann Pudeator, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott, and Samuel Wardwell.

Before she died, Mary Easty wrote a letter to the court. She did not beg for her own life. With great dignity, she asked the court to spare the lives of others. Her letter has been called one of the most moving papers in American legal history.

By now, the panic was wearing itself out. Too many people had been accused, and too many well-known citizens had been named. The accusations had begun to reach the highest levels of Massachusetts society. It is reported that even the governor’s wife, Lady Mary Phips, was named.

The turning point came in October 1692. Governor Phips was alarmed by the growing outcry. He closed the Court of Oyer and Terminer and set up a new Superior Court. This court refused to accept spectral evidence. Dreams and visions could no longer be proof of witchcraft, and guilty verdicts dropped sharply.

By May 1693, Phips had pardoned all the people still accused of witchcraft and let them out of prison. To pardon someone is to free them from punishment. The crisis was over.

But the harm was great. More than 200 people had been accused, 30 found guilty, and 20 put to death. Families were destroyed. Property was taken. Children were left without parents. The community carried the scar forever.

The apologies came slowly, over three centuries. In 1697, Judge Samuel Sewall stood in the Old South Church in Boston while his public apology was read aloud.

In 1706, Ann Putnam Jr. stood before her congregation. She had been one of the fiercest accusers and had named sixty-two people. She said sorry in public. She said she believed she had been “instrumental” in the shedding of innocent blood. That means she had helped to cause it.

The lawmakers of the Massachusetts colony passed laws in 1711 and 1722 to cancel the guilty verdicts. But the state of Massachusetts did not formally apologize until 1957, 265 years after the trials. That year it also cleared the names of some of the victims. More victims were not formally cleared until 2001.

The last name was cleared in 2022. Governor Charlie Baker formally pardoned Elizabeth Johnson Jr., a woman found guilty of witchcraft in 1693. Every earlier act had somehow missed her name. It took three hundred and thirty years to clear every name.

In a way, the Salem Witch Trials did not end in 1693. They return every time a community turns on its own people in a panic. They return every time a government uses fear to excuse injustice. They return every time an accusation counts as proof and a denial counts as guilt.

In 1953, the writer Arthur Miller saw this when he wrote the play The Crucible. It tells the story of Salem, but it points at Miller’s own time. Senator Joseph McCarthy was holding hearings to hunt for Communists in America. “We are what we always were in Salem,” Miller wrote, “but now we are everywhere.” People have used the comparison for the Red Scares, the Satanic Panic of the 1980s, and the waves of anger on social media in the twenty-first century.

Salem was not caused by moldy bread, or by the Devil, or by one cruel judge. It was caused by what causes every witch hunt. That is fear, made louder by people in power, aimed at the weak, and excused by belief.

Today a memorial stands in Salem. Stone benches are carved with the names of the dead and the dates they were killed. The words on the memorial say: “Here were hanged the innocent.” They are simple words for the most tangled crime in early American history. And they leave us with a question. Could fear do the same thing again?
