Salem Witch Trials: How Mass Hysteria and Fear Executed 20 People in Colonial America

It was the winter of 1692. In the strict, religious settlement of Salem Village, two young girls began to scream. Salem Village is now the town of Danvers, Massachusetts. Its people were Puritans, Christians who followed very strict rules.

The girls were Betty Parris, age nine, and her cousin Abigail Williams, age eleven. Betty was the daughter of the local minister, Reverend Samuel Parris, and Abigail was his niece. Accounts say the girls twisted their bodies into strange shapes. They said that invisible hands were pinching and biting them.

They spoke words that made no sense. They crawled under tables and made sounds that frightened the adults. The village doctor, William Griggs, could find nothing wrong with their bodies. His answer was simple, and it led to disaster. He said the girls were bewitched.

Within weeks, fear had taken hold of the whole community. This was the start of the Salem Witch Trials. More than two hundred people were accused. Thirty were found guilty, and twenty were put to death. Nineteen were hanged, and one was crushed under stones. At least five more died in jail.

It is the best known witch hunt in American history. It shows what can happen when fear, religion, and the courts join together and turn on people.

The trials were not one single event. They were a chain of accusation, confession, and judgment that lasted more than a year. Each case led to more, and each round was fiercer than the last.

To the Puritans of Salem Village, the Devil was as real as the soil under their feet. Any bad luck could be a sign of evil magic. It might be a failed crop, a sick child, or a dead cow.

The village was also split by quarrels over land and over who would lead the church. It stood at the edge of a wild forest, where some Puritans feared that demons lived.

So when the girls began to act in this frightening way, the village did not ask why. It asked who. And once people started to ask, they could not stop.

The first accusations came from a small group of girls and young women, mostly between eleven and twenty years old. After Betty Parris and Abigail Williams, the best-known accuser was Ann Putnam Jr. She was twelve, and she came from an important family in the village. Mercy Lewis, Mary Walcott, and Elizabeth Hubbard joined her.

Their shaking and visions, and their claims of being bitten by unseen spirits, were treated as evidence, not illness. The officials pushed the girls for answers, and the girls gave names. Those names say a lot about the village.

The first three people accused were Tituba, Sarah Good, and Sarah Osborne. They were among the weakest people in the village. Tituba was an enslaved woman in the house of Reverend Parris. Records call her “Indian.” Where she came from is not certain, but some historians think the West Indies. Sarah Good was a poor woman who asked neighbors for food and shelter. Sarah Osborne was a woman who had upset her neighbors. Church members did not see these three as part of the village.

Historians think they were not chosen by chance. They were outsiders, and they were easy to blame.

Tituba was questioned very hard. She confessed to witchcraft and named other women as witches. Later she said that Reverend Parris had beaten a confession out of her. Her confession helped to start more accusations.

In May 1692, the governor of Massachusetts, Sir William Phips, set up a special court. It was called the Court of Oyer and Terminer. Its head was Chief Justice William Stoughton. He strongly believed that witchcraft was real. The court worked with frightening speed.

The most argued rule was called spectral evidence. A specter is a ghost or spirit. A witness could say that the spirit of an accused person had come to them in a dream, a vision, or a waking hallucination. The witness could say that the spirit had hurt them, pinched them, bitten them, or choked them.

A girl could say she dreamed that the ghost of Rebecca Nurse was choking her. The court then treated that dream as evidence that Nurse was a witch. The problem was clear even at the time. Nobody could check this kind of evidence, or prove it false. Some historians say that without it, few guilty verdicts would have been possible.

The executions began on June 10, 1692. The first to be hanged was Bridget Bishop.

On July 19, five more women were hanged. They were Sarah Good, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin, Rebecca Nurse, and Sarah Wildes. One report says Sarah Good spoke to Reverend Nicholas Noyes. She said: “I am no more a witch than you are a wizard, and if you take away my life, God will give you blood to drink.”

Rebecca Nurse was an older woman, about seventy. Many people in her town believed she was innocent. Her case made some people doubt the trials.

On August 19, five more people were put to death. One was George Burroughs, a former minister. One account says that he said the Lord’s Prayer without a mistake. Many people believed that a witch could not do that.

The same account says the crowd was stunned. Then Cotton Mather, a Puritan minister, spoke to them. He said that the Devil could sometimes make a witch seem holy. Burroughs was hanged.

The most terrible death was not a hanging at all. Giles Corey was an 81-year-old farmer. He and his wife Martha were both accused of witchcraft. In court, he refused to say either “guilty” or “not guilty.” So he was given an ancient English punishment with a French name, peine forte et dure. He was laid on the ground with a board across his chest. Stones were piled on the board for two days.

Some think Corey had a reason to stay silent. If he was never found guilty, the government could not take his property. The most told story says that his last words were simply: “More weight.” He died on September 19, 1692. His wife Martha was hanged three days later.

On September 22, 1692, the last group of condemned prisoners was hanged. They were Martha Corey, Mary Easty, Alice Parker, Mary Parker, Ann Pudeator, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott, and Samuel Wardwell.

Before she died, Mary Easty wrote a letter to the court. She did not ask for her own life. She asked the court to stop killing innocent people, and to question the accusers more closely.

By now, the panic was wearing itself out. Too many people had been accused, and too many well-known citizens had been named. The accusations had begun to reach the highest levels of Massachusetts society. Even the governor’s wife, Lady Mary Phips, was named.

The turning point came in the fall of 1692. Governor Phips was alarmed by the growing outcry. He ended the Court of Oyer and Terminer on October 29. A new court met in January 1693. It sharply limited spectral evidence, and almost everyone brought before it was freed.

In May 1693, Phips gave a general pardon. To pardon someone is to free them from punishment. The crisis was over.

But the harm was great. More than 200 people had been accused, 30 found guilty, and 20 put to death. Families were destroyed. Property was taken. Children were left without parents. The community carried the scar forever.

The apologies came slowly, over three centuries. In 1697, Judge Samuel Sewall stood in church while his public apology was read aloud.

In 1706, Ann Putnam Jr. stood before her congregation. She had been one of the fiercest accusers and had named or spoken against more than sixty people. She said sorry in public. She said she feared she had been “instrumental” in bringing the guilt of innocent blood on the land. That means she had helped to cause it.

Cleared names came slowly. A Massachusetts law in 1957 cleared six people. Another law in 2001 cleared five more.

In 2022, Elizabeth Johnson Jr. was officially cleared. She had been found guilty of witchcraft in 1693, but the governor had stopped her death sentence. It took 329 years to clear her name.

In a way, the Salem Witch Trials did not end in 1693. Some people say they return when a community turns on its own people in a panic. They return when fear is used to excuse injustice. They return when an accusation counts as proof and a denial counts as guilt.

In 1953, the writer Arthur Miller saw this when he wrote the play The Crucible. It tells the story of Salem, but it points at Miller’s own time. Senator Joseph McCarthy was holding hearings to hunt for Communists in America. People have since compared Salem to other panics.

Historians do not agree on one cause. In 1976, a scientist said that a mold on rye bread made the girls ill. Most experts reject this idea. Fear, quarrels, belief, and a court that accepted weak evidence all played a part.

Today a memorial stands in Salem. It was dedicated in 1992. It has twenty stone benches, one for each person who was put to death. Each bench shows a name, how the person died, and the date. Words from the court records are carved in the walls. And they leave us with a question. Could fear do the same thing again?
