Interesting History

The Voynich Manuscript: The Most Mysterious Book in the World

A book that nobody can read

In 1912, a book dealer named Wilfrid Voynich is looking through a large collection of old manuscripts. He is a Polish-American who trades in rare old books. The collection belongs to a Jesuit college near Rome, at Frascati.

Among its books, he finds something odd. It has about 240 pages, and it is roughly the size of a modern paperback. The pages are made of vellum, a fine material made from animal skin. They are filled with small, neat writing in an alphabet that nobody on Earth can read.

Voynich bought the book from the Jesuits and brought it to America. More than a century later, it still puzzles scholars, codebreakers, and dreamers. Let us open it and look at the pages.

Odd plants and tiny bathers

The book has several sections, and each has its own kind of picture. The herbal section is the biggest. It fills about half the book. Each page shows one or two plants with some text.

The drawings are skilled and detailed. But most of the plants match no known species. Some look like mixes of leaves, flowers, and roots from different real plants.

The astronomical section shows stars, suns, and moons. Some circles with spokes look like medieval star tables. The cosmological section has complex circles and foldout pages with flower-like shapes. They may show a medieval model of the universe.

The pharmaceutical section shows roots, herbs, and medicines, with jars like those in old pharmacies. It looks most like known medieval books on herbs and medicines.

The biological section is the best known and the strangest. It shows dozens of small female figures in pools or tubs of green liquid. They are joined by pipes and channels, like a web of tubes.

People have read these pictures in many ways. Some say they show a medieval book about women’s health. Others see an alchemy diagram. Alchemy was an old art that tried to turn metals into gold and find magic cures. Others think the pictures show the human blood system.

Nobody knows which is right. And the writing does not help, because nobody can read it. Yet the writing is not as strange as it first seems.

Writing that looks like a language

The book uses an unknown alphabet of about 20 to 25 characters. They run from left to right in a smooth, confident hand. The alphabet matches no known writing system. It is not Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, or Cyrillic.

What makes the book so maddening is that it looks readable. The text is not random. Statistics show that it follows patterns of natural language. Some characters appear more often than others. Words have regular lengths. One example is Zipf’s Law, a pattern of word use found in many natural languages.

The text also has repeated phrases, and many words that appear only once in the whole book. Its word entropy is close to that of English or Latin. Entropy here means how unpredictable the words are. Studies of the handwriting suggest that more than one person wrote the book.

So is the book real? Tests say that it is old.

A real book from the Middle Ages

Radiocarbon dating is a method that measures old carbon in a material to tell its age. It showed that the vellum dates to between 1404 and 1438. So the book is a real medieval object, and the ink and paint fit the same period.

But how did it travel from then to 1912? A letter was found tucked inside the book. Using that letter and later research, scholars have traced its owners back to the early 1600s.

The letter was written in 1665 or 1666. It says that Emperor Rudolf II of Bohemia once owned the book. He lived from 1552 to 1612, and he loved alchemy and astrology. Rudolf is said to have paid 600 gold ducats for it. The letter also says some people believed the book was the work of Roger Bacon, an English thinker of the Middle Ages.

After Rudolf died, the book passed to his doctor, Jacobus Sinapius. Later it reached the Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher in Rome. After that, the trail goes cold for more than two centuries, until Voynich found it. A dealer named Hans Kraus bought it in 1961. He gave it to Yale University in 1969. Today it is kept at Yale’s Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, as MS 408.

A book like this called for the best codebreakers. How did they do?

Codebreakers who failed

The first serious modern study came in the 1920s. William Romaine Newbold, a philosophy professor at the University of Pennsylvania, claimed to have solved it. He said the text hid tiny marks that formed a secret code. It showed, he said, that Roger Bacon had used a microscope and a telescope.

Later scholars tore his claims apart. In 1931, John Manly showed that the method let a reader find almost any meaning. The tiny marks were cracks in the ink on rough vellum.

Then professional codebreakers took an interest. One was William Friedman. He led the US team that broke the Japanese PURPLE code in World War II. Later he became chief cryptologist of the US National Security Agency. Friedman gathered teams of codebreakers to study the book.

They did not crack it. Friedman himself guessed that the text might be a made-up language. Some experts still think so. Then, in the late twentieth century, computers arrived.

New tools, new claims

Computers brought new tools but no breakthrough. Studies confirmed that the text shows traits of natural language. Still, it matched no known language.

In 2014, Stephen Bax of the University of Bedfordshire published a partial reading. He said he had read ten names of plants and stars. He thought the book was about nature, in a language from the Near East or Asia.

In 2017, computer scientists Greg Kondrak and Bradley Hauer at the University of Alberta used artificial intelligence on the text. They said it was most likely Hebrew, with the letters of each word shuffled. But experts on medieval books were not convinced.

In 2018, a researcher named Ahmet Ardıç said the book was written in a language that came from Old Turkic, spelled by sound. Other scholars have criticized this claim. Nobody has produced a full translation.

Is there a simpler answer? Maybe there is nothing to read.

Was it a trick?

Some people think the book may be an elaborate medieval fake. The radiocarbon dates make it very unlikely that Voynich forged it himself. But medieval vellum does not prove that the text has meaning.

In 2003, computer scientist Gordon Rugg published a bold paper. He showed that a simple grid system could make text with the same statistics. It was called a cardan grille, a paper sheet with holes, invented around 1550, that can be used to make text that looks like language.

Rugg said a faker could have used it to make a convincing book of nonsense. He might have sold it to a rich buyer like Emperor Rudolf II.

Other experts say the match is only on the surface. They say the text has deeper patterns, so the question is still open.

A locked room with no door

Back to Frascati in 1912. Voynich held a book that looked real, old, and carefully made. More than a century later, we know much more about it. Yet it has beaten every method of decoding invented in the past hundred years.

Here is what we know. The vellum is from 1404 to 1438. The writing has patterns like a real language. The pictures follow clear sections. Here is what we do not know. Nobody can say if it says anything at all. It could be lost knowledge about medicine, plants, or the stars. Or it could be a trick played on a trusting emperor.

New researchers keep coming with stronger tools. Yale has put scans of the whole book online for free, so anyone can study every page. Machine learning programs have been trained on the text, and none has given a reading that most scholars accept.

The book does not care whether we understand it. It has waited six hundred years. It can wait six hundred more. So what does it say, if it says anything at all? Nobody knows.
