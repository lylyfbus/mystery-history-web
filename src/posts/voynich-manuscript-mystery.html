Interesting History

The Voynich Manuscript: The Most Mysterious Book in the World

In 1912, a book dealer named Wilfrid Voynich was looking through a large collection of old manuscripts. He was a Polish-American who traded in rare old books. The collection belonged to a Jesuit college near Rome, at Frascati. Among its books he found something that would puzzle scholars, codebreakers, and dreamers for more than a century.

It was a book of about 240 pages, roughly the size of a modern paperback. The pages were made of vellum, a fine material made from animal skin. They were filled with small, neat writing in an alphabet that nobody on Earth could read.

The pictures were even stranger than the writing. There were drawings of plants that match no known species. There were star diagrams that nobody has been able to explain. And there were dozens of tiny women bathing in pools of green liquid, joined by a web of tubes and pipes. It may be a medieval idea of plumbing for the human body.

The book was clearly old, carefully made, and impossible to understand. Voynich bought it from the Jesuits and brought it to America. There it caught the eye of the greatest codebreakers of the twentieth century. They included skilled codebreakers from World War II. None of them could read a single word.

Over the years, the book has been tested in many ways. Experts have used statistics, computers, language study, chemistry, and X-rays. Radiocarbon dating is a method that measures old carbon in a material to tell its age. It showed that the vellum dates to between 1404 and 1438. So the book is a real medieval object, not a modern fake. The ink and paint fit the same period.

Yet the text is still unread. It uses an unknown alphabet of about 20 to 25 characters. The writing flows with the ease of a real language. The Voynich Manuscript is often called the most mysterious book in the world.

What makes it so maddening is that it looks readable. The text is not random. Statistics show it follows patterns of natural language. Some characters appear more often than others. Words have regular lengths. There are repeats and structures that suggest grammar and meaning.

The pictures are odd, but they fall into clear sections. That suggests the book had a practical purpose, perhaps as a book of medicine or alchemy. Alchemy was an old art that tried to turn metals into gold and find magic cures.

Yet every try to match the text to a known language has failed. So has every try to find a secret code in it. Today the book is kept at Yale University’s Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library. Its number there is MS 408. It still beats the best minds in the world.

The book has several sections, and each has its own kind of picture. The herbal section is the biggest. It fills about half the book. Each page shows one or two plants with some text. The drawings are skilled and detailed. But most of the plants match no known species. Some look like mixes of leaves, flowers, and roots from different real plants.

The astronomical section shows stars, suns, moons, and zodiac signs. Some circles with spokes look like medieval star tables. The biological section is the strangest and best known. It shows dozens of small female figures in pools or tubs of green liquid, joined by pipes and channels.

People have read these pictures in many ways. Some say they show a medieval book about women’s health. Others see an alchemy diagram. Others think they show the human blood system.

The cosmological section has complex circles and foldout pages with flower-like shapes. They may show a medieval model of the universe. The pharmaceutical section shows roots, herbs, and medicines, with jars like those in old pharmacies. It looks most like known medieval books on herbs and medicines.

The book’s history reads like a thriller. A letter was found tucked inside it. Using that letter and later research, scholars have traced its owners back to the early 1600s. The letter was written in 1665 or 1666. It says that Emperor Rudolf II of Bohemia once owned the book. He lived from 1552 to 1612. He was a ruler of the Habsburg family who loved alchemy, astrology, and the occult.

Rudolf is said to have paid 600 gold ducats for the book. That was a huge sum. The letter also says some people believed the book was the work of Roger Bacon, an English thinker of the Middle Ages. After Rudolf died, the book passed to his doctor, Jacobus Sinapius.

Later it reached the Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher in Rome. He was one of the most learned men of the 1600s. He, too, did not solve it. After that, the trail goes cold for more than two centuries. Then Voynich bought it from the Jesuits in 1912. A dealer named Hans Kraus bought it in 1961. He gave it to Yale University in 1969.

The story of the attempts to crack the book is one of brilliant, stubborn, and sometimes desperate failure. The first serious modern study came in the 1920s. William Romaine Newbold, a philosophy professor at the University of Pennsylvania, claimed to have solved it. He said the text hid tiny marks that formed a secret code. It showed, he said, that Roger Bacon had used a microscope and a telescope.

Later scholars tore his claims apart. In 1931, John Manly showed that the method let a reader find almost any meaning. The tiny marks were cracks in the ink on rough vellum.

In the 1940s and 1950s, professional codebreakers took an interest. One was William Friedman. He led the US team that broke the Japanese PURPLE code in World War II. Later he became chief cryptologist of the US National Security Agency. Friedman gathered teams of codebreakers to study the book.

They did not crack it. Friedman himself guessed that the text might be a made-up language. Some experts still think so.

Computers arrived in the late twentieth century. They brought new tools but no breakthrough. Studies confirmed that the text shows traits of natural language. One is Zipf’s Law, a pattern of word use found in many natural languages. Others are how often each character appears and how long the words are. Still, the text matched no known language.

In 2014, Stephen Bax of the University of Bedfordshire published a partial reading. He said he had read ten names of plants and stars. He thought the book was about nature, in a language from the Near East or Asia. Nobody has produced a full translation.

In 2017, computer scientists Greg Kondrak and Bradley Hauer at the University of Alberta used artificial intelligence on the text. They said it was most likely Hebrew, with the letters of each word shuffled. But experts on medieval books were not convinced.

In 2018, a researcher named Ahmet Ardıç made another claim. He said the book was written in a language that came from Old Turkic, spelled by sound. Other scholars have criticized this claim.

Some people think the book may be an elaborate medieval fake. The radiocarbon dates of 1404 to 1438 make it very unlikely that Voynich forged it himself. But a medieval vellum does not prove the text has meaning.

In 2003, computer scientist Gordon Rugg published a bold paper. He showed that a simple grid system could make text with the same statistics. It was called a cardan grille, a paper sheet with holes, invented around 1550, that can be used to make text that looks like language. Rugg said a faker could have used it to make a convincing book of nonsense. He might have sold it to a rich buyer like Emperor Rudolf II.

Other experts say the match is only on the surface. They say the text has deeper patterns, so the question is still open.

The writing in the book looks like no other writing. It uses about 20 to 25 different characters. They run from left to right in a smooth, confident hand. The letters are elegant and very even from start to end. Studies of the handwriting suggest that more than one person wrote it.

The writer seems to use a script they knew well. The alphabet matches no known writing system. It is not Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, or Cyrillic.

The text has many “weird words.” These are words that appear only once in the whole book. It also has repeated phrases and formulas, which hint at some structure. Its word entropy is close to that of English or Latin. Entropy here means how unpredictable the words are.

The Voynich Manuscript is more than a puzzle. It is a challenge to how we see the medieval world. If someone reads it, it could reveal lost knowledge about medicine, plants, or the stars. The herbal section may describe real plants and their uses.

The bathing figures might shed light on medieval alchemy, medicine, or ideas about nature. Or the book could be a medieval fantasy. It might be an alchemist’s dream diary, a story in a private language, or a trick played on a trusting emperor.

Either answer would be amazing. If it is real, it may be a lost book of medieval knowledge. If it is a hoax, it is one of the longest-lasting frauds in history. It would have fooled the world’s greatest minds for more than a century.

New researchers keep coming, with ever stronger tools. Yale’s Beinecke Library has put scans of the whole book online for free, so anyone can study every page in high detail. Machine learning programs have been trained on the text. They search for patterns that people cannot see. None of this has produced a reading that most scholars accept.

The book is what it has been since 1912. It is a locked room with no door. It is a message from the medieval world that we can see but cannot hear. It is truly old and was written in an unknown script. Its pictures defy explanation. It has beaten every method of decoding invented in the past hundred years.

The best codebreakers of the twentieth century failed to read it. The most powerful computers of the twenty-first century have failed too. It sits at Yale, on about 240 pages of vellum. It is as puzzling today as when Wilfrid Voynich bought it from a Jesuit college near Rome.

The book does not care whether we understand it. It has waited six hundred years. It can wait six hundred more. So what does it say, if it says anything at all? Nobody knows.
