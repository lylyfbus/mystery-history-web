Spontaneous Human Combustion: Can People Really Self-Ignite?

A hot doorknob in St. Petersburg

On the morning of July 2, 1951, a landlady named Pansy Carpenter climbed the stairs of her building in St. Petersburg, Florida. She had a telegram to deliver to one of her tenants, a 67-year-old widow named Mary Hardy Reeser.

No one answered the door. Carpenter noticed that the doorknob was unusually hot, so she called the police. The officers who went in found a scene that they could hardly understand. Reeser’s easy chair was mostly destroyed. In its place lay a pile of gray ash.

Very little of Mary Reeser was left. And yet the room around the chair was damaged only a little. People asked a frightening question. Had a human body caught fire all by itself? Let us look at the clues together.

What the fire left alone

The strange part was not only what burned. It was what did not burn. In a crematorium, turning a body to ash takes flames of about 1,600 degrees Fahrenheit (870 degrees Celsius) for two hours or more. Yet in Reeser’s room, the fire had stayed in a small area. Plastic objects farther from the chair had softened and lost their shape, but that was all.

The police sent evidence to the FBI. The FBI said that Reeser had taken sleeping pills and had most likely fallen asleep while smoking a cigarette. The cigarette lit her nightclothes. This started what experts call the “wick effect.” We will see what that means soon.

First, we should learn that the story of Mary Reeser was not the first of its kind.

A very old idea

The idea that a human body could catch fire with no outside cause is called spontaneous human combustion, or SHC. It has been debated for more than three and a half centuries.

The earliest known written account dates to 1663. A Danish anatomist named Thomas Bartholin described a woman in Paris who was said to have turned to ashes in her bed. An anatomist is a person who studies how the body is built. The straw mattress that she lay on was said to be unharmed. Over the next centuries, many similar cases were reported in Europe and North America.

Even a famous writer used the idea. In his novel Bleak House, Charles Dickens used spontaneous human combustion to kill a character named Mr. Krook. Some critics said that this was not possible. Dickens answered that he had read published case reports. He did not change his mind.

The quarrel showed something that is still true. The idea of SHC is dramatic, but science has always doubted it.

Two more cases, and a verdict

In 1966, the death of Dr. John Irving Bentley added to the legend. He was a retired doctor, about 92 years old, who lived in Coudersport, Pennsylvania. On December 5, 1966, a meter reader came into his home and found smoke and a strange smell.

Almost nothing of Bentley was left, except part of one leg in the bathroom. The rest was a pile of ash in the basement, under a hole in the floor. But Bentley smoked a pipe, and he was known to drop hot ashes on his clothes. The coroner named burns and a lack of air as the cause.

A coroner is an official who decides how a person died. And one coroner in Ireland made a ruling that surprised the world.

On December 22, 2010, a 76-year-old man named Michael Faherty was found dead in his home in Galway, Ireland. His body was badly burned. Only the floor under him and the ceiling above were scorched. Forensic experts said that the fireplace fire did not cause the blaze. They found no sign of fuel poured on him.

In 2011, the coroner, Dr. Ciaran McLoughlin, gave a verdict of “spontaneous combustion.” The BBC said that it was the first such verdict in Ireland. Dr. McLoughlin said that the fire had been fully investigated and that he could find no better explanation.

A professor of pathology replied that some outside source of fire is always needed. The verdict was news around the world. Was SHC real, or had people read the evidence wrongly?

Idea one: a fire from inside

The first idea is the simplest to say. It says that a body can start to burn from the inside, with no spark or flame from outside. That is the idea in the name.

But there is a problem with it. Most scientists agree that alleged cases involve a missed outside source of fire. They say that no case has been proved to be a human body catching fire with no outside source.

So scientists looked for a better answer. They found one in something very ordinary, a candle.

Idea two: the body as a candle

The main scientific explanation is the wick effect. It says that a clothed human body can burn like a candle. The clothing acts as the wick. It soaks up melted body fat, which is the fuel, and pulls it up through the cloth. Cloth does this the way a paper towel pulls up water. Scientists call it capillary action.

The fat burns slowly, for several hours, and stays in one place. That helps explain why the fire does not spread far. Parts of the body far from the middle, such as the feet, may survive.

This matches the story of Mary Reeser, as the FBI saw it. A cigarette lit her nightclothes, and the fire stayed near her chair.

But does it really work? Someone had to test it.

A test with a blanket

In 1998, Dr. John de Haan did a test for the BBC science program QED. He wrapped a dead pig in a blanket in a furnished room. He lit the blanket with a match and a little gasoline. The pig burned for several hours at high heat, and only the part near it was burned to ash.

The wick effect can explain many of the usual features. The fire stays local. The ends of the body may survive. And an outside source of fire seems missing. In fact, that source is often small and common, such as a cigarette, a candle, or a fireplace ember. It can be burned up or missed during the investigation.

So the wick effect is the main answer. Yet the old fear has not gone away.

Back to the landlady’s door

Let us return to Pansy Carpenter and the hot doorknob. Here is what we know. A woman died in her chair, and a fire stayed in a small space. The FBI said that a cigarette and the wick effect explain it. Most scientists say that no case of a body burning with no outside fire has ever been proved.

Here is what is still open. In Galway, the coroner said that he could find no better explanation. And people still feel uneasy about such cases. The strange scenes stay in the mind: the pile of ash, the surviving foot, the quiet fireplace.

The wick effect explains how such fires happen. But the fear of burning from the inside is hard to shake. That is why a hot doorknob in 1951 is still talked about today.
