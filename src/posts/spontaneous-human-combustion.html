Spontaneous Human Combustion: Can People Really Self-Ignite?

Imagine you are sitting alone in your favorite armchair, maybe dozing after a meal. There is no spark and no flame. Then, the story goes, your body begins to burn from the inside. Within hours, all that is left is a pile of gray ash.

The chair under you is only partly burned. The carpet is scorched but still there. The newspapers on the table next to you are untouched, and the walls have only a thin coat of soot. No marks of fire lead away from the body. There is no sign of fuel poured on it and no sign of an outside flame. It is as though the fire came from within.

This is the idea of spontaneous human combustion, or SHC. It is one of the most lasting and disturbing mysteries in the history of crime science, which is the use of science to find out how a death happened. Doctors have written about it since the seventeenth century. It also inspired one of the most argued-over death scenes in English books.

The idea that a human body could catch fire with no outside cause has been debated for more than three and a half centuries. The earliest known written account dates to 1663. A Danish anatomist named Thomas Bartholin described a woman in Paris who was said to have turned to ashes in her bed. The straw mattress she lay on was said to be unharmed. (An anatomist studies how the body is built.)

Over the next centuries, many similar cases were reported in Europe and North America.

Charles Dickens caused an uproar. In his novel Bleak House, he used spontaneous human combustion to kill a character named Mr. Krook. Some critics said this was not possible. Dickens answered that he had read published case reports. He did not change his mind. The row showed a tension that is still with us. The idea of SHC is dramatic, but science has always doubted it.

Of all the cases, none is more famous, or more closely studied, than the death of Mary Hardy Reeser. It happened in St. Petersburg, Florida, in July 1951. On the morning of July 2, her landlady, Pansy Carpenter, came to the apartment of the 67-year-old widow. She had a telegram to deliver.

No one answered the door. Carpenter noticed that the doorknob was unusually hot, so she called the police. The officers who went in found a scene they could hardly understand. Reeser’s easy chair was mostly destroyed. In its place lay a pile of gray ash.

Reports say part of her spine, part of her left foot in a slipper, and her skull were left. One report said the skull had shrunk “to the size of a teacup.” A fire researcher who later saw the scene doubted this. The rest of her body had burned away.

What made the scene so puzzling was how much was spared. In a crematorium, turning a body to ash takes flames of about 1,600 degrees Fahrenheit (870 degrees Celsius) for two hours or more. Yet the room was damaged only a little. Plastic objects farther from the chair had softened and lost their shape. But the fire had stayed in a small area.

The St. Petersburg police were so puzzled that they called in the FBI. The FBI did its own investigation. It decided that Reeser took sleeping pills and had most likely fallen asleep while smoking a cigarette. The cigarette lit her nightclothes. This started what experts call the “wick effect.”

In 1966, the death of Dr. John Irving Bentley added to the legend. Bentley was a retired doctor, about 92 years old, who lived in Coudersport, Pennsylvania. On December 5, 1966, a meter reader came into his home and found smoke and a strange smell.

Almost nothing of Bentley was left except part of one leg in the bathroom. The rest was a pile of ash in the basement, under a hole in the floor. Bentley smoked a pipe and was known to drop hot ashes on his clothes. The coroner named burns and a lack of air as the cause.

A modern case happened on December 22, 2010, in Galway, Ireland. A 76-year-old man named Michael Faherty was found dead in his home. His body was badly burned. Only the floor under him and the ceiling above were scorched. Forensic experts said the fireplace fire did not cause the blaze. They found no sign of fuel poured on him.

The official ruling made the case famous. In 2011 the coroner, Dr. Ciaran McLoughlin, gave a verdict of “spontaneous combustion.” A coroner is an official who decides how a person died. The BBC said it was the first such verdict in Ireland. Dr. McLoughlin said the fire had been fully investigated and he could find no better explanation. A professor of pathology replied that some outside source of fire is always needed. The verdict was news around the world. Was SHC real, or had people read the evidence wrongly?

The main scientific explanation is the wick effect. It says that a clothed human body can burn like a candle. The clothing acts as the wick. It soaks up melted body fat, which is the fuel, and pulls it up through the cloth. Cloth does this the way a paper towel pulls up water, and scientists call it capillary action.

The fat burns slowly, for several hours, and stays in one place. That helps explain why the fire does not spread far. Parts of the body far from the middle, such as the feet, may survive.

In 1998, Dr. John de Haan did a test for the BBC science program QED. He wrapped a dead pig in a blanket in a furnished room. He lit the blanket with a match and a little gasoline. The pig burned for several hours at high heat, and only the part near it was burned to ash.

The wick effect can explain many of the usual features. The fire stays local. The ends of the body may survive. And an outside source of fire seems missing. In fact, that source is often small and common, such as a cigarette, a candle, or a fireplace ember. It can be burned up or missed during the investigation.

Most scientists agree. They say that alleged cases involve a missed outside source of fire. No case has been proved to be a human body catching fire with no outside source.

Yet the legend lives on. SHC speaks to a deep, old fear that our own bodies might turn against us without warning. The strange scenes add to the fear: the pile of ash, the surviving foot, the untouched newspaper. The wick effect explains how such fires happen. But the pictures stay in the mind. There is the elderly woman turned to ash in her armchair. There is the retired doctor with only a slippered foot left. There is the Irish man burned beyond recognition while his fireplace smoldered quietly.

Science is harder to use against such images. The mystery may be solved in the laboratory. But in people’s imagination, the fear of burning from the inside is still very much alive.
