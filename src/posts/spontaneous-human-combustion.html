Spontaneous Human Combustion: Can People Really Self-Ignite?

Imagine you are sitting alone in your favorite armchair, maybe dozing after a meal. There is no spark and no flame. Then, the story goes, your body begins to burn from the inside. Within hours, all that is left is a pile of gray ash.

The chair under you is only partly burned. The carpet is scorched but still there. The newspapers on the table next to you are untouched, and the walls have only a thin coat of soot. No marks of fire lead away from the body. There is no sign of fuel poured on it and no sign of an outside flame. It is as though the fire came from within.

This is the idea of spontaneous human combustion, or SHC. It is one of the most lasting and disturbing mysteries in the history of crime science, which is the use of science to find out how a death happened. Doctors have written about it since the seventeenth century. It also inspired one of the most argued-over death scenes in English books.

The idea that a human body could catch fire with no outside cause has been debated for more than three and a half centuries. The earliest known written account dates to 1663. A Danish anatomist named Thomas Bartholin described a woman in Paris who was said to have turned to ashes in her bed. The bedclothes around her were not scorched. (An anatomist studies how the body is built.)

Over the next centuries, dozens of similar cases were reported. They appeared in medical journals, newspapers, and legal records in Europe and North America.

In 1853, Charles Dickens caused an uproar. In his novel Bleak House, he used spontaneous human combustion to kill a character named Mr. Krook. Critics said this was medically impossible. Dickens answered that he had read published case reports. The argument filled the letters pages of London newspapers for months. Dickens was not persuaded. “I have no need to observe,” he wrote, “that I do not willfully or negligently mislead my readers.” The row showed a tension that is still with us. The idea of SHC is dramatic, but science has always doubted it.

Of all the cases, none is more famous, or more closely studied, than the death of Mary Hardy Reeser. It happened in St. Petersburg, Florida, in July 1951. On the morning of July 2, her landlady, Pansy Carpenter, came to the apartment of the 67-year-old widow. She had a telegram to deliver.

No one answered the door. Carpenter noticed that the doorknob was unusually hot, so she called the police. The officers who went in found a scene they could hardly understand. Reeser’s easy chair was mostly destroyed. In its place lay a pile of gray ash about 4 feet across.

In the ash were the chair’s coil springs and part of Reeser’s spine. There was her left foot, still in a black silk slipper. There was also a skull “shrunken to the size of a teacup.” The rest of her body had been entirely burned away.

What made the scene so puzzling was how much was spared. A fire strong enough to turn a body to ash usually needs 1,400 to 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit (760 to 980 degrees Celsius). In a modern crematorium, it takes two to three hours. Yet the room was damaged only a little. A candle nearby had melted into a puddle of wax. The plastic light switch covers were warped. The upper walls and ceiling had a thin coat of soot.

The rest of the apartment, including bedsheets just a few feet from the chair, was almost untouched. There was no trail of burning and no sign of fuel. Nothing showed that fire had spread from an outside source.

The St. Petersburg police were so puzzled that they called in the FBI. The FBI did its own investigation. It decided that Reeser had most likely fallen asleep while smoking a cigarette. The cigarette lit her nightgown. This started what experts call the “wick effect.”

The Reeser case was far from the first. One of the earliest cases often told is the death of Countess Cornelia di Bandi of Cesena in 1731. She was found in her bedroom reduced to ashes. Only her head, her lower legs, and three fingers were left.

In 1966, the case of Dr. John Irving Bentley added to the legend. Bentley was a 92-year-old retired doctor who lived in Coudersport, Pennsylvania. On December 5, 1966, a meter reader came into his home. The doctor’s body had been almost entirely burned. Only his lower right leg and foot remained, still in a slipper. They lay next to a hole burned through the floor. As in the Reeser case, the room had little damage. Bentley had smoked a lot and was known to have dropped cigarette ashes before.

Perhaps the most remarkable modern case happened on December 22, 2010, in Galway, Ireland. A 76-year-old man named Michael Faherty was found dead in his home. His body was badly burned. But the fire stayed almost entirely on his body and the space right around it. The fireplace in the room had been used recently, and there was some smoke damage. But there was no sign of an outside source of fire.

The official ruling made the case extraordinary. The coroner, Dr. Ciaran McLoughlin, recorded the cause of death as “spontaneous combustion.” A coroner is an official who decides how a person died. It was reportedly the first time an Irish coroner’s court had given such a verdict. Dr. McLoughlin said he had asked medical textbooks and fire experts and found no other explanation for such a local fire. The verdict was news around the world. It started the debate again. Was SHC real, or had people read the evidence wrongly?

The main scientific explanation is the wick effect. It says that a clothed human body can burn like a candle. The clothing acts as the wick. It soaks up melted body fat, which is the fuel, and pulls it up through the cloth. Cloth does this the way a paper towel pulls up water, and scientists call it capillary action.

The fat burns with a smoky yellow flame. The heat is between about 500 and 800 degrees Celsius (932 to 1,472 degrees Fahrenheit). That is enough to burn the middle of the body over 7 to 13 hours. The process is slow, steady, and stays in one place. That explains why the fire does not spread far. Feet, hands, and sometimes the head often survive. They have less fat and are farther from the center of the fire.

Brian J. Ford tested the wick effect in 1998. He wrapped pig bodies in cloth and lit them with a small flame. Pigs have about as much fat as humans. The results looked very much like the alleged SHC scenes. The middle was burned away, the ends survived, and the room was mostly unharmed.

Around the same time, Dr. John de Haan of the California Criminalistic Institute did a similar test, also with a pig. It showed that body fat could keep a fire going for hours at high heat. No fuel poured from outside was needed.

The wick effect explains almost all the usual features. The fire stays local. The ends of the body survive. Victims are often found sitting or lying down. And an outside source of fire seems missing. In fact, that source is usually small and common: a cigarette, a candle, a fireplace ember, or a faulty heating pad. These can be burned up or missed during the investigation.

Most scientists agree. No case in the medical record has been proved to be a human body catching fire with no outside source. The National Fire Protection Association does not accept SHC as a real cause of fires. Professors of crime science say that every alleged case, when studied carefully, has a likely explanation. It usually involves the wick effect and a missed source of fire.

Yet the legend lives on. SHC speaks to a deep, old fear that our own bodies might turn against us without warning. The strange scenes add to the fear: the pile of ash, the surviving foot, the untouched newspaper. The wick effect explains how such fires happen. But the pictures stay in the mind. There is the elderly woman turned to ash in her armchair. There is the retired doctor with only a slippered foot left. There is the Irish grandfather burned beyond recognition while his fireplace smoldered quietly.

Science is harder to use against such images. The mystery may be solved in the laboratory. But in people’s imagination, the fear of burning from the inside is still very much alive.
