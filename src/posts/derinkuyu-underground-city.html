Derinkuyu Underground City: Up to 20,000 People May Have Sheltered Beneath Turkey

A wall, a passage, and some chickens

In 1963, a man in the small town of Derinkuyu, in central Turkey, was fixing up his house. He knocked down a wall. Behind it he found a passage that went deep into the earth.

Local people tell the story this way. During the building work, a crack had opened in the ground. The man noticed that his chickens kept slipping into the crack and never came back. He wanted to know where they were going. So he looked into the crack and found the dark passage.

The passage led to the first level of an underground city. Some sources say the city has 18 levels. It goes down about 85 meters (280 feet). It was big enough to shelter as many as 20,000 people. There was also room for farm animals, food stores, and churches.

Who would dig such a place, and how? The answer begins with the ground itself.

Rock that is soft, then hard

Derinkuyu is in Cappadocia, in central Anatolia. Anatolia is the large piece of land that makes up most of Turkey. The region is a wide, high plain, about 1,000 meters (3,300 feet) above sea level.

Cappadocia looks like nowhere else. It is famous for its cone-shaped rocks, called “fairy chimneys.” It is famous for skies full of hot air balloons. And it is famous for its underground cities, cave homes, and churches cut into the rock.

The whole region sits on thick layers of volcanic tuff. Tuff is ash that was pressed into rock. The ash came from old eruptions of nearby volcanoes, such as Mount Hasan and Mount Erciyes. This rock is soft and easy to cut when it is first uncovered. Then it slowly hardens in the air. That makes it perfect for digging rooms underground.

The people of Cappadocia used this for thousands of years. They cut homes, stables, churches, and whole cities into the rock. Today many underground cities are known in the region. Among the most visited are Derinkuyu, Kaymakli, Gaziemir, and Ozkonak. In 1985, the Goreme National Park and the Rock Sites of Cappadocia became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. That title is given to places that matter to the whole world.

So let us go down into the biggest of them.

A town under the ground

It is hard to picture how big Derinkuyu is until you stand inside it. It goes about 85 meters (280 feet) below ground. That is about the height of a 25-story building. Reports say it has at least 18 levels of tunnels, rooms, stairs, and halls. Other sources count fewer levels, and only a small part is open to visitors today. Some sections have not been explored yet, so the real size may be larger.

The city was planned with great skill. Guides say the upper levels held living rooms, kitchens, storerooms, and stables for animals. Other rooms were shared spaces, dining halls called refectories, and presses for wine and oil. These suggest that people meant to stay a long time. It was not just a place to hide for a few days.

Guides point out a room on the second level that they call a missionary school, with study rooms and a curved ceiling. On a lower level there is a church cut from the rock. There are also tombs.

The water system is one of the cleverest parts. The city had its own deep wells. They reached down to the water under the ground. Enemies on the surface could not easily reach them. Some think the wells were placed so that nobody outside could poison or block them. In a war, that would matter a great deal.

Still, a city this deep had a problem that no well could solve.

Where did the air come from?

Think of 20,000 people and their animals shut in underground. They need a huge, steady supply of fresh air. Without it, the bad air that people breathe out, called carbon dioxide, would build up. The fires for cooking, heat, and light would also use up the oxygen.

The builders solved the problem with air shafts. More than 50 are known. Some reports count thousands of tiny ducts as well, but that number is not firmly established. The shafts were cut through the rock and joined the levels to the surface. One shaft 55 meters (180 feet) deep seems to have been used as a well.

The builders may have understood how air moves. Inside the city, people, animals, and cooking fires heated the air. On the surface, the air was cooler. Shafts could use that difference. Warm air can rise through some shafts and leave near the surface. That pulls fresh air in through other shafts and entrances. So the air can keep moving by itself, with no fans or machines.

Inside, it stays cool and steady all year. It does not change in the hot summers or the freezing winters above.

But air shafts would not stop an enemy who came through the door.

Doors made of stone

Safety came from great rolling stone doors. Each was a round block cut from the volcanic rock. People inside could roll a door across the way into a level and close it. The doors could be closed from the inside.

Once a door was in place, an attacker would find it very hard to open. The doors were meant to stop the dangers of the past: armies, raiders, and people who hunted others for their religion.

Each level could be closed by itself. So even if enemies got into the upper levels, the defenders could go down and close the passages behind them. The air shafts may have let them keep breathing while enemies waited above. More than 600 tunnel entrances have been found, and some are inside the cellars of private houses.

Such smart defenses would be impressive in any age. But who made them?

Who dug it?

The answer is not simple. No single people built Derinkuyu at one time. It was made bigger and changed over many centuries. Some writers say it was once called Elengubu. The work may have begun in the 8th century BCE.

The first work is often linked to the Phrygians. They were an ancient people with a strong kingdom in central Anatolia in the 8th–7th centuries BCE. But this link is not certain. The soft tuff could be dug out with simple tools, so many peoples could have started the work. Some writers think the first levels may be older than that. They suggest that the Hittites, who ruled Anatolia long before, may have used the site. No firm proof is known.

A Greek writer, Xenophon of Athens, described underground homes long ago. He wrote a work called Anabasis in the 4th century BCE. In it he said that the houses in Armenia, a land east of Cappadocia, were under the ground. People entered through an opening like the mouth of a well. He was not writing about Derinkuyu, but the idea was old in this part of the world.

Later, new groups made the city larger. It was enlarged most from about 780 to 1180 CE, during the wars between the Byzantine Empire and Arab armies. Christians may have used it as a hiding place when they were in danger. Much of what visitors see, such as the church, probably dates from this time.

The end came in 1923. Under the population exchange between Greece and Turkey, the Cappadocian Greeks, the last people there, were forced to leave Turkey. After that the city lay hidden and forgotten for decades. It sat under the houses, streets, and fields of the modern town.

One city, or many?

Derinkuyu did not stand alone. It was part of a huge underground network across Cappadocia. Some are small shelters with one level. Others are huge, like Derinkuyu and Kaymakli.

Some reports say a tunnel about 8–9 km (5–6 miles) long joined Derinkuyu to Kaymakli. Only one book is given as the source for this, and it is not fully proved. If the tunnel was real, people could escape from one city to the other if their own city fell. Communities may also have helped each other during a siege. A siege is when an enemy surrounds a place and waits.

Around 2013, workers found another underground city in the city of Nevsehir. They were tearing down old buildings for a housing project. Early reports say it may be about one-third bigger than Derinkuyu. It may go down about 113 meters (370 feet). The survey was still going on, so none of this is certain.

What else lies below?

Derinkuyu was not a rough cave. It was planned like a small town under the ground. Guides say wine presses were cut into the rock floor. People probably grew the grapes on the surface in safe times and carried them below. The room that guides call a school suggests that children may have kept learning during long stays below.

The city stayed hidden under a modern town for decades. Then a man knocked down a wall and found it by accident. We know it was found in 1963. We know it goes about 85 meters below ground, with its own air, water, stores, churches, and defenses. All of it was cut from soft volcanic rock with simple tools.

What we do not know is who began it, how many levels it has, and how many people really lived there. Perhaps more cities are still waiting. What else lies under our feet?
