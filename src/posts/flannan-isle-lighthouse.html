The Flannan Isle Lighthouse Mystery: Three Keepers Who Vanished Without a Trace

The ship that found an empty tower

It is December 26, 1900. A relief ship called the Hesperus arrives at a tiny, lonely island in the Atlantic Ocean. The island lies about 20 miles west of the Isle of Lewis, in Scotland. A white lighthouse stands on it.

The ship has brought fresh food and mail for three keepers. Joseph Moore, the relieving keeper, goes ashore and climbs the steep path to the tower. He finds the gate and the door closed. Nobody is inside.

The three keepers were never seen again. No bodies were found, and no sign of a struggle was recorded. For more than a century, people have asked what happened. Let us walk to that lighthouse and see what we really know.

Seven islands and one new tower

The tower is called the Flannan Isles Lighthouse. It stands on Eilean Mor, the biggest of a few tiny islands where nobody lives. Sailors called them the Seven Hunters. Where that name comes from is not clear.

Eilean Mor means “Big Island” in Gaelic. It covers about 17 hectares, and its highest point is about 88 meters above the sea. Steep cliffs on the western side drop straight into the water.

The islands are probably named after St. Flannan, an Irish preacher of the 7th century. The ruins of a tiny chapel for him still stand there. There are also old stone huts shaped like beehives. Nobody can say for sure who used them, or when.

So the island was a place of shelter long before the lighthouse.

The lighthouse was designed by David Alan Stevenson. He came from the famous Stevenson family of engineers, which also produced the novelist Robert Louis Stevenson. It was built between 1895 and 1899 and cost £1,899. That was a lot of money then.

Every stone, every timber, and every piece of equipment had to be hauled up the steep cliffs from supply boats. That was hard even in good weather. The tower stood 23 meters tall, and its light sat 101 meters above the sea. It was first lit on December 7, 1899.

Life there was very lonely. Three keepers lived on the island at a time, and a fourth man rested on shore. They kept the light burning and wrote down the weather and sea conditions. A supply ship from the Northern Lighthouse Board visited from time to time. The board is called the NLB for short.

The islands lie in some of the most dangerous waters of the British Isles. In winter, the waves could be very high and the wind could be fierce. A little more than a year after the first lighting, the light would go dark.

The light that nobody lit

On December 15, 1900, a passing steamer called the Archtor noticed that the lighthouse was dark. Its report reached the NLB only on December 18, when the ship arrived at the port of Leith. Nobody went to check at once. That delay would trouble the investigation later.

The Hesperus was under Captain Jim Harvie. Bad weather stopped it from sailing on December 20. It reached the islands around noon on December 26. That was eleven days after the light was last seen working.

Accounts say that no flag flew and that nobody answered the ship’s horn. Moore went ashore. The three men who should have greeted him were James Ducat, the principal keeper, and Thomas Marshall, the second assistant. The third was Donald McArthur, an occasional keeper. He was standing in for William Ross, the regular first assistant, who was on sick leave.

Ducat left a wife and four children. McArthur left a wife and two children. What had Moore seen inside?

What Moore saw inside

Moore found the lamps clean and refilled, ready to light. The keepers had done their morning work. But they had not come back to light the lamps for the evening. The kitchen was very clean, and Moore took this to mean that the keepers had left after dinner.

Then he looked for their waterproof coats, which are called oilskins. Marshall’s oilskins and boots were gone. Ducat’s boots and old waterproof coat were gone too. But McArthur’s coat was still in the lighthouse. Moore believed that he had gone out in his shirt sleeves.

Captain Harvie and his men searched the whole island. They found no bodies and no signs of violence. The only clue was the sign of a sudden departure.

The last entry in the log was made on December 13. Notes for December 14 and 15 were written on a slate, to be copied into the log later. Ducat wrote the last note on the morning of December 15. That is all the writing that the keepers left us. So where do the wilder stories come from?

Stories that the reports do not tell

You may have read that a meal sat untouched on the table and a chair lay knocked over. Those details come from a famous poem written years later. They are not in the official reports.

You may also have read that the log was full of frantic notes. They spoke of storms of unnatural fury, a weeping keeper, and prayers for rescue. Researchers such as Mike Dash say that these entries were made up later. The official NLB report mentions nothing like them.

Other ideas, such as a fight among the men, have no proof at all. So we are left with the real clues: clean lamps, missing coats, and one coat left behind. The NLB had its own answer to them.

A wave at the west landing

The NLB superintendent, Robert Muirhead, went to the island to investigate. His report is dated January 8, 1901. At the west landing, he saw bent and twisted railings. A box of mooring ropes had been washed away. A block of stone weighing about a ton had been moved from its place.

Muirhead decided that the men had probably gone down to secure the box of ropes. Then an unusually large wave swept them away. He noted that McArthur had gone out in his shirt sleeves, which broke the NLB rules. Some writers think that he ran out to warn the others. Nobody can know.

This fits the clues. The clean lamps suggest that the work of the day was done. Then something made the men go out in a hurry. It might have been a sound, a sight from the window, or the fear for the equipment at the landing. Two men seem to have dressed for the weather. McArthur may have left in such a rush that he did not stop for his coat.

The NLB’s conclusion is still the most widely accepted explanation. But could one wave really do this?

Rogue waves are real

For decades, many people doubted that one huge wave could sweep men off a cliff. Rogue waves are waves more than twice as high as the others around them in the same storm. For a long time, many scientists thought that they were sailors’ tales. The usual wave models said that they were almost impossible.

That changed in 1995. The Draupner platform in the North Sea recorded a wave of 25.6 meters (84 feet) on January 1. The usual waves in that sea were about 12 meters high. It was the first rogue wave ever measured by an instrument. Later research suggests that such waves are more common than people once thought.

This does not prove that a rogue wave hit the keepers. But it shows that a giant wave, striking without warning in a bad storm, is possible. It would explain the damage at the landing, which was found more than 30 meters above the sea.

A poem and an empty tower

The poet Wilfrid Wilson Gibson wrote “Flannan Isle” in 1912. His poem made the disappearance famous around the world. It describes the empty lighthouse in eerie detail. It begins: “Though three men dwell on Flannan Isle / To keep the lamp alight, / As we steered under the lee, we caught / No glimmer through the night.”

The poem added the untouched meal and the overturned chair. It also added three strange black birds sitting on the rocks like men. The islands became a ghostly tale of forces beyond understanding. The 2018 film The Vanishing, with Gerard Butler, is based on the story and takes many liberties with the facts.

The lighthouse was automated in 1971. That means machines run it, and it needs no keepers on the island.

Think again of Moore, standing at that closed door in 1900. He found a clean kitchen, ready lamps, and no one to answer. The likeliest explanation is a very large wave striking without warning. It has no villain and no plot. But the only witnesses were the ocean and the men, so nobody can be sure. Three men walked out of a lighthouse and never came back.
