The Phoenix Lights: Arizona's Massive 1997 UFO Sighting

On the evening of March 13, 1997, the skies above Arizona became the stage for one of the most widely witnessed unexplained events in American history. Between approximately 7:30 and 10:30 PM Mountain Standard Time, thousands of people — from the Nevada border south through Phoenix and onward to the outskirts of Tucson, a span of roughly 300 miles (480 kilometers) — looked up and saw something they could not explain. Some witnessed a massive V-shaped or boomerang-shaped formation of amber lights gliding silently overhead, so large that witnesses estimated it stretched anywhere from several hundred yards to a mile or more across. Others saw a stationary row of brilliant orbs hovering above the mountains south of Phoenix, captured on video by dozens of camcorder-wielding residents. The object — or objects — moved without sound, without visible means of propulsion, and without any immediately obvious explanation. Within hours, switchboards at police departments, radio stations, and newsrooms were jammed. Within days, the event had been christened the Phoenix Lights, and a debate was born that continues to this day: what did the people of Arizona actually see that night? Was it military flares, as the Air Force would later claim? A secret aircraft? Or something genuinely unknown — a mystery of the sky that resonates with the same sense of cosmic bewilderment as the Wow! Signal, the same stubborn resistance to easy resolution as the Bermuda Triangle, and the same collision of official denial and credible testimony that characterizes the Pentagon UAP reports of more recent vintage?

The setting was deceptively ordinary. Phoenix, Arizona in March 1997 was a rapidly growing metropolis of approximately 1.2 million people, sprawled across the Sonoran Desert beneath some of the clearest skies in the continental United States. The evening of March 13 was clear and mild — ideal conditions for stargazing and, as it turned out, for witnessing whatever passed through those skies. Arizona was home to major military installations including Luke Air Force Base west of Phoenix and Davis-Monthan Air Force Base in Tucson, as well as the vast Barry M. Goldwater Air Force Range in southern Arizona, a military training area stretching across more than 2,700 square miles of desert. The state's military presence was well known to its residents, many of whom were accustomed to seeing fighter jets and training exercises in the sky. Whatever happened that evening, it was not immediately dismissed by a population unfamiliar with aerial activity — which makes the universal bewilderment of the witnesses all the more striking.

Two Events, One Night: Understanding What Actually Happened

One of the most important — and often overlooked — facts about the Phoenix Lights is that the event actually consisted of two distinct phenomena that occurred on the same evening, separated by time and separated by character. Conflating the two has been the source of enormous confusion in the decades since. The first event was the V-shaped formation, sighted between approximately 7:30 and 8:30 PM, beginning near the Nevada border and moving steadily south-southeast across the entire state of Arizona. Witnesses in Henderson, Nevada — just southeast of Las Vegas — reported seeing a group of six to ten amber or orange lights in a V-formation at approximately 7:55 PM. From there, the formation was reported in Prescott, Prescott Valley, and Dewey in north-central Arizona around 8:15 PM, then over the Phoenix metropolitan area between approximately 8:30 and 8:45 PM, and finally continuing south toward Tucson. The entire traverse covered roughly 300 miles and lasted approximately three hours. Witnesses consistently described the lights as moving silently, without engine noise, and many reported that the formation appeared to be a solid object — a dark, triangular or V-shaped craft that blocked out the stars as it passed overhead.

The second event occurred later, between approximately 8:30 and 10:00 PM, and was witnessed primarily in the Phoenix area. This consisted of a series of stationary or near-stationary lights — typically described as between six and nine brilliant orbs — that appeared to hover in a curved or arched formation over the Sierra Estrella mountain range south of Phoenix. Unlike the V-formation, these lights did not move across the sky; they appeared, remained visible for a period, and then winked out one by one. Critically, the Phoenix lights were captured on video by multiple witnesses, providing the most widely circulated visual evidence of the evening. The two events — the transiting V-formation and the stationary Phoenix lights — have very different characteristics and very different possible explanations, and understanding the distinction between them is essential to evaluating the evidence.

📡 The Phoenix Lights by the Numbers

The events of March 13, 1997, generated some staggering statistics: the sightings spanned approximately 300 miles (480 km) from the Nevada border to the edge of Tucson, between 7:30 and 10:30 PM MST. The V-shaped formation was estimated by some witnesses to be between 1 and 2 miles (1.6-3.2 km) across. The National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) received hundreds of reports. The stationary Phoenix lights consisted of approximately 6-9 lights in a curved formation. The entire event was witnessed by an estimated thousands of people — some sources suggest as many as 10,000 or more — making it one of the most widely witnessed UFO events in American history. The Air Force attributed the stationary lights to A-10 Warthog aircraft from the Maryland Air National Guard's 175th Wing dropping LUU-2 illumination flares over the Barry M. Goldwater Range. These flares burn for approximately 5 minutes while descending slowly on parachutes. The V-shaped formation sighted earlier in the evening has no official explanation.

The Giant That Passed Overhead: Witnesses to the V-Formation

The witnesses to the V-shaped formation constitute a remarkable cross-section of Arizona society: police officers, physicians, pilots, teachers, retirees, and families. What united their accounts was a consistent set of details that resisted easy explanation. In Prescott Valley, callers began flooding emergency lines at approximately 8:17 PM, describing a cluster of reddish-orange lights in a V- or chevron-shaped formation. Witnesses in Prescott and the surrounding area described the formation as enormous — some estimated it was the size of multiple football fields — and moving slowly and silently. A former police officer from Paulden, Arizona, driving north at approximately 8:15 PM, reported seeing a cluster of reddish-orange lights comprising four lights together and a fifth trailing behind. He pulled over, went home, and observed the lights through binoculars until they disappeared over the southern horizon. In Phoenix, witnesses who were outside on the clear March evening looked up and saw the V-formation pass directly overhead. Many reported that the lights were not individual points but appeared to be attached to or embedded in a large, dark, solid structure — and that as this structure passed between them and the stars, the stars disappeared from view and then reappeared once the object had passed, strongly suggesting that the formation was not merely a group of independent lights but a single, massive object.

The sheer number and consistency of these accounts is what has made the Phoenix Lights so enduringly controversial. Skeptics have proposed that the V-formation was simply a flight of military aircraft flying in a triangular pattern — and there is evidence supporting this interpretation. Mitch Stanley, an amateur astronomer observing the sky that night through a telescope, reported seeing what appeared to be individual aircraft — possibly military planes flying in formation. His observation has been cited by skeptics as evidence that the V-formation was nothing more than a group of conventional aircraft misidentified by witnesses unfamiliar with nocturnal aerial formations. However, proponents of the unexplained interpretation counter that the absence of engine noise — consistently reported by witnesses across hundreds of miles — is inconsistent with conventional aircraft, particularly the number of planes that would be required to produce a formation a mile or more across. The debate remains unresolved: the V-formation has never been definitively explained, and it continues to be cited by UFO researchers as one of the most compelling mass sightings in history.

Dr. Lynne Kitei and the Documentation of the Unexplained

Among the most prominent witnesses to the Phoenix Lights was Dr. Lynne Kitei, a Phoenix physician who not only witnessed the event but went on to become one of its most dedicated investigators and advocates. Kitei reported seeing the lights on multiple occasions, beginning in 1995 — two years before the famous March 1997 event — and continuing through 1997 and beyond. She photographed and filmed the lights, compiled witness testimony, and eventually produced a documentary film, The Phoenix Lights (2005), and a companion book, arguing that the phenomenon represented something genuinely anomalous — not military flares, not aircraft, and not easily dismissed. Kitei's medical background gave her credibility as a rational, scientifically trained observer, and her insistence on the reality of what she witnessed has made her a central figure in the Phoenix Lights story. Her documentation, along with the many home videos captured that night, forms the core of the visual record of the event.

🔥 Flares Over the Estrellas: The Air Force Explanation

In June 1997, the United States Air Force released an official statement attributing the stationary Phoenix lights (the second event) to A-10 Warthog aircraft from the Maryland Air National Guard's 175th Wing, 104th Fighter Squadron, which were conducting training exercises at the Barry M. Goldwater Range in southern Arizona on the night of March 13. The aircraft were part of Operation Snowbird, a program that allowed Air National Guard units from cold-weather states to train in Arizona during the winter months. According to the Air Force, the A-10s dropped LUU-2B/B illumination flares — parachute-suspended magnesium flares that burn at approximately 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit for up to 5 minutes while descending slowly. Because the flares were dropped over the Goldwater Range, approximately 60-70 miles southwest of Phoenix, the Sierra Estrella mountain range between the range and the city would have been invisible in the darkness, making the flares appear to hover in the sky when viewed from Phoenix. As each flare burned out, it would wink out abruptly — consistent with the video evidence. The Air Force explanation is widely accepted for the second event (the stationary lights over Phoenix) but does not address the first event (the V-shaped formation seen earlier over northern Arizona).

The Governor Who Joked — Then Told the Truth

Perhaps the most extraordinary chapter in the Phoenix Lights story involves Fife Symington III, the Governor of Arizona in 1997, who was himself a witness to the event — and whose response evolved in a way that perfectly encapsulates the tension between official denial and personal experience that surrounds so many UFO encounters. In the days following the March 13 sighting, as public fascination grew and media coverage intensified, Symington held a press conference that became infamous. To lighten the mood — or, depending on one's perspective, to ridicule the witnesses — Symington had a member of his staff dressed in an alien costume paraded before the cameras while the governor made light of the whole affair. The press conference was widely covered and was seen by many witnesses as a dismissal of their genuine experiences. For a decade, Symington maintained the public position that the Phoenix Lights were nothing to worry about.

Then, in 2007, Symington stunned the UFO research community by publicly admitting that he had personally witnessed the V-shaped formation on the night of March 13, 1997. Speaking to the Arizona Republic and later on national television, Symington described seeing a large, otherworldly craft — not flares, not aircraft — gliding silently over Phoenix. "I'm a pilot," he told reporters, "and I know just about every machine that flies. It was bigger than anything that I've ever seen." He called the object "otherworldly" and expressed regret for his earlier mockery. Symington's testimony was particularly significant because of his credibility: as both a sitting governor and an experienced pilot, he was not a witness who could be easily dismissed as untrained or unreliable. His reversal — from ridiculing the event to confirming it — became one of the defining moments in the Phoenix Lights narrative and a powerful example of how institutional pressure can suppress honest testimony about unexplained aerial phenomena.

The Belgian Connection: A Pattern of Triangular Craft

The Phoenix Lights did not occur in isolation. Between 1989 and 1990, thousands of people in Belgium — including police officers, military personnel, and air traffic controllers — reported seeing enormous triangular or V-shaped craft flying silently at low altitude over the Belgian countryside. The Belgian UFO wave, as it became known, was officially investigated by the Belgian Air Force, which scrambled F-16 fighter jets to intercept the objects on several occasions. The Belgian Air Force publicly acknowledged that it could not identify the craft and that their performance characteristics exceeded those of any known aircraft. The similarity between the Belgian triangular craft and the object reported over Arizona in 1997 is striking: both were described as enormous, silent, V-shaped or triangular, and equipped with bright lights. Other similar mass sightings include the 1942 Battle of Los Angeles, in which anti-aircraft batteries fired on an unidentified object over Los Angeles, and the 1952 Washington, D.C. UFO incident, in which unidentified radar returns were tracked over the U.S. Capitol. The recurrence of large, triangular, silent craft in mass sightings across decades and continents suggests either a pattern of misidentification or a pattern of genuine phenomena that has yet to be officially acknowledged.

📺 After the Lights: Legacy, Hoaxes, and the Enduring Mystery

The Phoenix Lights have left an indelible mark on American popular culture. The event has been the subject of documentaries on the History Channel, Discovery Channel, ABC News, CNN, and numerous other networks. It has been referenced in films, television shows, and video games. In 2015, a group of hobbyists launched LED balloons in a V-formation over Phoenix, causing a brief UFO scare before the prank was revealed. The incident was also reported to have recurred in 2007 and 2008, when lights were again seen over Phoenix, though these later events were attributed to military flares and, in one case, to a resident firing flares from his backyard. In 2017, the Phoenix Lights were cited as one of the most significant historical precedents in the context of the Pentagon's Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) revelations, which disclosed that the U.S. Department of Defense had been investigating unidentified aerial phenomena. The lights remain one of the most thoroughly documented and widely witnessed UFO events in history — a case that has everything: thousands of credible witnesses, video evidence, official military explanations, a governor's testimony, and a mystery that still, after nearly three decades, refuses to be fully resolved.

💫 Lights in the Desert: A Mystery That Won't Go Dark

The Phoenix Lights remain what they have always been: two events, one partially explained, one not explained at all. The stationary lights over Phoenix at 10:00 PM have been convincingly attributed to LUU-2 flares dropped by A-10 Warthogs on the Barry M. Goldwater Range — an explanation supported by timing, trajectory, video analysis, and official Air Force confirmation. But the V-shaped formation that traversed 300 miles of Arizona between 7:30 and 8:30 PM remains unexplained. Hundreds of witnesses — including experienced pilots, police officers, and the Governor of Arizona — described a massive, silent object that blocked out the stars as it passed. The Air Force explanation for the flares does not cover the V-formation. No aircraft formation has been conclusively identified as the source. And the consistency of the witness accounts, spanning hundreds of miles and multiple communities, makes mass hallucination or collective misidentification an unsatisfying explanation. Perhaps the most honest assessment is the simplest: something flew over Arizona on March 13, 1997, that thousands of people saw and that nobody has adequately explained. The lights have faded from the sky. But they have not faded from the mystery.

Frequently Asked Questions

What were the Phoenix Lights?

The Phoenix Lights were a series of widely reported unidentified flying objects seen over Arizona, Nevada, and the Mexican state of Sonora on the evening of March 13, 1997, between approximately 7:30 and 10:30 PM Mountain Standard Time. The event consisted of two distinct phenomena: (1) a V-shaped formation of lights that moved silently across roughly 300 miles (480 km) of Arizona, from the Nevada border south through Phoenix to the edge of Tucson, witnessed between approximately 7:30 and 8:30 PM; and (2) a series of stationary lights that appeared over Phoenix between approximately 8:30 and 10:00 PM, captured on video by multiple witnesses. The event was witnessed by thousands of people and is one of the most widely documented mass UFO sightings in American history. The U.S. Air Force attributed the stationary lights to military flares dropped by A-10 aircraft; the earlier V-shaped formation has no official explanation.

Did the governor of Arizona see the Phoenix Lights?

Yes. Fife Symington III, the Governor of Arizona in 1997, initially ridiculed the event at a press conference where he had an aide dressed as an alien. However, in 2007, Symington publicly admitted that he had personally witnessed the V-shaped formation on the night of March 13, 1997, and described it as "otherworldly" — a large, silent craft unlike anything he had ever seen. As an experienced pilot, Symington's testimony carried significant weight, and his reversal from mockery to acknowledgment became one of the defining moments of the Phoenix Lights story.

What is the military's explanation for the Phoenix Lights?

The U.S. Air Force attributed the stationary lights over Phoenix (the second event, approximately 8:30-10:00 PM) to LUU-2B/B illumination flares dropped by A-10 Warthog aircraft from the Maryland Air National Guard's 175th Wing, 104th Fighter Squadron, which were conducting training exercises at the Barry M. Goldwater Range in southern Arizona as part of Operation Snowbird. The flares, suspended from parachutes, burn at high temperatures for approximately 5 minutes while descending slowly. Viewed from Phoenix, approximately 60-70 miles northeast, the flares would have appeared stationary due to the intervening Sierra Estrella mountain range. This explanation is widely accepted for the second event but does not address the earlier V-shaped formation reported across 300 miles of Arizona.

Are the Phoenix Lights related to other UFO sightings?

The Phoenix Lights bear striking similarities to several other mass UFO sightings. The Belgian UFO wave (1989-1990) involved thousands of witnesses — including police and military personnel — reporting enormous silent triangular craft over Belgium, officially investigated by the Belgian Air Force without resolution. The Battle of Los Angeles (1942) saw anti-aircraft batteries fire at an unidentified object over the city. The Washington, D.C. UFO incident (1952) involved unidentified radar returns tracked over the U.S. Capitol. The recurrence of large, silent, triangular or V-shaped craft across decades and continents — as explored in the Pentagon's UAP investigations beginning in 2017 — suggests either a persistent pattern of misidentification or a genuine phenomenon that remains unacknowledged.
