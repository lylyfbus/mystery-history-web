The Phoenix Lights: Arizona's Massive 1997 UFO Sighting

It was the evening of March 13, 1997, in Arizona. People stepped outside their homes and looked up at the sky. What they saw, they could not explain.

Between about 7:30 and 10:30 PM Mountain Standard Time, thousands of people watched strange lights. They lived all along a stretch of about 300 miles, from the Nevada border south through Phoenix and on toward Tucson.

Some people saw a huge V-shaped or boomerang-shaped group of amber lights gliding overhead. Witnesses guessed it was anywhere from several hundred yards to a mile or more across. Others saw a still row of bright glowing balls hanging above the mountains south of Phoenix. Dozens of people filmed them with camcorders.

Whatever it was, it moved without any sound. It showed no sign of an engine. Within hours, the phones at police stations, radio stations, and newsrooms were jammed.

Within days, the event had a name: the Phoenix Lights. A debate began that has not ended. What did the people of Arizona really see that night?

In March 1997, Phoenix was a fast-growing city of about 1.2 million people. It spread across the Sonoran Desert, under some of the clearest skies in the continental United States. The evening was clear and mild. It was a perfect night for looking at the stars.

Arizona had many military bases. Luke Air Force Base lay west of Phoenix, and Davis-Monthan Air Force Base was in Tucson. There was also the Barry M. Goldwater Air Force Range in southern Arizona. This is a military training area of more than 2,700 square miles of desert.

People in Arizona knew all about the military. Many were used to seeing fighter jets and training flights in the sky. So the surprise of the witnesses was a real puzzle. These were people who knew what military aircraft looked like.

One fact is often forgotten. There were really two different events on the same evening. They happened at different times and looked very different. Mixing them up has caused a lot of confusion.

The first event was the V-shaped formation. People saw it between about 7:30 and 8:30 PM. It began near the Nevada border and moved steadily south-southeast across the state.

At about 7:55 PM, people in Henderson, Nevada, saw six to ten amber or orange lights in a V shape. Around 8:15 PM, people in Prescott, Prescott Valley, and Dewey, in north-central Arizona, saw it. It passed over the Phoenix area between about 8:30 and 8:45 PM. Then it kept going toward Tucson.

The whole trip covered roughly 300 miles and took about three hours. Witnesses said the lights made no engine noise. Many believed it was one solid object, a dark triangle or V that blocked out the stars as it passed over them.

The second event came later, between about 8:30 and 10:00 PM. People saw it mostly in the Phoenix area. It was a set of lights that stayed still, or almost still. Witnesses described six to nine bright orbs in a curved line over the Sierra Estrella mountains, south of Phoenix.

These lights did not cross the sky. They appeared and stayed for a while. Then they went out one by one.

Several people filmed them. This became the best-known video proof of the night. The two events looked very different. They also have very different possible explanations.

The people who saw the V-shape came from every part of Arizona life. They were police officers, doctors, pilots, teachers, retired people, and families.

In Prescott Valley, people began flooding the emergency phone lines at about 8:17 PM. They described a group of reddish-orange lights in a V shape. Some said the V was as big as several football fields. It moved slowly and quietly.

A former police officer from Paulden, Arizona, was driving north at about 8:15 PM. He saw four lights together, with a fifth trailing behind them. In Phoenix, people outside saw the V pass straight overhead.

Many said the lights were not separate points. They seemed to be part of a large, dark, solid shape. As it passed between them and the stars, the stars vanished. Then the stars came back after it had gone by. This strongly suggested one huge object, not a group of separate lights.

Skeptics, people who doubt such claims, say the V was only a group of military planes flying in a triangle. Mitch Stanley, an amateur astronomer, was looking at the sky through a telescope that night. He said he saw what looked like separate aircraft, maybe military planes flying together. Skeptics use his words to argue that witnesses mistook ordinary planes for something strange.

Others disagree. They point out that witnesses over hundreds of miles heard no engine noise. A formation a mile or more wide would need a great many planes. That many planes would not be silent.

One of the best-known witnesses was Dr. Lynne Kitei, a doctor in Phoenix. She said she had seen the lights many times, starting in 1995, two years before the famous night. She photographed and filmed them, collected witness stories, and made a documentary film. Her film argues that the lights were truly unexplained.

In June 1997, the United States Air Force gave an official answer for the still lights, the second event. It said they came from A-10 Warthog planes of the Maryland Air National Guard’s 175th Wing. The planes were training at the Barry M. Goldwater Range as part of Operation Snowbird.

According to the Air Force, the A-10s dropped LUU-2B/B flares. These are bright flares that hang from small parachutes. They burn at about 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit for up to five minutes while they drift down slowly.

The flares were dropped over the Goldwater Range, about 60 to 70 miles southwest of Phoenix. The Sierra Estrella mountains lay between the range and the city. In the dark, the mountains could not be seen. So the flares looked as if they were hanging in the sky above Phoenix. When each flare burned out, it went dark all at once, just like the video shows.

Many people accept this explanation for the second event. But it does not explain the first event, the V-shape that crossed northern Arizona earlier in the evening.

The story has a surprising turn. Fife Symington III was the Governor of Arizona in 1997. He was a witness himself.

In the days after March 13, he held a press conference that became famous. Some say he wanted to lighten the mood. Others say he wanted to make fun of the witnesses. Either way, one of his staff members, dressed as an alien, was led in front of the cameras.

For ten years, Symington kept saying the Phoenix Lights were nothing to worry about. Then, in 2007, he surprised UFO researchers. He said in public that he had seen the V-shaped formation himself on the night of March 13, 1997.

“I’m a pilot,” he told reporters, “and I know just about every machine that flies. It was bigger than anything that I’ve ever seen.” He called the object “otherworldly.” He said he was sorry for his earlier mockery.

His words carried weight. He was a governor and an experienced pilot. Nobody could easily dismiss him as a careless witness.

The Phoenix Lights were not the only case of this kind. In 1989 and 1990, thousands of people in Belgium saw huge triangular or V-shaped craft. Police officers, soldiers, and air traffic controllers were among them. The craft flew silently and low over the Belgian countryside.

The Belgian Air Force investigated. It sent up F-16 fighter jets to chase the objects several times. It admitted publicly that it could not identify them.

The Belgian craft and the Arizona object sound alike. Both were huge, silent, V-shaped or triangular, and covered with bright lights.

There are other big sightings in history. In 1942, in the Battle of Los Angeles, guns on the ground fired at an unknown object over the city. In 1952, radar over Washington, D.C., tracked unknown objects near the U.S. Capitol.

Large, silent, triangle-shaped craft keep showing up across decades and continents. Maybe people keep making the same mistake. Or maybe something real has not yet been admitted by officials.

So the Phoenix Lights remain two events. One is partly explained. One is not explained at all.

The still lights over Phoenix at about 10:00 PM are very likely flares dropped by A-10 Warthogs on the Barry M. Goldwater Range. Timing, flight paths, video checks, and the Air Force all support this.

But the V-shape that crossed 300 miles of Arizona between 7:30 and 8:30 PM is still a mystery. Hundreds of witnesses described a huge, silent object that blocked out the stars. These included experienced pilots, police officers, and the Governor of Arizona. No group of aircraft has ever been proved to be the source.

The witness stories agree with each other across hundreds of miles and many towns. That makes it hard to say that everyone imagined it or made the same mistake.

Something flew over Arizona on March 13, 1997. Thousands of people saw it, and nobody has explained it well. The lights have faded from the sky. But the mystery has not faded.
