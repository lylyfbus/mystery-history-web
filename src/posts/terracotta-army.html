The Terracotta Army: 8,000 Unique Warriors Guarding an Emperor Who Conquered Death

In March 1974, some farmers were digging a well near the city of Xi’an, in Shaanxi province, China. Their tools hit something hard under the soil. It was not water. It was a clay head the size of a real head, with a face like a real person’s. It stared up at them after two thousand years underground.

The farmers had stumbled on one of the great finds of the twentieth century. It was the Terracotta Army. Terracotta means baked clay. Estimates say the army has more than 8,000 soldiers, about 130 chariots, and about 670 horses, all made of fired clay and set in battle lines. They were made to guard the tomb of Emperor Qin Shi Huang, the man who united China in 221 BCE.

Each warrior looks different. Each has its own face, its own hair, and its own armor. It is as if a real army had been frozen in clay and buried. And the army may be the smaller part of the story. The emperor’s own tomb is a huge mound. An ancient writer said it holds flowing rivers of liquid mercury, which stand for China’s great waterways. The mound has never been opened.

Before there was an army, there was an emperor. Ying Zheng was born in 259 BCE. He became king of Qin in 247 BCE. Qin was one of several warring states that fought for control of the Chinese heartland. By 221 BCE, when he was about thirty-eight, Ying Zheng had conquered every rival state. He named himself Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of a united China.

His reign was short, from 221 to 210 BCE, but it changed China. He made writing, money, weights, and measures the same across the empire. He ordered new walls that joined old ones along the northern border. They were a forerunner of the later Great Wall. And he had one great fear: death.

As Qin Shi Huang grew older, he searched for a way to live forever. He sent out expeditions to find magic islands in the Eastern Sea, where, the stories said, an elixir of life could be found. Some historians think he took drinks made with mercury, which can poison people. The true cause of his death at about age 49 is not known.

He also poured the wealth of the empire into his tomb. The ancient historian Sima Qian said 700,000 men were sent to work on it. Modern sources say the work lasted for decades. The Terracotta Army was only one part of this huge burial complex. Sources give its size as roughly 50 to 100 square kilometers. It was built to copy the emperor’s capital city in life.

The army stands in four pits east of the tomb mound. The soldiers face east. That was the direction of the lands he had conquered.

Pit 1 is the largest. It is about 230 meters long and 62 meters wide. It holds the main force of foot soldiers. Estimates range from more than 3,000 to about 6,000 figures. Much of the army has still not been dug up.

They stand in a huge underground hall held up by earth walls and wooden beams. The other pits hold more soldiers, horsemen, and chariots.

Then there is Pit 4, which is empty. It may have been left unfinished. There are no warriors, no horses, and no chariots.

The most common explanation is that the work stopped when the emperor died in 210 BCE. His son became the Second Emperor, and the dynasty fell a few years later. Some accounts say the workers were sent away to fight rebels. Nobody knows for sure why Pit 4 was left empty.

The most amazing feature of the army may be how different the faces are. Each warrior has different noses, cheekbones, ears, eyes, and expressions. Some look stern, some worried, some calm. Scholars say workers used at least ten basic face molds. Some sources say eight. But each face was changed by hand. Workers added clay and smoothed it with tools before the figures were fired. That is why the army feels so haunting.

The warriors were built from standard parts: heads, bodies, arms, legs, and hands. Workshops made these in large numbers. Then workers put them together and made each one different. This method was like factory work, two thousand years before factories. Sculptors and workshops marked their names on the figures. This lets researchers see which workshop made which warriors.

The warriors held real bronze weapons, not copies for show. These were swords, spears, halberds (spears with axe blades), and crossbow triggers. Some blades still have sharp edges after about 2,200 years. Some blades show a thin layer of chromium. Researchers first thought it was an ancient way to stop rust. A 2019 study found that the chromium most likely came from the lacquer on the weapon handles, and not from a coat added on purpose.

When the army was buried, the warriors were painted in bright colors. For more than two thousand years, the paint stayed whole under the soil.

But when the warriors met the air during digging, the lacquer under the paint began to curl within seconds. Then the paint flaked off in a few minutes. The air in Xi’an is dry, and that made the damage fast.

Conservators, the people who protect old objects, watched helplessly. Much of the color was lost. The loss of the paint is one of the great sad stories of modern archaeology.

For all its glory, the army is only a side part of Qin Shi Huang’s burial complex. The emperor himself lies, we assume, under a huge earth mound about 76 meters high. No one has ever dug into it.

What lies inside is known from only one ancient source. It is the Records of the Grand Historian by Sima Qian, written about a century after the emperor’s death. He wrote that the tomb held models of palaces. The ceiling showed the stars. He wrote that there were rivers and seas of liquid mercury, moved by machines to look like flowing water.

Crossbow traps were set to shoot at any robber. There were lamps made with the fat of an animal called a “man-fish.” That may mean a whale or a walrus. And after the tomb was sealed, Sima Qian wrote, the workers who had built it were shut inside, so that its secrets would never get out. Some scholars think parts of his story are not literal. One archaeologist has called it fiction.

In the 1980s and later, scientists tested the soil of the mound. They found more mercury in some samples than in the land around it. Some scholars say it could come from pollution. Ground-penetrating radar has found signs of large underground rooms. But no one knows what is in them.

China has chosen not to dig into the main tomb. The reasons given are to protect what is inside and to protect diggers from mercury. The Terracotta Army taught a hard lesson, because its precious paint was lost in minutes. So officials want to wait until conservation science is good enough to protect whatever lies within.

Today the Terracotta Army stands in its pits as it has for two thousand years. There are rank after rank of clay soldiers. Each has its own face, and each looks east, waiting for an enemy who will never come. An emperor made them. He conquered all of China and then tried, just as hard, to conquer death. He failed. His dynasty fell within four years of his death.

But his army survived, silent and patient, under the fields of Shaanxi until a few farmers with a shovel brought it back to light. The biggest mystery is what lies in the unopened tomb. It may stay unanswered for generations. But the warriors are mystery enough. There are more than 8,000 figures, and each one looks different. They stand guard over an emperor who wanted to live forever.
