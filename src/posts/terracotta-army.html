The Terracotta Army: 8,000 Unique Warriors Guarding an Emperor Who Conquered Death

In March 1974, some farmers were digging a well near the city of Xi’an, in Shaanxi province, China. Their tools hit something hard under the soil. It was not water. It was a clay head the size of a real head, with a face like a real person’s. It stared up at them after two thousand years underground.

The farmers had stumbled on one of the greatest finds of the twentieth century. It was the Terracotta Army. Terracotta means baked clay. The army has about 8,000 soldiers, 130 chariots, and 670 horses, all made of fired clay and set in battle lines. They were made to guard the tomb of Emperor Qin Shi Huang, the man who united China in 221 BCE.

No two warriors are exactly alike. Each has its own face, its own look, its own hair, and its own armor. It is as if a real army had been frozen in clay and buried. And the army may be the smaller part of the story. The emperor’s own tomb is a huge mound. It is said to hold flowing rivers of liquid mercury, which stand for China’s great waterways. It has never been opened.

Before there was an army, there was an emperor. Ying Zheng was born in 259 BCE. His father was the king of Qin, one of several warring states that fought for control of the Chinese heartland. By the age of thirty-eight, Ying Zheng had conquered every rival state. He named himself Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of a united China.

His reign was short, from 221 to 210 BCE, but it changed China. He made writing, money, weights, and measures the same across the empire. He joined old walls into the first Great Wall of China. He built many roads and canals. He also set up the system of government that would rule China for two thousand years. By all accounts he was also ruthless and suspicious. And he had one great obsession: to escape death.

As Qin Shi Huang grew older, his fear of death grew. He sent out expeditions to find the Isles of the Immortals, magic islands in the Eastern Sea. He asked alchemists to prepare drinks for him. These contained cinnabar, a mineral made of mercury and sulfur, which may have slowly poisoned him.

He also poured the wealth of the whole empire into his tomb. The project reportedly used 700,000 workers and took nearly forty years. The Terracotta Army was only one part of this huge burial complex. It covers about 56 square kilometers. It was a city of the dead, built to copy the emperor’s capital city in life.

The army stands in four pits east of the tomb mound. The soldiers face east. That was the direction from which the emperor’s defeated enemies would come.

Pit 1 is the largest. It is about 230 meters long and 62 meters wide. It holds the main force of foot soldiers, an estimated 6,000 warriors in eleven columns.

They stand in a huge underground hall held up by earth walls and wooden beams. Pit 2 holds a smaller but more varied force of infantry, horsemen, chariots, and archers. There are about 1,400 figures. Pit 3 seems to be the army’s command center. It has high officers and a war chariot. It has only about 68 figures, but they are placed as if they direct the armies in Pits 1 and 2.

Then there is Pit 4, which is empty. It was clearly begun, with the same frame as the others. But it was never filled. There are no warriors, no horses, and no chariots.

The most common explanation is that the work stopped when the emperor died in 210 BCE and his dynasty fell. His successor ruled for only three years before rebels brought the dynasty down. Perhaps the workers were called up for war. Perhaps they just left as the empire fell apart. But other ideas remain. Some researchers think Pit 4 was left empty on purpose, as a sign. It would remind us that no army, however big, can protect anyone from death.

The most amazing feature of the army may be how different the faces are. Each warrior has different noses, cheekbones, ears, eyes, and expressions. Some look stern, some worried, some calm. Scholars have found at least ten basic face molds. But each face was changed by hand. Workers added and took away clay and smoothed it with tools before the figures were fired. That is why the army feels so haunting.

The warriors were built from standard parts: heads, bodies, arms, legs, and hands. Workshops made these in large numbers. Then workers put them together and made each one different. This method was like factory work, two thousand years before factories. Archaeologists have found the names or marks of at least 87 master craftsmen. They were stamped or scratched in hidden places on the figures. This lets researchers see which workshop made which warriors.

The warriors held real bronze weapons, not copies for show. These were swords, spears, halberds (spears with axe blades), and crossbow triggers. Many swords are still sharp enough to cut paper after 2,200 years. Some blades show a thin coat of chromium salt. Researchers first thought it was an ancient way to stop rust. More recent studies suggest that the chromium may have come from the lacquer on the weapon handles, and not from a coat added on purpose.

When the army was buried, every warrior was painted in bright, lifelike colors. They had pink skin, black hair, red and blue robes, green armor, and fine patterns. For more than two thousand years, the paint stayed whole under the soil.

But when the warriors met the air during digging, the colors began to curl and flake within minutes, and sometimes within seconds. The sudden change in damp air dried the layer of lacquer under the paint, and the paint came away almost at once.

Conservators, the people who protect old objects, watched helplessly. Bright reds, blues, and greens turned to gray dust. Today only a few figures keep much of their first paint. Modern teams use new methods to make the colors stable on newly dug warriors before they meet the air. The loss of the paint is one of the great sad stories of modern archaeology.

For all its glory, the army is only a side part of Qin Shi Huang’s burial complex. The emperor himself lies, we assume, under a huge earth mound about 76 meters high. It stands about 1.5 kilometers west of the warrior pits. No one has ever dug into it.

What lies inside is known from only one ancient source. It is the Records of the Grand Historian by Sima Qian, written about a century after the emperor’s death. His description is amazing. He wrote that the tomb held a copy of the emperor’s palace, with towers and pavilions. The ceiling was set with pearls and gems to stand for the stars and sky. The floor had a map of China with rivers and seas of liquid mercury, moved by machines to look like real flowing water.

Crossbow traps were set to shoot at any robber. There were everlasting lamps, made with whale oil and combustible minerals. And after the tomb was sealed, Sima Qian wrote, the workers who had built it were shut inside while still alive, so that its secrets would never get out.

In the 1980s and again in the 2000s, Chinese scientists tested the soil of the mound. They found more mercury than in the land around it. Some researchers said it was gathered in patterns that matched China’s main rivers. Ground-penetrating radar and similar tools have found signs of large underground rooms. But no one knows what is in them.

China has chosen not to dig into the main tomb. The reason given is to protect what is inside. The Terracotta Army taught a hard lesson, because its precious paint was lost in minutes. So officials are very careful. They want to wait until conservation science is good enough to protect whatever lies within.

Today the Terracotta Army stands in its pits as it has for two thousand years. There are rank after rank of clay soldiers. Each has its own face, and each looks east, waiting for an enemy who will never come. An emperor made them. He conquered all of China and then tried, just as hard, to conquer death. He failed. The mercury he may have swallowed in search of life forever probably killed him. His dynasty fell within four years of his death.

But his army survived, silent and patient, under the fields of Shaanxi until a few farmers with a shovel brought it back to light. The biggest mystery is what lies in the unopened tomb. It may stay unanswered for generations. But the warriors are mystery enough. There are 8,000 faces, each one different. Each is a person who never lived. They stand guard over an emperor who wanted to live forever.
