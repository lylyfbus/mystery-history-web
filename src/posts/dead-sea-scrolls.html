Dead Sea Scrolls: The 2,000-Year-Old Texts That Rewrote Biblical History

A young shepherd named Muhammed edh-Dhib was looking after his goats. It was 1946 or 1947, and nobody is sure of the exact date. He was a Bedouin, one of the desert people of the region. He stood among rough limestone cliffs above the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. The land was dry, empty, and burned by the sun. Nearby lay the ruins of an old settlement called Khirbet Qumran.

The best-known version of the story goes like this. One of his goats had wandered off. Edh-Dhib threw a stone into a small cave opening in the cliff to make the animal come out. He did not hear a goat. He heard a clay pot break.

He was curious, so he climbed into the cave. Today it is called Cave 1. Inside he found several large clay jars, and their lids were still on. In the jars were old leather scrolls wrapped in linen cloth. Two thousand years of desert air had turned them dark.

He had found one of the greatest discoveries of the 20th century, and perhaps of all time. Over the next ten years, archaeologists and Bedouin treasure hunters explored 11 caves in the cliffs above Qumran. Archaeologists are people who study the past by digging up old things. Together they recovered about 981 texts.

Among them were the oldest copies of the Hebrew Bible that still exist. There were religious texts that nobody had known about. There was also a strange copper scroll that may describe hidden treasure worth billions of dollars. The Dead Sea Scrolls changed what we know about the history of the Bible, about ancient Judaism, and about how Christianity began.

The scrolls were found in the Judean Desert. It is one of the hardest places on Earth to live in. It is a hot land of rock, sand, and salt, with almost no rain. The ground drops from the hills of Judea down to the Dead Sea. That shore is the lowest point on the surface of the planet, about 1,400 feet (430 meters) below sea level.

The dry air is the reason the scrolls lasted for more than 2,000 years. Most were written on parchment, which is made from animal skin. Without water in the air, the skin did not rot.

Qumran stands on a flat shelf of soft, chalky rock. It is about 13 miles (21 kilometers) east of Jerusalem and a mile south of the Dead Sea shore. People lived there during the Second Temple period, from about 150 BCE to 68 CE. That is the age when the second great Jewish Temple stood in Jerusalem.

Most scholars believe the people of Qumran were the Essenes. The Essenes were a strict Jewish group. Three ancient writers described them: Philo of Alexandria, Flavius Josephus, and Pliny the Elder. The Essenes lived together and shared what they had. They followed careful rules about washing and staying pure. They studied holy writings. And they waited for a last great fight between good and evil at the end of the world.

The scrolls were written between about 250 BCE and 68 CE. A great deal happened in those years. The Roman Empire rose. Herod the Great ruled. Jesus of Nazareth lived. Then the Jews rose up against Rome, and in 70 CE the Second Temple was destroyed.

The story of how the scrolls reached the world is full of chance, secrets, and detective work by scholars. Edh-Dhib and his friends took the scrolls from Cave 1 to a dealer in Bethlehem. The dealer offered them to different buyers.

Four of the first scrolls were bought by Metropolitan Athanasius Yeshue Samuel. He was a leader of the Syrian Orthodox Church in Jerusalem. He saw that they might be very old, but he did not fully understand how important they were. The other three scrolls went to Professor Eleazar Sukenik of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. He saw almost at once that they were real.

In 1948, the Arab-Israeli War spread across Palestine. Metropolitan Samuel secretly took his four scrolls to the United States. There, Professor William F. Albright of Johns Hopkins University confirmed them. He was the best-known Bible archaeologist of his time. He said they were of “indubitable antiquity,” which means there was no doubt they were ancient. He dated them to about the 2nd century BCE.

The news excited scholars everywhere. In 1949, archaeologists found where Cave 1 was and dug there with proper care for the first time. They recovered more pieces of scrolls. They also confirmed that the cave was the place the scrolls had come from.

Then the search grew. Between 1951 and 1956, archaeologists and Bedouin searchers went over a strip of cliffs eight kilometers long. They found 11 caves that held writings. Bedouin found five of them, and archaeologists found six.

Cave 4 was the richest. Bedouin found it in 1952. It held about 15,000 fragments, or small pieces, from roughly 580 manuscripts. A manuscript is a text written by hand. It was like a giant jigsaw puzzle of ancient writing. Putting it together and publishing it took decades.

Most of the scrolls were written in Hebrew. Others were in Aramaic and Greek, and a few were in Nabataean. Most were on parchment, and some were on papyrus, a kind of paper made from reeds. One was very different. The Copper Scroll was cut into thin sheets of copper.

Some scrolls were complete and in good condition, like the Great Isaiah Scroll. Others were tiny scraps with only a few letters. But even the smallest scrap could hold important information.

Here are the main numbers. About 981 texts were found in 11 caves near Qumran between 1947 and 1956. They date from about 250 BCE to 68 CE. Before this, the oldest known Hebrew Bible manuscript was the Aleppo Codex, from about 920 CE. So the scrolls are more than 1,000 years older.

The texts include copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther. The longest is the Temple Scroll, at 28 feet (8.6 meters). Today the scrolls are kept in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. The last of the scroll material was not published until 2009. That was more than 60 years after the first discovery.

What is in the scrolls? They hold almost every kind of ancient Jewish religious writing. The most important group is the Bible manuscripts. They are more than a thousand years older than the copies scholars had before. So they show how the words of the Bible were passed down over time.

The star of the collection is the Great Isaiah Scroll. Scholars label it 1QIsaᵃ. It dates to about 125 BCE. It is the oldest complete copy of any book of the Hebrew Bible ever found.

Scholars compared it with the Masoretic Text. That is the standard Hebrew Bible text, which was fixed centuries later. The two are very close. The match is not perfect. Some words are spelled differently, there are small changes in grammar, and here and there a different wording appears. But over more than 1,000 years the text stayed nearly the same. The scribes, the people who copied the Bible by hand, had worked with great care.

The scrolls also include many texts that were unknown, or known only from later translations. Some are copies of the Book of Enoch, the Book of Jubilees, and Tobit. Scholars call such works apocryphal or pseudepigraphal. These books were left out of the official Hebrew Bible. But many people read them in the Second Temple period.

Other texts seem to come from the Qumran community itself. Scholars call them sectarian documents, because a sect is a small religious group. One is the Community Rule, also called the Manual of Discipline. It explains how the group was organized, who could join, and how they worshipped.

Another is the War Scroll, labeled 1QM. It describes a battle at the end of the world between the “Sons of Light” and the “Sons of Darkness.” Human armies fight in it, and so do powers beyond this world.

The Thanksgiving Hymns, or Hodayot, are a set of prayers written like poems. Some of them are said to be by a mysterious “Teacher of Righteousness.” The Damascus Document describes the laws and history of the community.

The Temple Scroll, labeled 11Q19, is the longest scroll found, at 28 feet (8.6 meters). It gives what it says are God’s own instructions for building and running the Temple. In some places its laws are different from the laws in the Bible.

One of the most puzzling people in the scrolls is that “Teacher of Righteousness.” In Hebrew he is Moreh ha-Zedek. He was a priest, and he appears in several texts as the founder or guide of the Qumran community. The texts say God showed him hidden truths.

He had enemies. The scrolls call them the “Wicked Priest” and the “Man of Lies.” They are said to have hunted and hurt the Teacher and his followers. Who were these three people? Scholars have argued for decades. They have suggested different real figures from the Hasmonean period, in the 2nd century BCE.

Some people have compared the Teacher of Righteousness to Jesus. Both were strong religious leaders who challenged the powerful and gathered loyal followers. But the Qumran group and the first Christians were different in ways that matter just as much.

The most exciting mystery of all is the Copper Scroll, labeled 3Q15. It was found in Cave 3 in 1952. Every other scroll is on parchment or papyrus. This one was written on thin sheets of copper. That choice suggests the writers thought the message was too important to trust to something that could rot.

When it was found, the copper was so rusted and easy to break that nobody could unroll it in the normal way. In the end it was cut into strips at the University of Manchester in 1955–1956.

The scroll lists 64 places where huge amounts of gold, silver, and other treasure were hidden. It gives exact measurements, landmarks, and descriptions of hiding places under the ground. The directions are very exact. One begins: “In the ruin that is in the Valley of Achor, under the steps leading to the east, forty-two cubits beneath.” There, it says, “lies a chest of silver and its vessels, with a total weight of seventeen talents.” A cubit was an old measure of length, and a talent was an old measure of weight.

If the treasure ever existed, people have worked out that it would be worth billions of dollars. Many teams have searched for decades. Not one piece of the treasure has ever been found.

Some scholars believe the treasure was real. They think the leaders of the Jerusalem Temple hid it when the Romans attacked the city in 70 CE. Others argue that the Copper Scroll is a made-up story or a legend with nothing real behind it.

Who wrote and collected the Dead Sea Scrolls? Bible scholars have fought over this question since the discovery. The leading answer is called the Qumran-Essene hypothesis. A hypothesis is an idea that has not been proved. Most scholars have held it since the 1950s. It says the scrolls were the library of the Essenes, the strict group described by Philo, Josephus, and Pliny the Elder.

Pliny wrote that the Essenes lived on the western shore of the Dead Sea, “above” Ein Gedi. That fits the place where Qumran stands. The ruins fit too. Archaeologists found pools for ritual washing, called mikva’ot. They found shared dining rooms, tools for writing, and a large cemetery with more than 1,100 graves. All this points to a religious group that lived together.

The community’s own texts agree. They describe people who kept pure, studied holy writings, ate together, and waited for a last battle between good and evil. That is just what the ancient writers said about the Essenes.

But not everyone accepts this idea. Another idea is the Jerusalem origin theory. It says the scrolls were part of the library of the Jerusalem Temple. People carried them out in secret and hid them in the Qumran caves during the Roman attack on Jerusalem in 70 CE. They wanted to save the texts from being destroyed.

Supporters of this theory point to how wide the scrolls range. They show many kinds of Jewish belief and practice. Not all of it fits the special beliefs of the Essenes.

Other scholars stand in the middle. They suggest the caves held a mix. Some texts came from the Essenes, and some came from other Jewish communities in the region. The argument is not settled. And it matters. Where the scrolls came from changes how we read them, and what they tell us about how Judaism and Christianity grew.

Perhaps the deepest change the scrolls made is in how we see Judaism and Christianity together. Rabbinic Judaism is the form of Judaism that grew after the Temple was destroyed. Before the scrolls, most people thought Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity were two completely separate religions that split sharply from the very start.

The scrolls showed a more complicated picture. In the Second Temple period, Jewish thought came in many forms. There were many groups. They read the holy writings in different ways. They had different hopes for a messiah, a leader sent by God. They had different ideas about purity, Temple worship, and life after death.

The Qumran community did things that look like early forms of Christian practice. They washed in water to become pure, much like baptism. They shared meals and blessed the bread and wine. They strongly expected a messiah. In fact they expected two, and possibly three. And they believed a final judgment was coming at the end of the world.

This does not mean Christianity grew straight out of the Qumran community. But it shows that many ideas of the first Christians were already part of the Jewish world of the first century BCE and CE. Today nobody can understand Christianity without understanding Second Temple Judaism. And nobody can understand Second Temple Judaism without seeing how many different beliefs it held.

At heart, the Dead Sea Scrolls are a library. People kept these texts, copied them, studied them, and at last hid them. Those people lived at the edge of the known world, in a tiny settlement above the salty Dead Sea. They lived in one of the most troubled and important ages in human history.

The scrolls lasted more than 2,000 years in the dry caves above Qumran. They outlived the people who wrote them and the group that kept them. They outlived the empires that rose and fell around them. When they came into the light in the 20th century, they answered questions scholars had asked for centuries. They also raised new questions nobody had thought to ask.

They showed that the text of the Bible had been passed down faithfully. They showed how rich and varied Jewish belief was in the Second Temple period. They threw light on how both Rabbinic Judaism and Christianity began. And they introduced us to the Teacher of Righteousness, the Sons of Light, and the treasure of the Copper Scroll.

The scrolls now rest in the Shrine of the Book in Jerusalem. The building is shaped like the lids of the clay jars they were found in. Anyone can read them online in the digital library of the Israel Antiquities Authority.

Their greatest lesson may be this. Holy texts, the stories we tell about our past, and the beliefs that shape whole peoples all come from real people. Those people lived in real places at real moments in time. They worked very hard to save what mattered most to them. But who exactly hid the scrolls in the caves? And is the treasure of the Copper Scroll still out there? Nobody knows.
