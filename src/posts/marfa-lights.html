Unknown Phenomena

The Marfa Lights: Texas Desert's Glowing Mystery

In 1883, a young rancher named Robert Reed Ellison was driving cattle across the open land of West Texas. Night came. Far away on the plain, he saw small lights that glowed in the dark. He thought they were the campfires of Apache people. Later he went to look. He found no fire, no camp, and no sign that anyone had been there.

Ellison had seen what we now call the Marfa Lights. People have watched them for well over a century. And nobody has fully explained them.

The lights appear after dark over a place called Mitchell Flat, south of the town of Marfa, Texas. This is high desert country. The Chihuahuan Desert stretches to the horizon. The Chinati Mountains rise like broken teeth against a very wide sky.

The lights are small, glowing balls. Most people say they are white, yellow, or orange. Some witnesses have also reported red, blue, and green ones. They hover and drift. They join together and split apart. They change color. Then they vanish, and nobody can say why.

Some look about as big as a basketball, and some look much larger. Sometimes they seem close, just beyond the next ridge. Sometimes they are so far away that they are hard to tell from stars.

They also move in odd ways. Sometimes they glide slowly across the desert floor. Sometimes they hang still for a long time. Sometimes they speed up and change direction very suddenly.

Many kinds of people have reported them. Ranchers, pilots, tourists, and scientists who study rocks and physics have all seen them. So have thousands of ordinary visitors. Most of them go to the official Marfa Lights Viewing Center on US Highway 90, about nine miles east of Marfa. Scientists have studied the lights several times. Even so, they are still among the best-known unexplained lights in the United States.

Marfa is a small town of about 1,700 people. It sits in Presidio County, about 4,688 feet above the sea. This part of West Texas is called the Trans-Pecos region. Very few people live there. The nearest city of any size is El Paso, about 200 miles to the northwest.

The land around the town is flat, dry grassland. Here and there stand flat-topped hills made of old volcanic rock. To the south is the sharp line of the Chinati Mountains.

The town is far from anywhere. The desert air is very clear. There are almost no city lights to spoil the dark. So the area is a perfect place to watch the night sky. The lights have interested local people since the first settlers arrived.

Today the lights are a big part of life in Marfa. The town holds the Marfa Lights Festival every year. The Marfa Chamber of Commerce, a group of local businesses, calls the lights the town’s top attraction for tourists.

Other places have strange lights too. Two examples are the Hessdalen Lights in Norway and the well-known Phoenix Lights in Arizona. But Marfa is special in three ways. The lights have been seen for a very long time. Ordinary visitors can easily go and look. And scientists have argued about them a great deal.

Ellison’s sighting in 1883 is the first one on record. The story comes from what local people have told each other over the years. The lights he saw appeared, moved, and went out. They left nothing behind.

Other early settlers told of similar sights. Over the years, the lights became part of local stories. Researchers have collected more than seventy-five folk tales about them from the area. Native American legends in the region also speak of strange lights in the desert. But those stories are older than any written record. So it is hard to prove they describe the same lights.

In 1945, during World War II, a pilot named Fritz Kahl reported the lights. He was based at the Marfa Army Air Field nearby, and he saw them while flying over the area. Kahl was an experienced pilot. He knew aircraft lights and the signal lights that guide planes. He said firmly that these lights did not match any aircraft, any vehicle, or any guide light he knew.

The airfield had been built to train pilots for the war. It brought hundreds of soldiers and airmen to the area. Many of them said they saw the lights during night exercises and training flights. Before this, the lights had mostly lived in the stories of ranchers. Now there were military reports as well.

After the war, people kept reporting the lights. In 1986, the Texas Department of Transportation built the official Marfa Lights Viewing Area. That made the lights a real place for tourists to visit. Since then, hundreds of thousands of people from around the world have come.

The Marfa Chamber of Commerce says about 30,000 to 40,000 people visit the viewing center each year. That makes it one of the most popular attractions in the Big Bend region. The best time to look is on evenings in fall, winter, and spring. Then the desert air is clear and cool.

Scientists have studied the lights more than once. The results taught people a lot, but they also started strong arguments.

The best-known study was done in 2004. A group called the Society of Physics Students, or SPS, at the University of Texas at Dallas carried it out. The leader was a physics professor, Dr. Karl Stephan. The team set up watching posts on Mitchell Flat. For several nights they used binoculars, telescopes, and tools that split light into its colors.

Their answer was clear. The lights they saw were car headlights on US Highway 67. That road runs between Marfa and Presidio through the Mitchell Flat area. The lights matched the cars in position, in movement, and in the kind of light they gave off.

The team showed how this could fool the eye. The highway curves across the desert floor. The viewing center stands higher up. From there, headlights at different distances and angles can play tricks. They can seem to hover, join, split, and change color. The state of the air changes the effect too.

The light itself gave another clue. Every kind of lamp gives off its own mix of colors. The mix from the Marfa Lights matched an ordinary car headlight. That kind of lamp glows because a thin wire of a metal called tungsten gets very hot.

But the SPS study did not end the argument. Critics said the team had watched on only a few nights. They may not have seen everything the lights can do.

There was a bigger problem. People reported the lights before US Highway 67 was built. Robert Ellison saw them in 1883. That was many years before cars were common in West Texas. It was also long before any paved road crossed Mitchell Flat.

Some witnesses have described things that are hard to blame on headlights. Some lights seemed to answer when people shone flashlights or fired flares. Some moved at speeds that did not fit cars and trucks. Some appeared in places where there are no roads at all.

In 2005, Edson Hendricks and a team from Texas State University did their own study of Mitchell Flat. They used side-scan sonar and other tools. Sonar is a way of mapping things with sound waves. Their results were less certain than the SPS results. They said that traffic could explain some of the lights. But headlights alone could not easily explain everything people had seen.

The main rival idea is about the air itself. Light bends when it passes through layers of air that are warmer or cooler, thicker or thinner. Scientists call this bending refraction.

Mitchell Flat often has a layer of warm air sitting above cooler air near the ground. This is called a temperature inversion. It can bend light that comes from far away. Such light could come from the town of Presidio, Texas. It could also come from the Mexican city of Ojinaga, in the state of Chihuahua, across the border. Both lie roughly 40 to 60 miles to the south.

The bent light makes a mirage, a false picture that seems to float above the desert. Some people call this the Fata Morgana explanation, after a famous kind of mirage. It can explain a lot. When the air shifts, the lights seem to move. Their color and brightness change. They come and go without warning.

But this idea has weak points too. The lights have been reported for over 140 years. Long ago, the electric lights of Presidio and Ojinaga were not bright enough to cause such effects. And some witnesses say the lights look close, not far away.

People have offered many other ideas. Each one has supporters and doubters.

One idea was that some minerals in the desert soil glow by themselves under certain conditions. But surveys of the rocks have not found any minerals there that could do this.

Another idea is swamp gas. People sometimes use it to explain lights over wet, marshy land. It fits Marfa badly. In the dry Chihuahuan Desert, standing water and rotting plants are very rare.

A more interesting idea is the piezoelectric effect. It is a hard name for a simple thing. Some minerals, mainly quartz, make an electric charge when they are squeezed. Mitchell Flat has rocks that are rich in quartz. Forces deep in the Earth could press on them. In theory, the charge could make the air above the ground glow.

The same idea has been offered for the Hessdalen Lights in Norway. That valley also has quartz-rich rock and cracks in the Earth called fault lines. But for Marfa, the idea is still only a guess. Nobody has published direct measurements of this effect on Mitchell Flat.

So the ideas run from the very ordinary to the very strange. That shows how hard the puzzle is. Thousands of people have seen the lights, in many different conditions, for more than a century.

The lights are now well known across America. They have inspired books, documentaries, films, and festivals.

In 1956, a big film called Giant was made near Marfa. It starred James Dean, Rock Hudson, and Elizabeth Taylor. The film brought the whole country’s attention to the tiny town and its lights. Since then, many television programs have shown the lights. One was Unsolved Mysteries, and others were shows about hunting for the unexplained.

In 1990, Judith M. Brueske published a book called The Marfa Lights. It is still the fullest collection in print of sighting reports, local stories, and ideas about the cause.

The Marfa Lights Festival has been held each year since 1986. It has live music, food stalls, and arts and crafts. People also go out together to watch for the lights. In 1988, the Texas Historical Commission put up a historical marker at the viewing site. The marker says the lights are an important part of Texas history and stories.

Marfa is known for modern art as well as for mystery. The artist Donald Judd started the Chinati Foundation there. It is a museum of art from our own time. Galleries, restaurants, and hotels have followed it to this far corner of West Texas.

The Marfa Lights are not alone. Around the world there are other “ghost lights.” These are unexplained lights that people see at night, in the same places, over many years.

The Brown Mountain Lights of North Carolina have been reported since at least the early 20th century. Scientists have studied them several times and have not reached a clear answer. The Hornet Spooklight has been seen along the border of Oklahoma and Missouri since the late 19th century. Like the Marfa Lights, it seems to move and change color, and nobody has explained it for certain.

Then there are the Naga Fireballs of the Mekong River in Thailand and Laos. Glowing balls of light rise from the river each October, during the season called Buddhist Lent. Hundreds of thousands of people come to watch. Scientists have studied the fireballs for decades and still cannot explain them. They may be the ghost lights that matter most to the people who live near them.

So where does that leave Marfa? Thousands of people have watched the lights for over 140 years. Physicists and doubters have studied them. People have argued about them in science journals and at the side of the road. Still the lights do not fit neatly into any box.

The headlight answer from the 2004 SPS study is strong. It probably explains the common lights that people see on most nights. But it cannot explain the whole record. It does not cover the sightings from the 19th century, before cars. It does not cover the lights that do not act like traffic. The bending-air idea is a neat second answer, but it has limits too.

One thing is certain. The lights have become much bigger than the lights themselves. They are part of how the town sees itself, and they bring visitors. They are also a reminder. Even with pictures from space and tools that split light into colors, the desert can still surprise us.

Are the lights headlights? Are they mirages, or glowing air, or something else? People still drive out to the dark desert east of Marfa to find out. They sit in lawn chairs beside Highway 90 and watch the horizon. They feel the same wonder that Robert Ellison felt in 1883.

The lights are out there. They always have been. What are they? Nobody can yet say for sure.
