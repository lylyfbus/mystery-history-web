The Marfa Lights: Texas Desert's Glowing Mystery

In 1883, a young rancher named Robert Reed Ellison was driving cattle through Paisano Pass in West Texas. Night came. Far away on the plain, he saw small lights that glowed in the dark. He thought they were the campfires of Apache people. The story says he later went to look. He found no fire, no camp, and no sign that anyone had been there.

Ellison had seen what we now call the Marfa Lights. People have told of them for well over a century. And nobody has fully explained them.

The lights appear after dark southwest of a roadside viewing area about nine miles east of Marfa, Texas. This is high desert country. The Chinati Mountains rise against a very wide sky.

The lights are small, glowing balls. Witnesses often describe them as yellow-orange. Some also report green, blue, or red ones. People say they hover and drift. They join together and split apart. Then they vanish, and nobody can say why.

Some look about as big as a basketball, and some look much larger. Sometimes they seem close. Sometimes they are so far away that they are hard to tell from stars.

Witnesses also say they move in odd ways. Sometimes they glide slowly across the desert. Sometimes they hang still for a long time. Sometimes they speed up and change direction very suddenly.

Many kinds of people say they have seen them. Ranchers, pilots, tourists, and thousands of ordinary visitors have gone to look. Most go to the Marfa Mystery Lights Viewing Center on US Highway 90, about nine miles east of Marfa. Scientists have studied the lights several times. Even so, people still argue about them.

Marfa is a small town of about 1,800 people. It sits in Presidio County, in West Texas, about 4,700 feet above the sea. Very few people live nearby. The nearest big city, El Paso, is about 190 miles to the northwest.

The land around the town is dry grassland. To the south is the line of the Chinati Mountains.

The town is far from anywhere. The desert air is very clear. There are almost no city lights to spoil the dark. So the area is a good place to watch the night sky.

Today the lights are a big part of life in Marfa. The town holds the Marfa Lights Festival every year, and many visitors come to watch for the lights.

Other places have strange lights too. Two examples are the Hessdalen Lights in Norway and the well-known Phoenix Lights in Arizona. But Marfa is special in a few ways. People have told of the lights for a very long time. Ordinary visitors can easily go and look. And scientists have argued about them.

Ellison’s sighting in 1883 is often called the first one on record. But the story comes mostly from what he and his family told later. One writer says no written record from that time shows that he saw the lights. So the story may be true, or it may have grown over the years.

Other early settlers are said to have told of similar sights. Over the years, the lights became part of local stories. Some people say that Native American legends also speak of strange lights in the desert. But we found no record that shows those stories describe the same lights.

Marfa Army Air Field, nearby, trained several thousand pilots during World War II. It closed in 1945.

After the war, people kept telling of the lights. A roadside viewing area was built on US Highway 90, and the Marfa Mystery Lights Viewing Center was built there in the early 2000s. That made the lights an easy place for tourists to visit.

Scientists have studied the lights more than once. The results taught people a lot, but they also started strong arguments.

The best-known early study was done in May 2004. A group from the Society of Physics Students at the University of Texas at Dallas spent four days watching and recording. They used lasers, a chase car, and high-speed video.

Their report said the lights they saw matched car headlights on US Highway 67. That road runs between Marfa and Presidio. The team said the number of lights followed the traffic. When they flashed headlights on the road, the flash looked like a Marfa light from the viewing area.

The team said this could fool the eye. Headlights far away can seem to hover, join, and split. The state of the air changes the effect too.

But the SPS study did not end the argument. Critics said the team had watched for only a few days. They may not have seen everything the lights can do. Some say the report itself called for more research into the rare lights that do not act like cars.

There was a bigger problem. People say they saw the lights before US Highway 67 was built. If Ellison saw them in 1883, that was long before cars were common in West Texas. But as noted, that story is hard to check.

Some witnesses have described things that are hard to blame on headlights. They say that some lights moved in ways that did not fit cars and trucks. Not everyone agrees that such lights exist.

In May 2008, scientists from Texas State University used tools that split light into its colors. They watched the lights from the viewing center for 20 nights. They reported that the lights they saw could be explained by car headlights or small fires. They also said more research was needed.

Another idea is about the air itself. Light bends when it passes through layers of air that are warmer or cooler. Scientists call this bending refraction.

Some skeptics say that a layer of warm air above cooler air can bend light from far away. This is called a temperature inversion. The bent light can make a mirage, a false picture that seems to float above the desert.

A mirage can explain why the lights seem to move, change color, and come and go. A skeptic named Brian Dunning argues that a mirage bends the light of faraway car headlights. He thinks this is the likely cause of the odd motion.

But no one has shown that this explains every report. Some witnesses say the lights look close, not far away.

People have offered many other ideas. Each one has supporters and doubters.

Another idea is that some minerals in the desert soil glow by themselves. We found no study that shows this happens at Marfa.

Another idea is swamp gas. People sometimes use it to explain lights over wet, marshy land. It fits Marfa badly, because the desert is dry.

A more unusual idea is the piezoelectric effect. It is a hard name for a simple thing. Some minerals, mainly quartz, make an electric charge when they are squeezed. In theory, a charge like this could make the air glow. A retired engineer named James Bunnell has suggested that minerals under the ground may do this at Marfa.

The same idea has been offered for the Hessdalen Lights in Norway. But for Marfa, the idea is only a guess. We found no direct measurements that support it.

So the ideas run from the very ordinary to the very strange. That shows how hard the puzzle is.

The lights are now well known in America. They have been shown in books, documentaries, and festivals.

The 1956 film Giant was filmed in Marfa. Some people say it helped bring the town to the notice of the country. Since then, television programs have shown the lights.

In 1989, Judith M. Brueske published a book called The Marfa Lights. She named the roadside spot on Highway 90, nine miles east of Marfa, as the best place to watch.

The Marfa Lights Festival is held each year. It has live music, food stalls, and arts and crafts. People also go out together to watch for the lights.

Marfa is known for modern art as well as for mystery. The artist Donald Judd moved to Marfa and made art there. After his death in 1994, the Chinati Foundation, a museum of modern art, carried on his work.

The Marfa Lights are not alone. Around the world there are other places with “ghost lights.” These are unexplained lights that people say they see at night, in the same places, over many years.

So where does that leave Marfa? People have told of the lights for over 140 years. Physicists and doubters have studied them. Still, people disagree about what they are.

The headlight answer from the 2004 study is strong. It may explain the common lights that people see on most nights. But some say it cannot explain every report. The bending-air idea is another possible answer, but it has limits too.

One thing is certain. The lights have become much bigger than the lights themselves. They are part of how the town sees itself, and they bring visitors.

Are the lights headlights? Are they mirages, or something else? People still drive out to the dark desert east of Marfa to find out. They sit in lawn chairs beside Highway 90 and watch the horizon.

The lights are out there. Nobody can yet say for sure what they are.
