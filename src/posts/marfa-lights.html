The Marfa Lights: Texas Desert's Glowing Mystery

Campfires that were not there

In 1883, a young cowhand named Robert Reed Ellison was driving cattle through Paisano Pass in West Texas. One night, far away on the plain, he saw lights. He thought they were the campfires of Apache people.

The story says that he later went to look. He found no fire, no camp, and no sign that anyone had been there.

Ellison had seen what we now call the Marfa Lights. People have told of them for well over a century. And nobody has fully explained them. So let us go and look for ourselves, in our minds, and see what the witnesses say.

What the witnesses see

The lights appear after dark southwest of a roadside viewing area about nine miles east of Marfa, Texas. The Chinati Mountains rise against a very wide sky.

The lights are small, glowing balls. Witnesses often describe them as yellow-orange. Some also report green, blue, or red ones. People say that they hover and drift. They join together and split apart. Then they vanish.

Some look about as big as a basketball, and some look much larger. Sometimes they seem close. Sometimes they are so far away that they are hard to tell from stars.

Witnesses also say that the lights move in odd ways. Sometimes they glide slowly across the desert. Sometimes they hang still for a long time. Sometimes they speed up and change direction very suddenly.

Ranchers, pilots, tourists, and thousands of ordinary visitors have gone to look. Most go to the Marfa Mystery Lights Viewing Center on US Highway 90. But why here?

A small town in a dark desert

Marfa is a small town of about 1,800 people. It sits in Presidio County, in West Texas, about 4,700 feet above the sea. Very few people live nearby. The nearest big city, El Paso, is about 190 miles to the northwest.

The land around the town is dry grassland. To the south is the line of the Chinati Mountains. The desert air is very clear, and there are almost no city lights to spoil the dark. So the area is a good place to watch the night sky.

Other places have strange lights too. Two examples are the Hessdalen Lights in Norway and the well-known Phoenix Lights in Arizona. But Marfa is special in a few ways. People have told of the lights for a very long time. Ordinary visitors can easily go and look. And scientists have argued about them.

Let us ask next how old the story really is.

The first sighting, or a story that grew?

Ellison’s sighting in 1883 is often called the first one on record. But the story comes mostly from what he and his family told later. One writer says that no written record from that time shows that he saw the lights. So the story may be true, or it may have grown over the years.

Other early settlers are said to have told of similar sights. Over the years, the lights became part of local stories. Some people say that Native American legends also speak of strange lights in the desert. But we found no record that shows those stories describe the same lights.

Marfa Army Air Field, nearby, trained several thousand pilots during World War II. It closed in 1945. After the war, people kept telling of the lights. A roadside viewing area was built on US Highway 90, and the Marfa Mystery Lights Viewing Center was built there in the early 2000s. That made the lights an easy place for tourists to visit.

Scientists went too. Their ideas run from the very ordinary to the very strange.

Idea one: glowing ground and gas

People have offered many ideas about the lights. Each one has supporters and doubters.

One idea is that some minerals in the desert soil glow by themselves. We found no study that shows this happens at Marfa.

Another idea is swamp gas. People sometimes use it to explain lights over wet, marshy land. It fits Marfa badly, because the desert is dry.

A more unusual idea is the piezoelectric effect. It is a hard name for a simple thing. Some minerals, mainly quartz, make an electric charge when they are squeezed. In theory, a charge like this could make the air glow. A retired engineer named James Bunnell has suggested that minerals under the ground may do this at Marfa.

The same idea has been offered for the Hessdalen Lights in Norway. But for Marfa, it is only a guess. We found no direct measurements that support it.

Idea two: bending air

Another idea is about the air itself. Light bends when it passes through layers of air that are warmer or cooler. Scientists call this bending refraction.

Some skeptics say that a layer of warm air above cooler air can bend light from far away. This is called a temperature inversion. The bent light can make a mirage, a false picture that seems to float above the desert.

A mirage can explain why the lights seem to move, change color, and come and go. A skeptic named Brian Dunning argues that a mirage bends the light of faraway car headlights. He thinks that this is the likely cause of the odd motion.

But no one has shown that this explains every report. Some witnesses say that the lights look close, not far away. And a mirage still needs a light to bend. Where does the light come from?

Idea three: car headlights

The best-known early study was done in May 2004. A group from the Society of Physics Students at the University of Texas at Dallas spent four days watching and recording. They used lasers, a chase car, and high-speed video.

Their report said that the lights they saw matched car headlights on US Highway 67. That road runs between Marfa and Presidio. The team said that the number of lights followed the traffic. When they flashed headlights on the road, the flash looked like a Marfa light from the viewing area. Headlights far away can seem to hover, join, and split. The state of the air changes the effect too.

In May 2008, scientists from Texas State University used tools that split light into its colors. They watched the lights from the viewing center for 20 nights. They reported that the lights they saw could be explained by car headlights or small fires. They also said that more research was needed.

But the headlight answer did not end the argument. Critics said that the 2004 team had watched for only a few days. They may not have seen everything the lights can do. Some say that the report itself called for more research into the rare lights that do not act like cars.

There was a bigger problem. People say that they saw the lights before US Highway 67 was built. If Ellison saw them in 1883, that was long before cars were common in West Texas. But as we saw, that story is hard to check. And some witnesses describe lights that moved in ways that did not fit cars and trucks. Not everyone agrees that such lights exist.

A town that kept its lights

The lights are now well known in America. They have been shown in books, documentaries, and festivals. In 1989, Judith M. Brueske published a book called The Marfa Lights. She named the roadside spot on Highway 90, nine miles east of Marfa, as the best place to watch.

The 1956 film Giant was filmed in Marfa. Some people say that it helped bring the town to the notice of the country. The Marfa Lights Festival is held each year. It has live music, food stalls, and arts and crafts. People also go out together to watch for the lights.

Marfa is known for modern art as well as for mystery. The artist Donald Judd moved to Marfa and made art there. After his death in 1994, the Chinati Foundation, a museum of modern art, carried on his work.

So where does that leave us? People have told of the lights for over 140 years. The headlight answer from the 2004 study is strong. It may explain the common lights that people see on most nights. But some say that it cannot explain every report. The bending-air idea has limits too.

Picture Ellison again, far out on the plain, looking at a glow where no fire should be. The lights are out there. Nobody can yet say for sure what they are.
