Pompeii: The City Frozen in Ash for 1,700 Years That Changed How We See Ancient Rome

It was a summer morning in the year 79 CE, in the Roman city of Pompeii. People went about their day. They did not know that the mountain above their city was about to destroy it.

Bakers put bread in their ovens. A landlord painted a picture on his garden wall. Merchants haggled in the forum, the city’s main square. Children played in the streets. Somewhere, a person scratched a rude comment about a neighbor into the plaster of a doorway.

All of these details would be kept safe in amazing detail. Within 24 hours, Mount Vesuvius would bury Pompeii under millions of tons of volcanic ash, pumice, and burning hot gas. The city was frozen at the moment of its death. Pumice is a light rock full of tiny holes, made when lava cools quickly.

The eruption of Vesuvius is one of the best-recorded disasters of the ancient world. We owe this to a lucky stroke of history. A 17-year-old Roman named Pliny the Younger watched it from across the Bay of Naples. Later he wrote two long letters to the historian Tacitus about what he saw.

His account, and almost three centuries of digging, give us a close look at a Roman city in its last hours. No other ancient site can match it. Even so, Pompeii still gives up new secrets.

In 2018, a charcoal writing was found on the wall of a house that was being repaired. It moved the date of the eruption from the old date of August 24 to October 17. This overturned a belief that had lasted for centuries.

In 2023, researchers used artificial intelligence to start reading burned scrolls from the nearby Villa of the Papyri. Nobody had been able to read these texts for nearly 2,000 years. And new digs keep changing how we see the way the city died and the way its people lived.

Mount Vesuvius had been quiet for hundreds of years before 79 CE. People had even forgotten it was a volcano. A century earlier, the Greek geographer Strabo wrote that its top looked like an amphitheater, an open round arena. Its crater had been worn flat by old eruptions.

But the mountain was not dead. A huge pool of hot melted rock, called magma, was building up pressure underneath. In the years before, earthquakes shook the area. A major one in 62 CE did serious damage to Pompeii.

The eruption began around midday. A column of pumice, ash, and burning gas shot 33 kilometers (21 miles) into the sky. At the top it spread out like an umbrella pine tree. Pliny the Younger described this shape. Today, scientists call this kind of eruption a Plinian eruption.

For hours, pumice and ash fell on Pompeii. It piled up at about 15 centimeters an hour. By evening, the city lay under nearly 3 meters of pumice.

Many people had already fled. Others stayed, by choice or because they could not leave. They hid in their homes as the pumice piled up. Roofs began to fall in under the weight.

Then, in the early hours of the next morning, the eruption turned deadly. The tall column of ash fell in on itself. This sent a pyroclastic surge down the sides of Vesuvius. That is a burning avalanche of gas, ash, and pieces of rock.

It raced down at more than 300 kilometers per hour (185 mph). It was almost 300 degrees Celsius (570 degrees Fahrenheit). When it reached Pompeii, anyone still alive died almost at once.

The ash that came after covered the bodies. It left perfect hollow shapes in the ash, and they would last for 1,700 years.

The volcano threw out molten rock and ash at 1.5 million tons per second. Its heat energy was about 100,000 times that of the Hiroshima atomic bomb. Experts think 1,500 to 3,500 people died in Pompeii alone. Up to 16,000 may have died in the whole region.

Our best account comes from Pliny the Younger, who lived from about 61 to 113 CE. He was staying with his mother and uncle at Misenum, a Roman naval base. It was about 30 kilometers across the Bay of Naples from Vesuvius.

His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commanded the Roman fleet. He was also one of the greatest students of nature of his time. When he saw the strange cloud over the mountain, he ordered ships made ready. He sailed straight toward the eruption to look closer and to rescue friends. This choice cost him his life.

The younger Pliny wrote his two letters to Tacitus about 25 years later. They are vivid, and surprisingly scientific. He described the earthquake shaking the ground and the cloud shaped like an umbrella pine. He wrote about swaying buildings, the sea pulling back from the shore, and the darkness that fell over the land. His notes were so exact that the Plinian eruption type was named after his account.

Pompeii and the nearby coastal town of Herculaneum were destroyed by the same eruption. But they were destroyed in different ways. Pompeii lay about 8 kilometers southeast of the crater. It was buried mainly in pumice and ash that fell from the sky in the first phase.

Many Pompeii buildings kept their walls, and some kept their upper floors. The blanket of volcanic material protected them.

Herculaneum was much closer to Vesuvius. It stood right in the path of the pyroclastic flows. It lay under 20 meters (65 feet) of burning pyroclastic material. This was thicker and hotter than the pumice that covered Pompeii.

The great heat turned wood, furniture, and papyrus scrolls into charcoal. This kept them in a form that would not have lasted in the cooler burial of Pompeii.

For centuries, people believed most of Herculaneum’s people had escaped. That changed in the 1980s. Archaeologists digging at the old waterfront found more than 300 skeletons in boat chambers along the beach. These people had run to the shore, hoping to escape by sea. The surge swept through the chambers at temperatures above 500 degrees Celsius.

The most haunting finds at Pompeii are not objects, but people. When the surge struck, the last residents fell where they stood. The ash hardened around them. Over the centuries their bodies broke down. They left hollow spaces in the ash. These kept the exact pose of each person, and even the folds of their clothes.

In 1863, an Italian archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli had an idea that changed archaeology. Diggers sometimes broke into empty spaces with bones inside. Fiorelli realized these spaces were molds of the bodies. He began pouring liquid plaster into them and letting it set.

Then workers carefully chipped away the ash. What they found was astonishing. The plaster copies showed the victims in their final poses. You can see faces, jewelry, and the texture of clothes.

The most famous group of casts is in the Garden of the Fugitives. There, 13 adults and children were found huddled in a corner of a vineyard. Their faces were turned toward the city walls, as if hoping for a way out.

Pompeii is special for archaeologists because it was kept so complete. The ash that killed the city also protected it. People began careful digging in the 18th century. They found a Roman town sealed like a time capsule. Everyday objects lay where their owners had last used them.

In the bakeries, 81 loaves of bread were found still in the ovens. They were burned black but easy to recognize. They were stamped with the baker’s name.

The city had thermopolia, which were the fast-food shops of ancient Rome. Their serving counters had built-in jars called dolia. These held traces of the last meals served, such as stewed meat, fish, and chickpeas. Pompeii had at least 89 thermopolia, about one on every block. This shows how many people ate out instead of cooking at home.

Writing covered the walls all over the city. It gives an honest look at private Roman life. There are insults, boasts, love notes, political slogans, and ads for gladiator games. There were also rude comments that shocked visitors in Victorian times.

The political writing shows that elections were hotly fought. One slogan says: “the fruit sellers urge you to vote for Holconius Priscus for aedile.” An aedile was a city official.

In the 1750s, workers were digging tunnels under Herculaneum. They found something astonishing. It was a huge country house by the sea, filled with bronze statues, mosaics, and a library. The library held about 1,785 scrolls of burned papyrus. Papyrus is an old kind of paper made from a plant.

This was the Villa of the Papyri. People believe it belonged to Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, the father-in-law of Julius Caesar. It held the only whole library to survive from the ancient world.

The surge had turned the scrolls to charcoal. They were rolled into lumps so fragile that trying to unroll them ruined the writing. For more than two centuries, scholars could unroll and read only a small part. Most of what they read was Epicurean philosophy by the Greek thinker Philodemus.

But in recent years, things have changed. Researchers now use X-ray scans and machine learning, which is a way of teaching computers to find patterns. With these, they have begun to read scrolls without unrolling them.

In 2023, a contest called the Vesuvius Challenge offered prizes for reading the scrolls with AI. A team had the first breakthrough. They used machine learning to find Greek letters inside an unopened scroll. The text seems to be about music, food, and pleasure, which fits Epicurean thought.

What if the library holds lost works by writers such as Sophocles, Livy, or Sappho? Even a few could change how we understand the ancient world.

Pompeii lay buried and forgotten for nearly 1,700 years. In 1592, workers digging a channel to move the Sarno River found ancient walls and writing. Nobody linked them to the lost city.

The first planned digs began in 1748. They were led by a Spanish army engineer, Rocque Joaquin de Alcubierre. He was looking for old treasures to decorate the palace of the King of Naples. These early digs were treasure hunts. Workers tunneled through the ash, took the valuables, and buried the rest again.

Fiorelli is mostly credited with turning Pompeii from a place of looting into a science project. He became director of the digs in 1860. He dug from the top down instead of tunneling. He left buildings in place, recorded everything, and gave each building a number.

Only about two-thirds of Pompeii has been dug out. The last third, about 22 hectares, is still buried. People argue about whether it should be dug at all.

Pompeii is a rare disaster that became a gift to history. The eruption that killed thousands also kept their world safe. No act of saving things on purpose could have done this so well.

We know what the people of Pompeii ate for breakfast. We know how they decorated their bedrooms. We know the jokes they scratched on walls, and how they died. No other ancient site shows ordinary life so closely.

But Pompeii also shows how weak a great city can be. A busy city of 15,000 people was wiped out in one afternoon. The mountain is still there, and it is still active. It could erupt again. The last big eruption of Vesuvius was in 1944. Today, more than 3 million people live in its danger zone.

So the story of Pompeii is about the future too. The ground under our feet is never as steady as it seems.
