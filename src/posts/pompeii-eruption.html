Pompeii: The City Frozen in Ash for 1,700 Years That Changed How We See Ancient Rome

It was a day in the year 79 CE, in the Roman city of Pompeii. People went about their day. They did not know that the mountain above their city was about to destroy it.

What was left behind shows a busy town. There were bakeries and snack bars. There were painted walls, a forum, which was the city’s main square, and rude comments scratched into plaster.

All of these details would be kept safe. Within about a day, Mount Vesuvius would bury Pompeii under ash, pumice, and burning hot gas. The city was frozen at the moment of its death. Pumice is a light rock full of tiny holes, made when lava cools quickly.

The eruption of Vesuvius is one of the best-recorded disasters of the ancient world. We owe this to a lucky stroke of history. A 17-year-old Roman named Pliny the Younger watched it from across the Bay of Naples. Later he wrote two long letters to the historian Tacitus about what he saw.

His account, and almost three centuries of digging, give us a close look at a Roman city in its last hours. Few ancient sites can match it. Even so, Pompeii still gives up new secrets.

In 2018, a charcoal writing was found on the wall of a house that was being repaired. It gave a date that matches October 17. Some scholars think the eruption came in the autumn, not on August 24. The August date comes from a printed copy of Pliny’s letter. Scholars still argue about the true date.

In 2023, contest winners used artificial intelligence to start reading burned scrolls from the nearby Villa of the Papyri. Nobody had been able to read these unopened scrolls for nearly 2,000 years. And new digs keep changing how we see the way the city died and the way its people lived.

Mount Vesuvius had been quiet for a long time before 79 CE. Many people may not have known it was a volcano.

But the mountain was not dead. A huge pool of hot melted rock, called magma, was building up pressure underneath. In the years before, earthquakes shook the area. A major one in 62 CE damaged Pompeii.

The eruption began in the early afternoon. A column of pumice, ash, and burning gas shot 33 kilometers (21 miles) into the sky. At the top it spread out like an umbrella pine tree. Pliny the Younger described this shape. Today, scientists call this kind of eruption a Plinian eruption.

For hours, pumice and ash fell on Pompeii. This first fall left about 2.8 meters (9 feet) of pumice and ash on the south side of the volcano.

Many people had already fled. Others stayed, by choice or because they could not leave. They hid in their homes as the pumice piled up.

Then, in the early hours of the next morning, the eruption turned deadly. The tall column of ash fell in on itself. This sent a pyroclastic surge down the sides of Vesuvius. That is a burning avalanche of gas, ash, and pieces of rock.

It raced down at great speed. One study estimates that the first surge to reach Pompeii was about 300 degrees Celsius (570 degrees Fahrenheit). Anyone still alive in its path died almost at once.

The ash that came after covered the bodies. It left hollow shapes in the ash, and they would last for about 1,700 years.

At its peak, the volcano threw out pumice and ash at 1.5 million tons per second. Its heat energy was about 100,000 times that of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombs together. Experts guess that 1,500 to 3,500 people died in the area. Some say up to 16,000 may have died. About 1,500 bodies have been found.

Our best account comes from Pliny the Younger, who was 17 at the time. He was staying with his mother and uncle at Misenum, a Roman naval base. It was about 29 kilometers across the Bay of Naples from Vesuvius.

His uncle, Pliny the Elder, commanded the Roman fleet. He was also a famous writer about nature. When he saw the strange cloud over the mountain, he ordered ships made ready. He sailed toward the eruption to look closer and to rescue friends. He died on the shore at Stabiae. Experts are not sure of the cause.

The younger Pliny wrote his two letters to Tacitus about 25 years later. They are vivid, and surprisingly scientific. He described the earthquake shaking the ground and the cloud shaped like an umbrella pine. He wrote about swaying buildings, the sea pulling back from the shore, and the darkness that fell over the land. The Plinian eruption type was named after his account.

Pompeii and the nearby coastal town of Herculaneum were destroyed by the same eruption. But they were destroyed in different ways. Pompeii lay southeast of the crater. It was buried mainly in pumice and ash that fell from the sky in the first phase, and then by surges.

Many Pompeii buildings kept their walls, and some kept their upper floors. The blanket of volcanic material protected them.

Herculaneum was much closer to Vesuvius. It stood right in the path of the pyroclastic flows. It lay under up to about 20 meters (70 feet) of volcanic material. This was thicker and hotter than the pumice that covered Pompeii.

The great heat turned wood, furniture, and papyrus scrolls into charcoal. This kept them in a form that would not have lasted in the cooler burial of Pompeii.

For a long time, people believed most of Herculaneum’s people had escaped. That changed in 1980. Archaeologists digging at the old shore found about 300 skeletons in arched vaults near the beach. These people had probably run to the shore, hoping to escape by sea. The surge swept through the vaults with great heat.

The most haunting finds at Pompeii are not objects, but people. When the surge struck, the last residents fell where they stood. The ash hardened around them. Over the centuries their bodies broke down. They left hollow spaces in the ash. These kept the exact pose of each person, and even the folds of their clothes.

In 1863, an Italian archaeologist named Giuseppe Fiorelli took charge of the digs. He had an idea that changed archaeology. Diggers sometimes broke into empty spaces with bones inside. Fiorelli realized these spaces were molds of the bodies. He began pouring liquid plaster into them and letting it set.

Then workers carefully chipped away the ash. What they found was astonishing. The plaster copies showed the victims in their final poses. You can see the folds of clothes.

The most famous group of casts is in the Garden of the Fugitives. It is named for thirteen bodies found where they fell. Over 1,000 victims have been found at Pompeii. A little over 100 casts have been made.

Pompeii is special for archaeologists because it was kept so complete. The ash that killed the city also protected it. People began digging in the 18th century. They found a Roman town sealed like a time capsule. Everyday objects lay where their owners had last used them.

Archaeologists have mapped about 39 bakeries in Pompeii. Burned bread has also been found.

The city had thermopolia, which were the fast-food shops of ancient Rome. Their serving counters had built-in jars called dolia. These held food and wine. Pots held goat, pig, duck, fish, and snails. Archaeologists have found 89 thermopolia so far. This shows how many people ate out instead of cooking at home.

Writing covered the walls all over the city. It gives an honest look at private Roman life. There are insults, boasts, love notes, political slogans, and ads for gladiator games.

The political writing shows that elections were hotly fought.

In 1750, farmers digging a well found a huge country house at Herculaneum. Tunnels were later dug there in the 1750s. The house held a library of more than 1,800 scrolls of burned papyrus. Papyrus is an old kind of paper made from a plant.

This was the Villa of the Papyri. It may have belonged to Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, the father-in-law of Julius Caesar. It is the only library from the ancient world that survives whole.

The surge had turned the scrolls to charcoal. They were rolled into lumps so fragile that trying to unroll them ruined the writing. For more than two centuries, scholars could unroll and read only a small part. Much of what they read was Epicurean philosophy by the Greek thinker Philodemus.

But in recent years, things have changed. Researchers now use X-ray scans and machine learning, which is a way of teaching computers to find patterns. With these, they have begun to read scrolls without unrolling them.

In 2023, a contest called the Vesuvius Challenge offered prizes for reading the scrolls with AI. A student, Luke Farritor, found the Greek word for “purple” inside an unopened scroll. In February 2024, a team of three won the grand prize for reading whole passages. The text talks about pleasure and food, which fits Epicurean thought.

What if the library holds lost works by famous writers? Nobody knows. Even a few could change how we understand the ancient world.

Pompeii lay buried and forgotten for nearly 1,700 years. In 1592, the architect Domenico Fontana was digging a channel for water. He found painted walls and writing. Nobody linked them to the lost city.

Digging at Pompeii began in 1748. It was led by a Spanish army engineer, Rocque Joaquin de Alcubierre. The city was not identified then. These early digs were mostly searches for valuable objects.

Fiorelli is mostly credited with turning Pompeii from a place of looting into a science project. He took charge of the digs in 1863. He divided the city into districts and recorded the buildings.

About two-thirds of Pompeii has been dug out. The last third is still buried. People argue about whether it should be dug at all.

Pompeii is a rare disaster that became a gift to history. The eruption that killed thousands also kept their world safe.

We know a great deal about what the people of Pompeii ate. We know how they decorated their walls. We know the jokes they scratched on walls, and how many of them died. Few ancient sites show ordinary life so closely.

But Pompeii also shows how weak a great city can be. A busy city of 10,000 to 20,000 people was wiped out in about a day. The mountain is still there, and it is still active. It could erupt again. Many people live near it today.

So the story of Pompeii is about the future too. The ground under our feet is never as steady as it seems.
