The Pyramids of Giza: 4,500 Years Later, We Still Cannot Fully Explain How They Were Built

A logbook from a boat inspector

In 2013, archaeologists were working at Wadi al-Jarf, an ancient port on the Red Sea coast of Egypt. They found a set of papyrus pieces. Papyrus is an ancient kind of paper. The pieces added a lot to what we know about how the Great Pyramid was built. They are now called the Wadi al-Jarf Papyri.

The most important one is the “Diary of Merer.” It is a daily logbook kept by a middle-ranking Egyptian inspector named Merer. He led a team of boat workers.

The diary dates to about the 26th or 27th year of Khufu’s reign. It covers several months. In that time, Merer’s team carried blocks of Tura limestone from the quarries across the Nile to the building site at Giza. Workers opened the quarries, cut the stones, and loaded them on boats. The boats sailed through canals to the Giza harbor. It is the oldest known record from the time of how the stone was moved.

So here is a real man at his real work. Let us see what he was working on.

Three giants on the plateau

The Giza Plateau lies on the edge of modern Cairo, on the west bank of the Nile River. Here the golden sand meets the green ribbon of the Nile Valley. Three huge shapes rise from the plateau. They have stood there for more than 4,500 years.

This was the time of the Old Kingdom, about 2686 to 2181 BCE. The plateau was chosen as the burial ground for the most powerful pharaohs of the Fourth Dynasty. The three pyramids were built within about 85 years, in the 2500s BCE. They were built in the reigns of Khufu (called Cheops by the Greeks), Khafre (Chephren), and Menkaure (Mycerinus).

The Second Pyramid, for Khafre, looks taller than Khufu’s because it stands on higher ground. But it is a little shorter, at 448 feet. The Third Pyramid, for Menkaure, is the smallest, at 213 feet.

The builders used copper tools, wooden sledges, rope, and human muscle. The complex also has the Great Sphinx, temples, and causeways, which are raised roads. Near Khufu’s pyramid, archaeologists found boat pits with full-size wooden boats in pieces. The most famous is the Khufu Ship. It was put back together from its pieces.

But the biggest of the three is the one that Merer’s stones were meant for.

The Great Pyramid in numbers

The Great Pyramid was built for Pharaoh Khufu, about 2600 to 2560 BCE. It is the oldest and the largest of the three. For about 3,800 years, it was probably the tallest building made by people anywhere on Earth. Few buildings have held a record for so long.

Its first height was 481 feet (146.6 meters). Over time, it lost its smooth casing stones, and today it is about 455 feet tall. Each side of the base is about 756 feet long. The pyramid covers about 13 acres. It is made of about 2.3 million stone blocks. Most weigh about 2.5 tons each. Some of the granite blocks in the King’s Chamber weigh up to 80 tons.

The casing stones were polished white limestone from Tura, across the Nile. In the Egyptian sun, they would have shone brightly. In later centuries, most were taken away to build in Cairo.

The work was also very exact. The base is level to within about 2.1 centimeters across its whole 13-acre footprint. The sides face north, south, east, and west with an error of only a fraction of a degree. Some say that a razor blade could not fit between the joints of the casing stones.

Now step inside.

Rooms in the stone

Inside, the pyramid has a clever system of passages and rooms. The King’s Chamber holds a huge granite sarcophagus, a stone coffin. It is too big to have been carried in after the building was done. There is also the Queen’s Chamber. The Grand Gallery is a tall corridor, 153 feet long and 28 feet high, with a ceiling that steps inward. Above the King’s Chamber are relieving chambers, which spread out the great weight of the stone above.

The first entrance is on the north face, about 56 feet up. It was hidden with great care. Visitors today use another tunnel. Caliph al-Ma’mun had it dug in about 820 CE, when he tunneled through the limestone looking for treasure.

The pyramid still keeps a secret. In 2017, the ScanPyramids project used muon tomography to look inside. This method uses tiny particles from space to see through stone. It found a large empty space above the Grand Gallery, at least 100 feet long. It was the first large hidden space found in the Great Pyramid since the 1800s. Nobody knows what it was for.

A building this big needed a great many people.

The Drunkards of Menkaure

The Greek historian Herodotus visited Egypt in the 5th century BCE. He wrote that the Great Pyramid took 20 years and 100,000 workers. Experts today think that these numbers are probably too big. Modern Egyptologists, who study ancient Egypt, estimate that the workforce was often about 14,000 and at most about 40,000. They worked in shifts for about twenty years.

Who were they? The archaeologists Mark Lehner and Zahi Hawass dug in the workers’ village from the 1990s on. It suggests that the workers were skilled, well-fed, and well-organized. This is very different from the popular picture of slaves beaten under the desert sun. Archaeologists found many bakeries that made large loaves of bread. Skeletons show medical care, including healed broken bones and amputations. Animal bones show that workers ate beef, sheep, and goat.

The workers formed teams that competed. They had names like “The Drunkards of Menkaure” and “The Friends of Khufu.” This may show pride and friendship. Some scholars think that many workers came from villages and took turns in shifts. Whether they were paid or forced to work is still debated.

We know who the workers were. How they did the job is harder.

No instruction book

The big problem is that no Egyptian text, picture, or plan has ever been found that explains the building work. The Egyptians kept records of many things. But they left no instruction book for building a pyramid. Perhaps the methods were so well known that nobody needed to write them down. Or perhaps they were sacred knowledge that was not shared.

Most theories are about ramps. A ramp is a sloping path. Blocks would be hauled up it on wooden sledges.

The straight ramp theory says that one long ramp ran from the quarry to the top of the pyramid. The trouble is that such a ramp would have to be enormous. Most experts doubt it.

The spiral ramp theory says that a ramp wound around the outside of the pyramid, going upward. This fixes the length problem. But it is hard to turn the corners and keep everything lined up.

Around 2006, a French architect named Jean-Pierre Houdin offered the internal ramp theory. He says that builders used an outside ramp for the lower part. For the upper part, they used a spiral ramp built inside the pyramid. He says that part of it may still be hidden in the stone. Some experts call the idea far-fetched, and it is not proven.

So each theory explains a piece of the puzzle.

The wonder that is left

The Great Pyramid is the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that still stands. The Colossus of Rhodes, the Lighthouse of Alexandria, the Temple of Artemis, the Statue of Zeus, and the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus are all gone. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon may never have existed.

We know much more now than we did a century ago. Merer’s diary gives us the voice of a man who helped deliver stone to Khufu’s pyramid. The workers’ village shows us the people who did the work. ScanPyramids has found a hidden space that no explorer has entered.

But the big question is still open. Exactly how were the pyramids built? Each ramp theory explains part of the evidence. None explains all of it.

The pyramids are not magic. They came from people with great skill who did not write down their methods in a form that we can read. Somewhere on the Nile, over 4,500 years ago, a man called Merer wrote down his day’s work on papyrus. His small note, and these huge stones, are two parts of one story. The rest is still waiting in the sand.
