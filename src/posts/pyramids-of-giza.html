The Pyramids of Giza: 4,500 Years Later, We Still Cannot Fully Explain How They Were Built

Picture the edge of the Western Desert. Here the golden sand meets the green ribbon of the Nile Valley. Three huge shapes rise from the Giza Plateau, and a deep silence hangs around them.

They have stood there for more than 4,500 years. They have seen pharaohs rise and fall. They have seen Greeks and Romans conquer Egypt. They have seen Christianity and Islam spread, and the modern world begin.

The biggest is the Great Pyramid of Giza. For over 3,800 years, it was the tallest building made by people anywhere on Earth. No other building has held that record for so long.

It is made of about 2.3 million stone blocks. Many weigh 2.5 tons each. Some of the granite blocks in the King’s Chamber weigh up to 80 tons.

Its base is level to within about 2.1 centimeters. Its sides face the four main compass directions, north, south, east, and west, with an error of only 3/60th of a degree. Yet after centuries of study, even by some of the best archaeologists in history, we still cannot fully explain how it was built.

The Giza Plateau lies on the edge of modern Cairo, on the west bank of the Nile River. It looks out over the rich floodplain that fed Egyptian civilization for thousands of years.

This was the time of the Old Kingdom, about 2686 to 2181 BCE. The plateau was chosen as the burial ground for the most powerful pharaohs of the Fourth Dynasty. This time is often called the “Age of the Pyramids.”

The three pyramids at Giza were built within about 85 years, from about 2589 to 2504 BCE. They were built in the reigns of Khufu (called Cheops by the Greeks), Khafre (Chephren), and Menkaure (Mycerinus).

The complex also has the Great Sphinx, temples, and causeways, which are raised roads. There are smaller pyramids, and boat pits that hold full-size wooden boats taken apart in pieces.

The builders used copper tools, wooden sledges, rope, and human muscle. Yet the size and exactness of the work are among the greatest feats of engineering in human history.

The Great Pyramid was built for Pharaoh Khufu, in the Fourth Dynasty, about 2560 BCE. It is the oldest and the largest of the three. Its first height was 481 feet (146.6 meters). Over time, it lost its smooth casing stones, and today it is about 455 feet tall.

Each side of the base is about 754 feet long. The pyramid covers about 13 acres.

The casing stones were polished white limestone from Tura, across the Nile. In the Egyptian sun, they would have shone brightly. People could have seen them from miles away across the desert. In later centuries, most were taken away to build in Cairo.

The precision is amazing by any standard. The base is level to within about 2.1 centimeters across its whole 13-acre footprint. The sides face true north, south, east, and west to within 3/60th of a degree. The joints between the casing stones were so fine that a razor blade could not fit between them.

Inside, the pyramid has a clever system of passages and rooms. The King’s Chamber holds a huge granite sarcophagus, a stone coffin. It is too big to have been carried in after the building was done.

There is also the Queen’s Chamber. The Grand Gallery is a tall corridor, 153 feet long and 28 feet high, with a ceiling that steps inward. Above the King’s Chamber are relieving chambers, which spread out the great weight of the stone above.

The first entrance is on the north face, about 56 feet up. It was hidden with great care. Visitors today use another tunnel. Caliph al-Ma’mun had it dug in about 820 CE, when he tunneled through the limestone looking for treasure.

How were the pyramids built? Scholars, engineers, and dreamers have asked this for thousands of years. The Greek historian Herodotus visited Egypt in the 5th century BCE. He wrote that the Great Pyramid took 20 years and 100,000 workers. Experts today think these numbers are probably too big.

Modern Egyptologists, who study ancient Egypt, estimate 20,000 to 30,000 skilled workers. They worked in shifts for about twenty years.

The big problem is that no Egyptian text, picture, or plan has ever been found that explains the building work. The Egyptians kept careful records of religious rituals, farming seasons, court cases, and wars. But they left no instruction book for building a pyramid.

This missing record is a mystery too. Perhaps the methods were so well known that nobody needed to write them down. Or perhaps they were sacred knowledge that was not shared.

Most theories are about ramps. A ramp is a sloping path. Blocks would be hauled up it on wooden sledges.

The straight ramp theory was suggested in the 19th century by Flinders Petrie, after he carefully measured Giza. It says one long ramp ran from the quarry to the top of the pyramid. The trouble is that such a ramp would have to be enormous, maybe longer than the pyramid itself.

The spiral ramp theory says a ramp wound around the outside of the pyramid, going upward. This fixes the length problem. But it is hard to turn the corners and keep everything lined up.

In 2007, a French architect named Jean-Pierre Houdin offered the internal ramp theory. He says builders used an outside ramp for the lower part. For the upper part, they used a spiral ramp built inside the pyramid. He says it is still there today, hidden in the stone. His idea drew attention when a gravity scan seemed to find a spiral-shaped difference in density inside the pyramid.

In 2013, archaeologists were working at Wadi al-Jarf, an ancient port on the Red Sea coast of Egypt. They made a discovery that changed what we know about how the work was organized. It was a set of papyrus pieces, now called the Wadi al-Jarf Papyri. Papyrus is an ancient kind of paper.

The most important one is the “Diary of Merer.” It is a daily logbook kept by a middle-ranking Egyptian inspector. He led a team of about 200 workers.

The diary dates to the 27th year of Khufu’s reign. It covers several months. In that time, Merer’s team carried blocks of Tura limestone from the quarries across the Nile to the building site at Giza.

The entries describe the steps. Workers opened the quarries, cut the stones, and loaded them on boats. The boats sailed through canals to the Giza harbor. Then the stones were delivered to the site. It is the most direct record from the time of how the stone was moved.

Another key find at Giza is the workers’ village. The archaeologists Mark Lehner and Zahi Hawass began digging there in the 1990s.

The village shows that the workers were skilled, well-fed, and well-organized. This is very different from the popular picture of slaves beaten under the desert sun. There were bakeries that could make thousands of loaves a day, and there were breweries.

There is proof of medical care, including treated broken bones and amputations. Food was handed out in an orderly way. Animal bones show that workers ate good cuts of beef, sheep, and goat.

The workers formed teams that competed. They had names like “The Drunkards of Menkaure” and “The Friends of Khufu.” This shows pride and friendship. The workers seem to have come from all over Egypt. They served in shifts, as a kind of national service.

The Great Pyramid does not stand alone. The Second Pyramid was built for Pharaoh Khafre. It looks taller than Khufu’s because it stands on higher ground. But it is a little shorter, at 448 feet. The Third Pyramid, for Pharaoh Menkaure, is the smallest, at 213 feet.

The Great Sphinx was carved from a single piece of limestone rock. It is about 240 feet long and 66 feet high. People believe it shows the face of Pharaoh Khafre, though this is still debated.

Near Khufu’s pyramid, archaeologists found boat pits with full-size wooden boats in pieces. The most famous is the Khufu Ship. It is 143 feet long, and it was put back together from 1,224 pieces.

In 2017, the ScanPyramids project used muon tomography to look inside. This method uses tiny particles from space to see through stone. It found a large empty space above the Grand Gallery, about 100 feet long. It was the first major hidden structure found in the Great Pyramid since the 19th century. Nobody knows what it was for.

The Pyramids of Giza have outlasted every other wonder of the ancient world. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon, the Colossus of Rhodes, the Lighthouse of Alexandria, the Temple of Artemis, the Statue of Zeus, and the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus are all gone. They have become rubble, memory, and legend. Only the pyramids remain.

We know much more now than we did a century ago. The Wadi al-Jarf Papyri give us the voice of a man who really delivered stone to Khufu’s pyramid. The workers’ village shows us the human side of the work force. ScanPyramids has found hidden spaces that no explorer has ever entered.

But the big question is still not fully answered. Exactly how were the pyramids built? Each ramp theory explains part of the evidence. None explains all of it.

The exact alignment, the size of the task, and the clever design inside are still partly a mystery. They are not magic. They come from a people with great engineering skill who did not write down their methods in a form we can read.

The pyramids remind us that human genius is not new. Great things can be done with simple tools and great organization. And even the most studied monuments on Earth can still surprise us.
