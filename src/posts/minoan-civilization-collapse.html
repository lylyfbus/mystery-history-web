The Fall of the Minoans: How Earth's Biggest Eruption Toppled a Civilization

A busy harbor on Crete

Picture the island of Crete in the second millennium BCE. That means a time between about 2000 and 1000 years before the year 1. Ships rock in a busy harbor. They are loaded with olive oil, wine, and saffron, a costly spice. Inside a great palace, painters are at work on a wall. They are drawing young acrobats who leap over a charging bull.

These people were the Minoans. Modern archaeologists gave them that name after the legendary King Minos of Crete. The Minoans are often called the first civilization in Europe. Yet around 1450 BCE, their world began to change. Why did it fade? To find out, we must first see how it grew.

An island at the crossroads

The Minoan civilization grew out of the early farming cultures of Crete. It began around 3100 BCE. Towns and palaces grew around 2000 BCE.

The island had fertile plains and a mild climate. It also lay in the middle of the eastern Mediterranean Sea. That made it a natural crossroads for trade between Egypt, the Levant, Anatolia, and mainland Greece. Anatolia is now Turkey.

The Minoans sailed as traders. Their goods and ideas reached Egypt, Syria, and Anatolia. Some scholars think they may have reached as far west as the Iberian Peninsula, where Spain and Portugal are today.

The time from about 1700 to 1450 BCE was a rich one. Archaeologists call it the Neopalatial period. Some scholars think the Minoans may have held a leading place in sea trade across much of the Mediterranean. And at the heart of it all stood the palaces.

The palace that became a maze

The palaces were huge groups of buildings. Scholars now think they served as places for worship, work, and storing goods. The largest and most famous was Knossos. The British archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans began large digs there in 1900.

Knossos covered about 20,000 square meters and had several floors. Its plan was complex. In later Greek tradition, it was remembered as the Labyrinth, from the myth of the Minotaur, a monster that was part man and part bull. Other major palaces stood at Phaistos, Malia, and Zakros.

The Minoans were skilled builders. The palace at Knossos had drains, water channels, and a water supply. Light wells and open courts let daylight and air into the inner rooms. Their wooden columns were often narrower at the base than at the top, which was a special Minoan style.

The walls held pictures that still make people wonder.

Bulls, saffron, and writing nobody can read

Minoan art shows bull-leapers and saffron gatherers. It is lively and full of movement. The best-known pictures are the bull-leaping paintings at Knossos. Young athletes grab a charging bull and jump over its back.

Was this a real sport, a religious ritual, or a story from myth? People still debate it. The bull probably had a religious meaning. Some link the images to the Greek myth of Theseus and the Minotaur. That link is tempting, but it is only a guess.

The Minoans also wrote. They used Cretan hieroglyphs and a script called Linear A. Nobody has been able to read either one. So the Minoans cannot tell us their own story. We must read it from ruins, paintings, and the writing of their later neighbors.

Then, something enormous happened to the north of Crete.

The day Thera blew apart

Sometime around 1600 BCE, the volcano on Thera erupted. Thera is the island now called Santorini. The exact date is still argued. Radiocarbon tests, which measure old carbon in wood and bone, point to about 1627 to 1600 BCE. Evidence from archaeology points to a later date, about 1550 to 1480 BCE. The gap between the two methods is not solved.

The blast was hard to imagine. It was rated 7 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index, a scale that measures how big an eruption is. That makes it one of the largest eruptions in human history. Scientists estimate that it threw out 28 to 41 cubic kilometers of dense rock equivalent. That means the amount of rock if all the air were squeezed out. The cloud of ash may have climbed 30 to 35 kilometers into the sky.

Pyroclastic flows rushed across Thera. These are glowing avalanches of hot gas, ash, and rock. The eruption also made tsunamis, which are giant sea waves. Some researchers link the eruption to odd weather as far away as China and Egypt. But that link is debated.

Crete lies about 110 kilometers to the south. What did the waves do there?

Waves, ash, and a silent town

Studies suggest that the waves reached northern Crete. At the Minoan site of Palaikastro, they may have been about 9 meters high. Scientists have found a layer of sea-washed material there, mixed with volcanic ash from Santorini. This is physical evidence that a tsunami reached the coast of Crete. Such waves could wreck seaside towns and ships.

The Minoan economy depended on trade by sea. Losing ships and harbors would have been a serious blow. But the Minoans seem to have recovered, and the years after the eruption were a high point of their culture.

On Thera itself, the town of Akrotiri was buried under volcanic ash. The ash kept its buildings and wall paintings safe. People often call it the “Minoan Pompeii.” Strangely, nobody has found any human remains there. This suggests that the people left before the worst part of the eruption.

Some people think the destruction of Thera lies behind Plato’s legend of Atlantis. The Greek archaeologist Spyridon Marinatos proposed the link. But today most scholars do not support it.

If not the volcano, then what?

Here is the key fact. Minoan civilization did not end with the eruption. The great destruction on Crete came about 1450 BCE. By some dates the gap is about 150 years. By other dates it is shorter.

During that time the Minoans rebuilt damaged places and kept trading. Major sites such as Knossos still thrived. So the eruption was not one hammer blow. At most it was one of several problems.

Some scholars point to earthquakes, which could have damaged palaces. Others point to ash and salt water that spoiled farmland. One study suggests that the climate around Crete became drier after about 1450 BCE. Less food would have strained the economy and the palace system. But nobody can say for sure.

Then came the Mycenaeans, Greek-speaking people from mainland Greece. After about 1450 BCE, they dominated Crete in culture and perhaps in politics. The clay tablets at Knossos were now written in Linear B, an early form of Greek. Scholars read Linear B in 1952. This shows that Greek-speaking officials were now running the palace.

How this happened is still argued. Some researchers blame invaders, because many sites were burned around 1450 BCE. Others think the cause was trouble inside Crete. It may have been slow, with trade deals, alliances, and rulers who adopted the new ways.

A chain with many links

The fall of the Minoans was probably not one event. Some scholars think the palaces were central to the Minoan economy. If so, damage to the palaces would have hurt the whole system. The sea routes were also important. If ships and harbors were hurt, goods and wealth from abroad would have slowed.

So what do we know? A great eruption shook the Aegean. Many palaces on Crete were destroyed around 1450 BCE, and then the Mycenaeans became strong there. What do we not know? How much of the change was the volcano, and how much was something else. The Minoans’ own script, Linear A, has never been read, so they cannot tell us.

Think again of the busy harbor with its ships of oil and wine. That world faded, and left behind ruins, legends, and a mystery. Archaeologists have studied it for over a century, and scholars still ask the question.
