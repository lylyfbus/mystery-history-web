Black Dahlia: The Gruesome 1947 Murder That Remains Hollywood's Greatest Unsolved Case

The Black Dahlia murder of 1947 remains Los Angeles' oldest unsolved homicide, a case that continues to captivate and horrify nearly eight decades later.

The woman who saw something pale

It was the morning of January 15, 1947, in Los Angeles. A young mother named Betty Bersinger was walking with her three-year-old daughter along South Norton Avenue. This was in a neighborhood called Leimert Park. Before 11 a.m., she saw something pale in a vacant lot. She thought it was a store mannequin that someone had thrown away.

She moved closer. It was not a mannequin. It was the body of a young woman. Betty Bersinger hurried away to call the police.

The woman was Elizabeth Short, who was twenty-two years old and dreamed of being an actress. Within days, the newspapers gave her a name: the Black Dahlia. People still use it today.

Her murder is one of the oldest unsolved killings in Los Angeles. It was also one of the biggest investigations by the Los Angeles Police Department, the LAPD. No one was ever charged. Let us start with the young woman herself, before the legend covered her.

A girl from Boston

Elizabeth Short was born on July 29, 1924, in Hyde Park, a neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts. She was the third of five daughters. Her father, Cleo Short, worked as a bookkeeper and then built miniature golf courses. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 destroyed his business.

In 1930, the family heard that Cleo’s car had been found by the Charlestown Bridge. Everyone thought that he had died. In truth, he had walked away from his family to escape his money troubles.

Elizabeth grew up in Medford, Massachusetts. She spent winters in Miami, Florida. By most accounts she was shy and quiet, with dark hair and fair skin. She had bad asthma and had lung surgery at the age of 15.

In late 1942, a letter came from her father. He was alive, and he was living in Vallejo, California. Elizabeth was eighteen, and she went to live with him. They argued, and she moved out in January 1943.

Six days without a trail

From then on, Elizabeth moved between California, Florida, and Massachusetts. She stayed with friends and people she knew. She worked for a short time at the Camp Cooke military store in California. On September 23, 1943, she was arrested in Santa Barbara for drinking under the legal age. She tried to become an actress but never won a real part.

In January 1947, she was in Los Angeles. On January 9, a salesman named Robert Manley said that he dropped her at the Biltmore Hotel in the city. Her body was found six days later. What happened to her in those six days is still unknown.

That gap is the heart of the mystery. The police needed to learn who she was, and quickly.

Fingerprints and a faraway fax

The police named her within a day. They used fingerprint records from her 1943 job and her arrest. A newspaper, the Examiner, sent her prints to Washington with a device called a Soundphoto. It was an early kind of fax machine used by news services. The FBI matched the prints, and by the evening of January 16 she had a name.

Now the police had to find out what had happened. Doctors thought that she had been dead for about ten hours. No blood was found at the lot. So the police knew that Elizabeth Short had been killed somewhere else. Her body was brought to the lot and left there to be found. The lot was not the crime scene.

The coroner, Dr. Frederick Newbarr, studied the body. Some investigators believed that the killer had medical training or great skill with a blade. That was only a belief, and it was never proved.

About 750 investigators from the LAPD and other police groups worked on the case at the start. Detective Finis Brown was one of the lead detectives. Yet they never found the killer. One reason, he later said, was the newspapers.

How the press made a legend

The nickname “Black Dahlia” was invented by reporters in the days after the murder. It may have come from a 1946 film noir called The Blue Dahlia. A film noir is a dark crime film. Some stories say that friends in Long Beach used the name because she liked black dresses. Where the name came from is not certain.

Detective Brown later blamed the newspapers for making the work harder. They printed shocking details about Short’s life, and many were wrong. They called her a wild party girl. But the people who knew her said that she was shy and quiet.

The name turned a real person into a spooky symbol. And the case was soon treated like a legend, not like a crime against a young woman.

Letters, hoaxes, and false confessions

Then the teasing began. The killer, or someone pretending to be the killer, sent letters and packages to the LAPD and to newspapers. One package held Short’s personal papers and an address book. The letters used words cut out of newspapers and magazines. Many have never been tied to the real killer. They may have been hoaxes by people who wanted attention.

There were also false confessions. Dozens of people came forward at first to say that they did it, drawn by the fame of the case. A reward of $10,000 may have brought more. Nearly all of the confessions were found to be false.

Each false lead took time. The police looked at more than 150 suspects. A few names stand out.

Leslie Dillon became a suspect in 1948. Police questioned him, but he was never charged. Mark Hansen owned a nightclub, and his name was in Short’s address book. He was cleared. Robert Manley, the salesman, was cleared too.

The most talked-about suspect is Dr. George Hill Hodel (1907–1999), a well-known Los Angeles doctor. The LAPD formally investigated him in 1950. Soon after, he left the country. He lived in Hawaii and then the Philippines until 1990, and he was never charged.

Hodel was arrested in 1949 on a charge involving his own daughter, Tamar Hodel. He was found not guilty at trial. But the case led the police to listen secretly to his home for about five weeks. Notes about the recordings became public in 2003. In one of them, Hodel is reported to have said, “Supposin’ I did kill the Black Dahlia.” Police did not find enough evidence to link him to Short.

In 2003, his son Steve Hodel, a former LAPD detective, published a book called Black Dahlia Avenger. He argued that his father was the killer. He pointed to his father’s medical training and to the recordings. Critics, including another LAPD detective, say that the theory rests on guesses. One writer says there is no good proof that George Hodel ever met Short.

A novel brings her back

In 1987, the writer James Ellroy published a novel called The Black Dahlia. Many people credit it with bringing back public interest in the case. Ellroy’s own mother was murdered in 1958, in a case that was never solved. He has said that his interest in the Dahlia case could not be separated from his grief.

His book mixed real details with the drama of film noir. In 2006 it was made into a film, directed by Brian De Palma. The novel was fiction, and it helped spread some myths about Short’s life. But it turned a fading cold case into a lasting part of American true crime stories.

So what do we truly know?

The woman behind the name

The case is still officially unsolved. The main suspects have all died, and no suspect has ever been proved guilty. Some writers think that fewer false leads and less press interference might have found the killer. By the time the police found their footing, the trail had gone cold.

The Black Dahlia is remembered not because it can be solved, but because it cannot. But the story is not about a legend. It is about a twenty-two-year-old from Boston who wanted to be an actress. She was not a criminal and not a dark queen of Hollywood.

Think again of Betty Bersinger on that January morning, and the thing she took for a mannequin. More than 75 years later, nobody has named the person who killed Elizabeth Short. The police looked at more than 150 suspects. They found many false confessions, and none of them solved the case. What is known is her name, and her short life.
