Black Dahlia: The Gruesome 1947 Murder That Remains Hollywood's Greatest Unsolved Case

The Black Dahlia murder of 1947 remains Los Angeles' oldest unsolved homicide, a case that continues to captivate and horrify nearly eight decades later.

It was the morning of January 15, 1947, in Los Angeles. A young mother named Betty Bersinger was walking with her three-year-old daughter along South Norton Avenue. This was in a neighborhood called Leimert Park. Before 11 a.m., she saw something pale in a vacant lot. She thought it was a store mannequin that someone had thrown away.

She moved closer. It was not a mannequin. It was the body of a young woman. She had been laid out carefully, with her arms raised above her head. Betty Bersinger hurried away to call the police.

The woman was Elizabeth Short, a twenty-two-year-old who dreamed of being an actress. Within days, the newspapers gave her a name: the Black Dahlia. The name is still used today.

Her murder is one of the oldest unsolved killings in Los Angeles. It was also one of the biggest investigations by the Los Angeles Police Department, the LAPD. No one was ever charged.

Elizabeth Short was born on July 29, 1924, in Hyde Park, a neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts. She was the third of five daughters of Cleo and Phoebe Short. Her father worked as a bookkeeper. Then the Wall Street Crash of 1929 destroyed his business, which built miniature golf courses.

In 1930, the family heard that Cleo Short had left his car by the Charlestown Bridge. Everyone thought he had jumped into the Charles River and drowned. In truth, he had walked away from his family. He had faked his own death to escape his money troubles.

Elizabeth grew up in Medford, Massachusetts. She spent winters in Miami, Florida. By most accounts she was a striking girl, with dark hair and fair skin. She was also shy and quiet. She had bad asthma and had lung surgery at the age of 15.

In late 1942, a letter came from Cleo Short. He was alive, and he was living in Vallejo, California. The family was stunned. Elizabeth was eighteen. She traveled to California to live with her father.

The reunion did not last. They argued, and she moved out in January 1943.

From then until her death, Elizabeth moved between California, Florida, and Massachusetts. She stayed with friends, boyfriends, and people she knew. She worked for a short time at the Camp Cooke military store in California. On September 23, 1943, she was arrested in Santa Barbara for drinking under the legal age.

She tried to become an actress but never won a real part.

In the months before her death, Short moved around Los Angeles. On January 9, 1947, a salesman named Robert Manley said he dropped her at the Biltmore Hotel in downtown Los Angeles. What happened to her between that day and the day her body was found, six days later, is still unknown.

The nickname “Black Dahlia” was invented by reporters in the days after the murder. It may have come from a 1946 film noir called The Blue Dahlia. A film noir is a dark crime film. Some stories say that friends in Long Beach used the name because she liked black dresses. Where the name came from is not certain.

The press spread the name. It turned a real person into a spooky symbol. It also helped the case to be treated as a legend.

The place where she was found was 3815 South Norton Avenue. Some investigators believed the killer had medical training or great skill with a blade.

The body had been washed clean and placed in a pose on purpose. There was no blood anywhere at the scene. Doctors thought she had been dead for about ten hours. So the police knew that Elizabeth Short had been killed somewhere else. Her killer had cleaned her, carried her to the lot, and left her there for people to find. The vacant lot was not the crime scene. It was a place to show her.

The coroner who studied the body was Dr. Frederick Newbarr. He found that she had died from bleeding from cuts to her face and from shock after blows to her head. There were also marks on her wrists and ankles that showed she had been tied. Terrible injuries had also been done to her body after she died.

The police named her within a day. They used fingerprint records from her 1943 job and her arrest. A newspaper, the Examiner, sent her fingerprints to Washington with a device called a Soundphoto. It was an early kind of fax machine used by news services.

The FBI matched the prints to the ones from her job at Camp Cooke and her arrest in Santa Barbara. By the evening of January 16, she had been named.

Detective Finis Brown was one of the lead detectives. About 750 investigators from the LAPD and other police groups worked on the case at the start. And yet they never found the killer.

Detective Brown later blamed the newspapers for making the work harder. Newspapers printed shocking details about Short’s life, and many were wrong. They called her a wild party girl. But the people who knew her said she was shy and quiet. She was not the dangerous glamorous woman the press invented. But the myth had already taken root.

The killer, or someone pretending to be the killer, teased the police. Letters and packages were sent to the LAPD and to newspapers. One package held Short’s personal papers and an address book. The letters used words cut out of newspapers and magazines. Many of them have never been tied to the real killer. They may have been hoaxes by people who wanted attention.

There were also many false confessions. Dozens of people came forward at first to say they did it, drawn by the fame of the case. Some wanted attention. A reward of $10,000 may have brought more.

Nearly all of the confessions were found to be false. The real killer has never been named.

The police looked at more than 150 suspects. A few stand out. Dr. George Hodel was a Los Angeles doctor. The LAPD looked into him around 1950. His son Steve Hodel, a former LAPD detective, has spent decades arguing that his father was the killer.

Leslie Dillon became a suspect in 1948. Police questioned him, but he was never charged. Mark Hansen owned a nightclub, and his name was in Short’s address book. He was cleared. Robert “Red” Manley was a salesman who said he gave her a ride to the Biltmore. He was cleared too.

The most talked-about suspect is Dr. George Hill Hodel (1907–1999). He was a well-known Los Angeles doctor who earned his medical degree in 1936. The LAPD formally investigated him in 1950. Soon after, he left the country. He lived in Hawaii and then the Philippines until 1990, and he was never charged. His friends included the artist Man Ray and the film director John Huston. He lived in the Sowden House, a mansion in Los Angeles.

Hodel came under suspicion after he was arrested in 1949 on a charge involving his own daughter, Tamar Hodel. He was found not guilty at trial. But the case led the police to listen secretly to his home for about five weeks. Notes about the recordings became public in 2003. In one of them, George Hodel is reported to have said: “Supposin’ I did kill the Black Dahlia.” Police did not find enough evidence to link him to Short.

In 2003, Steve Hodel published a book called Black Dahlia Avenger. He argued that his father killed Elizabeth Short. He pointed to his father’s medical training and to the recordings.

Critics, including another LAPD detective, say his theory rests on guesses. One writer says there is no good proof that George Hodel ever met Short.

In 1987, the writer James Ellroy published a novel called The Black Dahlia. Many people credit it with bringing back public interest in the case. Ellroy’s own mother was murdered in 1958, in a case that was never solved.

His book mixed real details of the case with the drama of film noir. In 2006 it was made into a film, directed by Brian De Palma. The novel was fiction, and it helped spread some myths about Short’s life. But it turned a fading cold case into a lasting part of American true crime stories.

Ellroy has said that his interest in the case could not be separated from his grief over his mother. Two unsolved crimes were tied together in the dark legend of Los Angeles.

In almost every way, the Black Dahlia case is a cold case. The main suspects have all died. No suspect has ever been proved guilty.

The case is still officially unsolved. Some writers think that a careful investigation with fewer false leads and less press interference might have found the killer. The flood of false leads and false confessions buried the real clues. By the time the police found their footing, the trail had gone cold.

Yet the fame of the case has only grown. The Black Dahlia lasts not because it can be solved, but because it cannot. It tells an old kind of tale. A beautiful young woman is destroyed by an unknown hand. A city of dreams shows its dark side. And the people trusted to keep order fail to bring justice.

The murder of Elizabeth Short is no longer a police case. It is a cultural story, told so many times that the real woman has almost vanished behind the legend. She was not a dangerous glamorous woman. She was not a criminal. She was not the dark queen of Hollywood. She was a twenty-two-year-old from Boston who wanted to be an actress.

The LAPD looked at more than 150 suspects and solved nothing. The case brought many false confessions and not one true one. More than 75 years later, the person who killed Elizabeth Short has never been identified. Who was the careful stranger who vanished without a trace? The truth died with the killer, and the legend lives on.
