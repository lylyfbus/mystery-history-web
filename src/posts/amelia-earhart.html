Amelia Earhart: 88 Years Later, Are We Close to Finding Her Plane?

Amelia Earhart and her Lockheed Electra, shortly before the 1937 around-the-world attempt that would become aviation's greatest mystery

It was 8:43 in the morning on July 2, 1937. A famous voice came over the radio in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. The voice belonged to Amelia Earhart. She was flying her silver plane, a Lockheed Model 10 Electra, somewhere over the empty sea.

Her message went to a US Coast Guard ship called the Itasca. The ship was waiting near tiny Howland Island. “We are on the line 157-337,” she said. “We are running north and south.” A few minutes before, she had said, “We must be on you, but cannot see you — gas is running low.”

The crew of the Itasca looked hard into the bright sky. They sent black smoke up from the ship’s funnel so she could see them. But the sky stayed empty. Her voice grew weaker with each message. Then it stopped.

Earhart, her navigator Fred Noonan, and their plane were gone. A navigator is the person who works out the route. They had vanished over the Pacific, an ocean so large that it covers about one-third of the Earth.

The United States Navy began the biggest search and rescue in its history up to that time. Ships and planes searched 250,000 square miles of ocean for sixteen days. The search cost $4 million, which is about $90 million in today’s money.

They found nothing. There was no piece of the plane, no oil on the water, and no life raft. There was no sign of the world’s most famous woman pilot. More than 80 years later, it is still one of the great unsolved mysteries of the twentieth century. People have studied it, argued about it, and made up wild stories about it.

The mystery is bigger because of who she was. Amelia Mary Earhart was born in 1897. She was not only a pilot. She was known all over the world. At that time, women were still fighting for basic rights. To many people, she stood for the courage and skill of women.

In 1932, she became the first woman to fly alone across the Atlantic Ocean. That was five years after Charles Lindbergh’s famous flight. She set records for height and for speed. She wrote bestselling books and designed clothes. She worked hard to help other women become pilots.

Her flight around the world in 1937 was meant to be her greatest success. The route was 29,000 miles long and followed the equator, the line around the middle of the Earth. No plane had ever tried to fly so far around the world.

On June 29, 1937, she reached Lae in New Guinea. By then she had flown about 22,000 miles. Only 2,556 miles of ocean lay between her and Howland Island. Howland is a tiny island of 1.7 square miles, and it is one of the loneliest pieces of land on Earth. She planned to get more fuel there. Then she would fly on to Hawaii and home.

This part of the trip should have been normal. Instead, it became the most famous missing-person case in the history of flying.

Many things went wrong on the way to Howland. There were problems with the plane, with finding the way, and with the radio.

The Electra had the number NR16020. It was an all-metal plane with two engines and one pair of wings. It had been changed a lot for this trip. Extra fuel tanks filled the cabin, so the plane could fly about 4,000 miles. The flight to Howland was 2,556 miles. That was far enough inside the limit, but it left little room for mistakes.

Howland Island was a very hard place to find. It was only 1.5 miles long and 0.5 miles wide. It was a dot of coral and sand in a huge blue sea. Even in good weather, a pilot could hardly see it from the air.

The US government had built a simple dirt runway on the island just for Earhart. But nobody lived there, and there was nothing tall to look for. The island also looked like the many other low islands and reefs in that part of the ocean.

The radio problems were the most serious. The Electra had a modern radio receiver for finding the way. It was made to pick up signals from the Itasca. The ship was there to give her radio bearings. A bearing is a direction to fly.

But for reasons that are still not clear, Earhart seems to have used the wrong radio channel for parts of the flight. She spoke on a channel called 3105 kHz. The Itasca listened on several channels. Yet she never seemed to hear the ship’s messages to her.

The crew said her voice was loud and clear. That meant she was quite close. But again and again she said that she could not hear the ship. The radio worked in one direction only. So the Itasca could not send her the bearings she needed to find Howland.

There were also reports of thick clouds in the area. Clouds would have made the tiny island even harder to see.

Her last sure messages suggest that she was flying a search pattern. She was going north and south along a line called 157/337 degrees. She was trying to see the island or the ship’s smoke. Then there was silence.

The US Navy and most flight historians accept one simple answer. It is called the “crash and sink” theory. The plane ran out of fuel and came down in the sea. This probably happened within 100 miles of Howland Island. Then it sank in water that is thousands of feet deep.

The best-known other idea is about an island called Nikumaroro. It used to be called Gardner Island. It is a coral island in the Phoenix Islands, and nobody lives there. It lies about 350 nautical miles south-southeast of Howland.

A group called the International Group for Historic Aircraft Recovery supports this idea. Its short name is TIGHAR, and its director is Ric Gillespie. They think Earhart and Noonan could not find Howland and were low on fuel. So they turned southeast, along the 157-degree line from the last message. They flew toward the Phoenix Islands and landed on a flat coral reef near Nikumaroro.

In this story, the plane landed safely on the reef when the sea was low. Earhart and Noonan lived for days, or perhaps weeks, on the island. They sent calls for help from the plane’s radio. Then the rising sea pulled the plane off the reef and into the ocean.

Some exciting finds have kept this idea alive. In 1940, a British officer named Gerald Gallagher found part of a human skeleton on Nikumaroro. Near it were a woman’s shoe, a box for a sextant, and a bottle. A sextant is a tool that sailors and pilots use to find their position. The bottle seemed to have been used as a compass bowl.

Gallagher knew about the Earhart mystery. He reported the find to his bosses and guessed that the bones might be hers. The skeleton was sent to Fiji to be studied. A doctor there first decided that it belonged to a man with both Polynesian and European family roots.

Later, experts on bones looked again at the doctor’s measurements. One of them was Dr. Karen Burns. They suggested that the bones fitted a European woman of Earhart’s height and build. Sadly, the skeleton was lost in Fiji during World War II. Nobody can test it with modern DNA methods.

TIGHAR has made more than a dozen trips to Nikumaroro since 1989. Its teams have brought back many objects. One is a jar of freckle cream that fits a brand Earhart was known to use. There are bits of aluminum plane skin that may match the Electra. There are pieces of a woman’s shoe from the 1930s. And there is a sextant box of a type that fits Noonan’s tools.

In 2012, a TIGHAR team used sonar on the sea floor near the reef. Sonar makes pictures with sound. It showed a strange shape 600 feet down. Some researchers thought it could be part of a plane. But the picture was not clear.

The biggest test of the Nikumaroro idea came in 2017. Robert Ballard led a trip to the island, paid for by National Geographic. Ballard is the ocean scientist who found the wreck of the Titanic in 1985. He had said before that he doubted the Nikumaroro idea.

He brought the best deep-sea tools. They included underwater robots called ROVs, which people steer from a ship. The robots could search the sea floor around the island down to 3,000 feet.

Ballard’s team searched the waters with great care. They found no clear sign of the Lockheed Electra. There was no wreck, no engine part, and no object that surely came from Earhart’s plane. The team did bring back some objects from the island. They also recorded things that fit the idea of people stranded there. But the empty sea floor was a heavy blow to the Nikumaroro idea.

In January 2024, there was new excitement. A company from South Carolina called Deep Sea Vision, or DSV, builds sea robots. It said it had found a strange shape on the floor of the Pacific. The shape was about 100 miles from Howland Island.

The shape lay about 16,000 feet down. An underwater robot called a Kongsberg HUGIN 6000 found it with sonar. This robot works by itself, with nobody steering it. The shape looked like a Lockheed Model 10 Electra. It was about the right size. Some experts thought they could see wings and the body of a plane.

The head of DSV is Tony Romeo, who once worked in intelligence for the US Air Force. He said the shape was in the general area where the crash-and-sink theory puts the plane. News of the find went around the world. People hoped the mystery might be solved at last.

But experts said to be careful. Sonar pictures from 16,000 feet down are never clear. The shape could be a natural rock. It could be the wreck of a different plane or ship. It could even be a fault in the sonar picture. In early 2026, nobody had yet seen the shape with a camera, and the search went on.

The second big idea is very different. It says that Earhart and Noonan lived through the flight but were caught by the Japanese military. This would have happened in the Marshall Islands. Japan ran those islands at the time, under a mandate from the League of Nations. A mandate was permission to govern a place.

In this story, the two fliers crashed or landed in the Marshall Islands. Japanese forces thought they were American spies and took them prisoner. They were moved to Saipan in the Mariana Islands. There they were kept in prison and perhaps put to death.

The idea rests on stories that people told. Some people from Saipan and the Marshall Islands spoke up many years later. They said they had seen or heard about a white woman pilot held by the Japanese in the late 1930s.

This idea got a lot of attention in 2017. The History Channel showed a film called “Amelia Earhart: The Lost Evidence.” It showed a photograph. The film said the photo showed Earhart and Noonan alive on Jaluit Atoll in the Marshall Islands, after they vanished.

A retired federal agent named Les Kinney had found the black-and-white photo in the US National Archives. It seemed to show a woman with short hair and a man on a dock beside a Japanese ship. There was also a blurred shape on a barge, a flat boat. Some people thought the shape was the Electra.

But within days, other researchers proved the claim wrong. They found the same photo in a Japanese travel book from 1935. That was two full years before Earhart vanished. So it could not show her in 1937. The History Channel then took the film off its schedule and said it had made a mistake.

Another form of this idea goes even further. It says Earhart was a secret spy for the US government. In this story she worked for President Franklin D. Roosevelt, who was her friend. Her flight around the world was only a cover. Her real job was to photograph Japanese military bases in the Pacific.

People who believe this point to how much the government helped her. It built the runway on Howland Island and sent the Itasca. They say this was more help than an ordinary record flight would get. But nobody has ever found a document that supports the spy idea. Most historians see it as a guess.

There is an even stranger idea. The writer Joe Klaas made it popular in his 1970 book Amelia Earhart Lives. He said Earhart lived through her time as a prisoner and came back to the United States with a new name. She then lived as Irene Bolam, a housewife in New Jersey, until she died in 1982.

Bolam said firmly that this was not true, and she even went to court over it. Later scientific tests proved for certain that the idea was wrong.

One of the most interesting clues is called “Betty’s Notebook.” People still argue about it. In July 1937, Betty Klenck was a 15-year-old girl in St. Petersburg, Florida. She said she heard calls for help from Amelia Earhart on her family’s shortwave radio.

Klenck said she heard a woman’s voice. The woman was upset and was begging for help. She said she was Earhart. She said she had run out of fuel and was stuck on land near water. She said the man with her was hurt.

Klenck wrote what she heard in a notebook. She kept it for more than sixty years before she showed it to researchers. TIGHAR says her story fits the Nikumaroro idea. Earhart may have landed there and called for help for several days, until the radio died.

Doubters point to three problems. Shortwave radio across 5,000 miles of ocean works very badly. There is no way to check that the voice was really Earhart’s. And the notebook was not shared until many years later. So the story is not proven. But it is hard to forget. It may show the last hours of a woman who would not give up.

The mystery still holds people, more than 80 years later. This is not because the answer is likely to be magic or a secret plot. It is because the story is so human. A brave woman tried to do something that seemed impossible. She flew out over the empty Pacific, and she did not come back.

The crash-and-sink theory is still the most likely answer. The Electra ran out of fuel and came down in the sea within 100 miles of Howland Island. The water there was too deep for the tools of 1937.

The Nikumaroro idea keeps hope alive that proof may still be found on a lonely island. The sonar shape that Deep Sea Vision found in 2024 gives another hope. Modern tools may solve what the tools of 1937 could not.

The ideas about Japanese capture have very little good evidence. But they show how tense that time was. They also show that people did not want to believe a hero could simply vanish.

One question keeps the mystery alive. She was so famous. Her plane was so well prepared, and her flight was so carefully planned. How could she just disappear?

The most likely answer is simple. The Pacific Ocean is very, very big. Even the most famous people on Earth are small beside it. Somewhere in the Pacific, under miles of water, the answer may be waiting. We just have not found it yet.
