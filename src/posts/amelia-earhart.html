Amelia Earhart: 88 Years Later, Are We Close to Finding Her Plane?

Amelia Earhart and her Lockheed Electra, shortly before the 1937 around-the-world attempt that would become aviation's greatest mystery

It was the morning of July 2, 1937. A famous voice came over the radio in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. The voice belonged to Amelia Earhart. She was flying her silver plane, a Lockheed Model 10 Electra, somewhere over the empty sea.

Her message went to a US Coast Guard ship called the Itasca. The ship was waiting near tiny Howland Island. “We are on the line 157-337,” she said. “We are running north and south.” Earlier she had said, “We must be on you, but cannot see you — gas is running low.”

The crew of the Itasca looked hard into the bright sky. But the sky stayed empty. Her voice grew weaker with each message. Then it stopped.

Earhart, her navigator Fred Noonan, and their plane were gone. A navigator is the person who works out the route. They had vanished over the Pacific, an ocean so large that it covers about one-third of the Earth.

The United States Navy began a search that was the costliest and biggest of its kind up to that time. Ships and planes searched a huge area of ocean until July 19. The search cost $4 million, which is about $90 million in today’s money.

They found nothing. There was no piece of the plane, no oil on the water, and no life raft. There was no sign of the world’s most famous woman pilot. More than 80 years later, it is still one of the great unsolved mysteries of the twentieth century. People have studied it, argued about it, and made up wild stories about it.

The mystery is bigger because of who she was. Amelia Mary Earhart was born in 1897. She was not only a pilot. She was known all over the world. At that time, women were still fighting for basic rights. To many people, she stood for the courage and skill of women.

In 1932, she became the first woman to fly alone across the Atlantic Ocean. That was five years after Charles Lindbergh’s famous flight. She set records for height and for speed. She wrote bestselling books and designed clothes. She worked hard to help other women become pilots.

Her flight around the world in 1937 was meant to be her greatest success. The route was 29,000 miles long and followed the equator, the line around the middle of the Earth. She hoped to be the first woman to fly around the world.

On June 29, 1937, she reached Lae in New Guinea. By then she had flown about 22,000 miles. Only 2,556 miles of ocean lay between her and Howland Island. Howland is a tiny island, and it is one of the loneliest pieces of land on Earth. She planned to get more fuel there. Then she would fly on to Hawaii and home.

This part of the trip should have been normal. Instead, it became one of the most famous missing-person cases in the history of flying.

Many things went wrong on the way to Howland. There were problems with the plane, with finding the way, and with the radio.

The Electra had the number NR16020. It was an all-metal plane with two engines and one pair of wings. It had been changed a lot for this trip. Extra fuel tanks filled the cabin. The plane left Lae with about 1,100 gallons of fuel. The flight to Howland was 2,556 miles and was planned to take about 20 hours. That left little room for mistakes.

Howland Island was a very hard place to find. It was only about 1.2 miles long, a third of a mile wide, and very low. It was a dot of coral and sand in a huge blue sea. A pilot could hardly see it from the air.

There was nothing tall on the island to look for.

The radio problems were serious. The Itasca was there to help her with radio. A bearing is a direction to fly, and she hoped to get one from the ship.

Earhart spoke on a channel called 3105 kHz. The Itasca could hear her, but its direction finder could not tune in to that channel. Yet she never seemed to hear the ship’s messages to her. Experts still argue about exactly why.

The crew said her voice was very loud. That meant she was quite close. But she said that she could not hear the ship. So the Itasca could not give her the bearings she needed to find Howland.

In her last message she said she was flying along a line called 157/337 degrees. That line passes through Howland. Then there was silence.

The official view, and the one most experts accept, is called the “crash and sink” theory. The plane ran out of fuel and came down in the sea near Howland Island. Then it sank in very deep water. The Coast Guard thought she ran out of fuel north of the island.

The best-known other idea is about an island called Nikumaroro. It used to be called Gardner Island. It is a coral island in the Phoenix Islands, and nobody lives there. It lies about 350 nautical miles south-southeast of Howland.

A group called the International Group for Historic Aircraft Recovery supports this idea. Its short name is TIGHAR, and Ric Gillespie leads it. They think Earhart and Noonan could not find Howland and were low on fuel. So they turned southeast, along the 157-degree line from the last message. They flew toward the Phoenix Islands and landed on a flat coral reef near Nikumaroro.

In this story, the plane landed safely on the reef when the sea was low. Earhart and Noonan lived for days, or perhaps weeks, on the island. They sent calls for help from the plane’s radio. Then the rising sea pulled the plane off the reef and into the ocean.

Some exciting finds have kept this idea alive. In 1940, a British officer named Gerald Gallagher found part of a human skeleton on Nikumaroro. He said it was possibly a woman. He also found a box for a sextant. A sextant is a tool that sailors and pilots use to find their position.

Gallagher reported the find to his bosses. The bones were sent to Fiji to be studied. A doctor there decided in 1941 that they belonged to a man over the age of 45.

In 1998, other experts looked again at the doctor’s measurements. They said the bones might fit a woman of European family. A review in 2015 favored the doctor’s first answer. Experts still disagree. The bones were lost, so nobody can test them with modern DNA methods.

TIGHAR has made about a dozen trips to Nikumaroro since the late 1980s. Its teams have brought back many objects. One is a small jar that TIGHAR links to a skin cream Earhart used. There are bits of aluminum and pieces of a woman’s shoe from the 1930s. But none of these finds proves that Earhart was there. In 2017, a museum said one aluminum panel matched a different kind of plane.

In 2012, a TIGHAR team used sonar on the sea floor near the reef. Sonar makes pictures with sound. It showed a strange shape that some people thought could be part of a plane. But the picture was not clear, and nothing was proven.

A big test of the Nikumaroro idea came in 2019. Robert Ballard led a trip to the island, with National Geographic. Ballard is the ocean scientist who found the wreck of the Titanic in 1985. He said he was going with the idea that she landed there.

He brought deep-sea tools. They included underwater robots called ROVs, which people steer from a ship. The robots searched the sea floor around the island.

Ballard’s team searched with great care. They found no sign of the Lockheed Electra. The team did not find a wreck or any part that surely came from Earhart’s plane. This did not prove the idea wrong, but it did not help it either.

In January 2024, there was new excitement. A company from South Carolina called Deep Sea Vision, or DSV, builds sea robots. It said an underwater robot had found a strange shape on the floor of the Pacific. The shape was in the sea west of Howland Island.

The shape lay very deep. The robot works by itself, with nobody steering it. In the sonar picture, the shape looked a little like a Lockheed Model 10 Electra.

The head of DSV is Tony Romeo, who once worked in intelligence for the US Air Force. News of the find went around the world. People hoped the mystery might be solved at last.

But experts said to be careful. Sonar pictures from so deep are not clear. In November 2024, DSV took a sharper picture. It showed that the shape was only a natural rock formation, not the plane. The company said its search would go on.

The second big idea is very different. It says that Earhart and Noonan lived through the flight but were caught by the Japanese military. This would have happened in the Marshall Islands. Japan ran those islands at the time, under a mandate from the League of Nations. A mandate was permission to govern a place.

In this story, the two fliers crashed or landed in the Marshall Islands. Japanese forces thought they were American spies and took them prisoner. They were moved to Saipan in the Mariana Islands. There they were kept in prison and perhaps put to death.

The idea rests on stories that people told. Some people from Saipan spoke up many years later. They said they had seen or heard about a white woman pilot held by the Japanese. Nobody has been able to confirm these stories.

This idea got a lot of attention in 2017. The History Channel showed a film called “Amelia Earhart: The Lost Evidence.” It showed a photograph. The film said the photo showed Earhart and Noonan alive on Jaluit Atoll in the Marshall Islands, after they vanished.

A retired federal agent named Les Kinney had found the black-and-white photo in the US National Archives. It seemed to show a woman with short hair and a man on a dock beside a Japanese ship. There was also a blurred shape on a barge, a flat boat. Some people thought the shape was the Electra.

But within days, a Japanese researcher named Kota Yamano found the same photo in a Japanese travel book from 1935. That was two years before Earhart vanished. So it could not show her in 1937.

Another form of this idea goes even further. It says Earhart was a secret spy for the US government. This story grew after a 1943 film, Flight for Freedom. By 1949, US Army Intelligence had found the rumor groundless. In the story, her flight around the world was only a cover. Her real job was said to be photographing Japanese military bases in the Pacific.

People who believe this point to how much the government helped her. It sent the Itasca and prepared Howland Island for her. They say this was more help than an ordinary record flight would get. But nobody has ever found a document that supports the spy idea. Most historians see it as a guess.

There is an even stranger idea. The writer Joe Klaas made it popular in his 1970 book Amelia Earhart Lives. He said Earhart lived through her time as a prisoner and came back to the United States with a new name. She then lived as Irene Bolam, a banker in New York.

Bolam said firmly that this was not true, and she took the writer’s publisher to court. The publisher pulled the book. Later research, including a 2006 National Geographic study, ruled the idea out.

One of the most interesting clues is called “Betty’s Notebook.” People still argue about it. In July 1937, Betty Klenck was a young girl in St. Petersburg, Florida. She later said she heard calls for help from Amelia Earhart on her family’s shortwave radio.

Klenck said she heard a woman’s voice. The woman was upset and was begging for help. She said she was Earhart. She said she had run out of fuel and was stuck on land near water. She said the man with her was hurt.

Klenck said she wrote what she heard in a notebook. TIGHAR says her story fits the Nikumaroro idea. Earhart may have landed there and called for help for several days, until the radio died.

Doubters point to problems. Shortwave radio over such a long way is not reliable. There is no way to check that the voice was really Earhart’s. And the story was told many years later. So it is not proven. If it were true, it would show the last hours of a woman who would not give up.

The mystery still holds people, more than 80 years later. This is not because the answer is likely to be magic or a secret plot. It is because the story is so human. A brave woman tried to do something that seemed impossible. She flew out over the empty Pacific, and she did not come back.

The crash-and-sink theory is still the official view. The Electra ran out of fuel and came down in the sea near Howland Island. The water there was too deep for the tools of 1937.

The Nikumaroro idea keeps hope alive that proof may still be found on a lonely island. Modern tools may solve what the tools of 1937 could not.

The ideas about Japanese capture have very little good evidence. But they show how tense that time was. They also show that people did not want to believe a hero could simply vanish.

One question keeps the mystery alive. She was so famous. Her plane was so well prepared, and her flight was so carefully planned. How could she just disappear?

The simple answer may be this. The Pacific Ocean is very, very big. Even the most famous people on Earth are small beside it. Somewhere in the Pacific, under miles of water, the answer may be waiting. We just have not found it yet.
