Amelia Earhart: 88 Years Later, Are We Close to Finding Her Plane?

Amelia Earhart and her Lockheed Electra, shortly before the 1937 around-the-world attempt that would become aviation's greatest mystery

A voice over the Pacific

It was the morning of July 2, 1937. A famous voice came over the radio in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. The voice belonged to Amelia Earhart. She was flying her plane, a Lockheed Model 10 Electra, somewhere over the empty sea.

Her message went to a US Coast Guard ship called the Itasca. The ship was waiting near tiny Howland Island. “We are on the line 157-337,” she said. “We are running north and south.” Earlier she had said, “We must be on you, but cannot see you — gas is running low.”

The crew of the Itasca watched the sky. But the sky stayed empty. Then it stopped.

Earhart, her navigator Fred Noonan, and their plane were gone. A navigator is the person who works out the route. They had vanished over the Pacific, an ocean so large that it covers about one-third of the Earth. More than 80 years later, it is still one of the great unsolved mysteries of the twentieth century. So who was this woman, and what went wrong?

The most famous woman pilot

Amelia Mary Earhart was born in 1897. She was not only a pilot. She was known all over the world. At that time, women were still fighting for basic rights. To many people, she stood for the courage and skill of women.

In 1932, she became the first woman to fly alone across the Atlantic Ocean. That was five years after Charles Lindbergh’s famous flight. She worked hard to help other women become pilots.

Her flight around the world in 1937 was meant to be her greatest success. The route was 29,000 miles long and followed the equator, the line around the middle of the Earth. She hoped to be the first woman to fly around the world.

On June 29, 1937, she reached Lae in New Guinea. By then she had flown about 22,000 miles. Only 2,556 miles of ocean lay between her and Howland Island. She planned to get more fuel there.

The loneliest island on Earth

Many things went wrong on the way to Howland. There were problems with the plane, with finding the way, and with the radio.

The Electra had been changed a lot for this trip, and extra fuel tanks filled the cabin. The plane left Lae with about 1,100 gallons of fuel. The flight was planned to take about 20 hours. That left little room for mistakes.

Howland Island was a very hard place to find. It was only about 1.2 miles long, a third of a mile wide, and very low. It was a dot of coral and sand in a huge blue sea.

The radio problems were serious. Earhart spoke on a channel called 3105 kHz. The Itasca could hear her, but its direction finder could not tune in to that channel. Yet she never seemed to hear the ship’s messages to her. Experts still argue about exactly why.

The crew said her voice was very loud. That meant she was quite close. But she said that she could not hear the ship. So the Itasca could not give her the bearings she needed to find Howland. A bearing is a direction to fly. Then there was silence.

The United States Navy began a search that was the costliest and biggest of its kind up to that time. Ships and planes searched a huge area of ocean until July 19. The search cost $4 million, which is about $90 million in today’s money.

They found nothing. There was no piece of the plane, no oil on the water, and no life raft. There was no sign of the world’s most famous woman pilot.

So people began to ask what had happened. Over the years, three main answers have grown. The first is the simplest.

Idea one: she came down in the sea

The official view, and the one most experts accept, is called the “crash and sink” theory. The plane ran out of fuel and came down in the sea near Howland Island. Then it sank in very deep water. The Coast Guard thought she ran out of fuel north of the island.

The water there was too deep for the tools of 1937. In January 2024, there was new excitement. A company from South Carolina called Deep Sea Vision, or DSV, builds sea robots. It said that a robot had found a strange shape on the floor of the Pacific, west of Howland Island. In the sonar picture, the shape looked a little like a Lockheed Model 10 Electra.

News of the find went around the world. But experts said to be careful. In November 2024, DSV took a sharper picture. It showed that the shape was only a natural rock formation, not the plane.

Idea two: the island of Nikumaroro

The best-known other idea is about an island called Nikumaroro. It used to be called Gardner Island. It is a coral island in the Phoenix Islands, and nobody lives there. It lies about 350 nautical miles south-southeast of Howland.

A group called the International Group for Historic Aircraft Recovery supports this idea. Its short name is TIGHAR, and Ric Gillespie leads it. They think Earhart and Noonan could not find Howland and were low on fuel. So they turned southeast, along the 157-degree line from the last message. They flew toward the Phoenix Islands and landed on a flat coral reef near Nikumaroro. They lived for days, or perhaps weeks, on the island. Then the rising sea pulled the plane off the reef and into the ocean.

Some finds have kept this idea alive. In 1940, a British officer named Gerald Gallagher found part of a human skeleton on Nikumaroro. He also found a box for a sextant, a tool that sailors and pilots use to find their position. The bones were sent to Fiji. A doctor there decided in 1941 that they belonged to a man over the age of 45.

In 1998, other experts looked again and said the bones might fit a woman. A review in 2015 favored the doctor’s first answer. The bones were lost, so nobody can test them with modern DNA methods.

TIGHAR has made about a dozen trips to the island since the late 1980s. Its teams have brought back many objects. But none of these finds proves that Earhart was there. In 2017, a museum said one aluminum panel matched a different kind of plane.

A big test came in 2019. Robert Ballard led a trip to the island, with National Geographic. Ballard is the ocean scientist who found the wreck of the Titanic in 1985. He went with the idea that she landed there. His underwater robots searched the sea floor around the island. They found no sign of the Electra. This did not prove the idea wrong, but it did not help it either.

Idea three: captured by Japan

The third idea is very different. It says that Earhart and Noonan lived through the flight but were caught by the Japanese military in the Marshall Islands. Japan ran those islands at the time. In this story, the fliers were taken for American spies and moved to Saipan in the Mariana Islands.

The idea rests on stories that people told. Some people from Saipan spoke up many years later. They said they had seen or heard about a white woman pilot held by the Japanese. Nobody has been able to confirm these stories.

In 2017, the History Channel showed a film that used a photograph. The film said that it showed Earhart and Noonan alive on Jaluit Atoll after they vanished. But within days, a Japanese researcher named Kota Yamano found the same photo in a Japanese travel book from 1935. That was two years before Earhart vanished. So it could not show her in 1937.

Other ideas go further. One says Earhart was a secret spy for the US government. By 1949, US Army Intelligence had found the rumor groundless. A book from 1970, the 1970 book Amelia Earhart Lives, said that she lived as a New York banker named Irene Bolam. Bolam said firmly that this was not true, and a 2006 National Geographic study ruled the idea out.

Betty’s Notebook

One clue is still argued about. In July 1937, Betty Klenck was a young girl in St. Petersburg, Florida. She later said she heard calls for help on her family’s shortwave radio. She said a woman gave her name as Earhart, said she had run out of fuel, and was stuck on land near water.

Klenck said she wrote what she heard in a notebook. TIGHAR says her story fits the Nikumaroro idea. Doubters say that nobody can check that the voice was really Earhart’s. And the story was told many years later.

The ocean is very big

So what do we know? The Electra left Lae and did not reach Howland. The Navy found nothing. The crash-and-sink view is still the official one. The Nikumaroro idea keeps hope alive that proof may still be found on a lonely island. The ideas about Japanese capture have very little good evidence.

Think of that morning of July 2 again. A crew looked at an empty sky and listened to a fading voice. How could such a famous woman just disappear? The simple answer may be this. The Pacific Ocean is very, very big. Even the most famous people on Earth are small beside it. Somewhere in the Pacific, under miles of water, the answer may be waiting. We just have not found it yet.
