The Mandela Effect: Why Millions of People Remember a History That Never Happened

A funeral that never happened

Fiona Broome is a paranormal researcher. She says that around 2009 or 2010, she was at a convention called Dragon Con. She talked with others about a vivid memory. She remembered Nelson Mandela dying in prison in the 1980s. She remembered the news coverage, the funeral, and the mourning. She was certain.

She was certain, and she was wrong. Mandela did not die in prison. So where did such a clear memory come from? And why did other people have it too?

What really happened to Mandela

Let us start with the facts. Nelson Mandela was released from prison on February 11, 1990. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993. He was elected President of South Africa in 1994 and served until 1999. He died on December 5, 2013, at the age of 95.

The funeral that Broome remembered never happened. But she was not alone. She wrote about her experience online and found that many other people shared the same false memory. They also recalled news reports and memorial services for Mandela’s death, decades before it happened.

Broome gave this a name: the Mandela Effect. It is when a large group of people share a vivid, confident, and completely false memory of an event, detail, or fact.

It began as a curious story about one man’s death. Since then, many people have found that their memories of movies, brand logos, and cartoon characters are wrong. And many others share the same wrong memories.

Berenstain, and a missing monocle

The best way to understand the effect is through examples. They are not obscure facts that a few people get wrong. They are widely shared false memories, and many people who learn the truth feel that something is wrong.

Perhaps the most famous example is the Berenstain Bears. This children’s book series was created by Stan and Jan Berenstain. It has been published as The Berenstain Bears from the start. But many people remember the name as “Berenstein,” ending in “-ein” and not “-ain.”

The memory is often very specific. Some people say that they recall the spelling on book covers they read as children. But the real name has always been Berenstain, matching the authors’ real surname.

Then there is a movie misquote. In The Empire Strikes Back (1980), Darth Vader tells Luke Skywalker who he really is. Many people remember the line as “Luke, I am your father.” The real line is “No, I am your father.” Vader never says “Luke” in that scene.

In Disney’s Snow White (1937), the real line is “Magic mirror on the wall,” not “Mirror, mirror on the wall.” The “mirror, mirror” version is how many people tell the old fairy tale.

Some Mandela Effects are about pictures. Quick, picture the Monopoly Man, the mustached mascot of the classic board game. Does he wear a monocle? A monocle is a single eyeglass lens. If you said yes, you are in good company, and you are wrong. Rich Uncle Pennybags has never worn a monocle.

Many people also remember Curious George with a tail, but he has no tail. And many remember the Fruit of the Loom label with a cornucopia, a horn full of fruit. The label shows only a pile of fruit. Others remember a peanut butter brand called “Jiffy,” but the brands are Jif and Skippy.

These examples are interesting because people see such logos so often. Yet many of us remember them wrongly. So what is going on? Let us look at three ideas, starting with the boldest.

Idea one: another universe

Not everyone accepts a simple explanation. For some people, the Mandela Effect feels too specific, too widely shared, and too “real” to be just bad memory.

One popular idea is the parallel universes, or multiverse, theory. It says that the effect happens when a person or a group shifts from one universe to another. By this idea, the false memories are not false at all. They are true memories of events in a different reality. In that reality, Mandela died in prison, the Bears were Berenstein, and Vader said “Luke.”

The idea borrows from real physics, from a theory called the many-worlds interpretation. Hugh Everett III proposed it in 1957. It suggests that every possible result of every event happens in some branch of reality.

But there is a big leap from “the math allows parallel universes” to “you remember Berenstein Bears because you came from a universe where they were spelled that way.” No evidence supports that leap.

Idea two: a glitch or an experiment

Other ideas go further still. One of the most elaborate involves CERN and its Large Hadron Collider. It claims that particle physics experiments changed the fabric of reality. They made small changes to the past, which now show up as Mandela Effects.

Another is the simulation hypothesis. The philosopher Nick Bostrom wrote a well-known paper about it in 2003. It says that our reality may be a computer simulation, and Mandela Effects are “glitches” in the code.

Neither theory has any proof to support it. Yet many people online have embraced such ideas. That shows how strongly we like explanations that put the blame outside ourselves. The fault, they say, lies not in our brains but in reality itself.

The main scientific answer looks inward instead.

Idea three: memory rebuilds itself

The main scientific explanation comes from cognitive psychology. Cognitive psychology is the study of how the mind thinks and remembers. One key figure is Dr. Elisabeth Loftus, a professor at the University of California, Irvine. She has spent decades studying how easily human memory can change.

Loftus’s work suggests that memory is not a recording device. It is a process of rebuilding. Each time you recall an event, your brain puts it together from fragments. It fills the gaps with guesses, expectations, and facts learned later.

In her “Lost in the Mall” study (1995), Loftus and her colleague Jacqueline Pickrell tested this. About 25 percent of the 24 people in the study came to “remember” a made-up event from childhood. The event was being lost in a shopping mall. Some of them believed that they remembered it, and some added details of their own.

In a 1974 study, Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of car crashes. Students asked about cars that “smashed” into each other guessed higher speeds than students asked about cars that “hit” each other. Some even “remembered” broken glass that was not there.

If a person can be led to build a false memory, groups of people may build shared false memories too. Repetition and media may help.

How a false memory takes shape

Psychologists suggest several ways that Mandela Effects may arise. The first is the schema, a mental framework that helps us sort information. Your schema for a “rich old man in a top hat” includes a monocle. So your brain adds one to the Monopoly Man. Some writers also think that people mix him up with Mr. Peanut, who wears a monocle.

The second is the source monitoring error. You remember information correctly but link it to the wrong source. You may have heard “Luke, I am your father” in a parody, from a friend, or in a meme. Your brain then files it as an actual movie quote.

The third is social reinforcement. When you talk about a false memory with others who share it, your shared confidence makes it feel more real. The fourth is confabulation. It happens when the brain puts believable details into a memory to make a complete story.

Many psychologists think that the brain likes a story that holds together more than an exact record.

Can you trust what you remember?

So what do we know? Mandela did not die in prison. The Bears are the Berenstain Bears. Vader says “No, I am your father.” Most scientists think that these false memories show something remarkable about the mind. Many people, working on their own, can settle on the same wrong version of events. And once that version is fixed, it can be hard to shift.

What is open? The multiverse and simulation ideas have no proof, and nobody has shown that they are true. The Mandela Effect is not really a mystery of the outside world. It is a mystery of the inside one.

Think again of Fiona Broome at her convention, so sure of a funeral. Science suggests that memory can be wrong in surprising ways. Can you trust what you remember?
