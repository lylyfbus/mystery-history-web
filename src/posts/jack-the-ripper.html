Jack the Ripper: The Victorian Killer Whose Identity Remains a Mystery

A shape in the dark

It was very early on August 31, 1888. A cart driver named Charles Allen Cross was walking to work in London. His way took him along Buck’s Row, a dark and narrow street in a poor area called Whitechapel. Near a gate, he saw a shape on the ground. He thought it was an old sheet of cloth.

It was not cloth. It was a woman, and she was dead. Her name was Mary Ann Nichols, and her friends called her Polly. She was about 43 years old. Someone had killed her with a knife.

In the next ten weeks, four more women died in the same small part of London. The killer was never caught. You probably know the name that people gave him: Jack the Ripper. But nobody has ever found his real name. Let us look at why.

Two Londons

To understand this story, we must first visit the place. In the 1880s, London was like two cities. The West End was rich and bright. The East End was very different. Whitechapel and the streets around it were crowded and poor.

About 80,000 people lived in the Whitechapel area, and many had almost nothing. Imagine that you have no home. Each evening, you must find four pence to pay for a bed in a cheap lodging house. That is how some people there lived. The area was known for crime and dirt.

The five women were among the poorest people in this poor place. Each one had lost a husband or a partner, through death or because the marriage had ended. Each one did any work she could find to pay for a bed. And they had nobody to protect them.

Five women in ten weeks

It is easy to remember the killer and forget the women. So let us learn their names.

Mary Ann Nichols was the first. Annie Chapman was the second, and she was 47. She was found at about 6:00 in the morning on September 8, in the yard behind 29 Hanbury Street.

Then came the night of September 30. Elizabeth Stride was found at about 1:00 in the morning in a place called Dutfield’s Yard. About three-quarters of an hour later, Catherine Eddowes was found nearby, in a small, dark square called Mitre Square. People call that night the “Double Event.” Many experts think that someone disturbed the killer the first time, and he ran away.

The fifth woman was Mary Jane Kelly. She was about 25, the youngest of the five. On November 9, a man named Thomas Bowyer came to her small room at 13 Miller’s Court to collect the rent. He found her there. She was the only one of the five who was killed indoors.

After that, the killings seemed to stop. Nobody knows why.

Experts call these women the “Canonical Five.” This means the five victims that most experts link to one killer. But even this is not certain. Some experts think that the killer had other victims, or that not all five were his.

So London had a killer with no face. Soon he would get a name.

A name from a letter

During that autumn, hundreds of letters arrived at police stations and newspapers. Many of them said that they came from the killer. Most were jokes or lies. But three became famous.

The first is called the “Dear Boss” letter. It was written on September 25, and it reached a London news office two days later. It was signed “Yours truly, Jack the Ripper.” This was the first time that anyone used that name.

The second was a postcard that arrived on October 1. It is known as the “Saucy Jacky” postcard, and it made fun of the police.

The third was the most frightening. It is called the “From Hell” letter. It was sent on October 16 to George Lusk, the leader of a group of local men who were hunting for the killer. A small box came with it. Inside the box was half of a human kidney. Some people believed that it came from Catherine Eddowes. Others think that it was a cruel joke.

Now here is the surprising part. The name that the whole world knows probably did not come from the killer. Police later said that a newspaper writer made up the “Dear Boss” letter. A reporter named Fred Best is said to have confessed to this in 1931.

So the letters gave us a name, but not a person. Who did people suspect?

Suspects who do not fit

Since 1888, people have named more than one hundred suspects. The list has doctors, butchers, artists, teachers, and even a prince. Let us start with three famous ones.

The prince was Albert Victor, a grandson of Queen Victoria. This idea became well known in 1970. But the prince had alibis. An alibi is proof that a person was in a different place at the time of a crime. And a man who told a story about a royal plot later said that it was a hoax.

The artist was Walter Sickert, a well-known painter. The crime writer Patricia Cornwell named him in her 2002 book Portrait of a Killer. She reportedly bought 31 of his paintings. But there is strong evidence that Sickert was in France during most of the murders. Most researchers reject her case.

Then there is James Maybrick, a cotton trader from Liverpool. In the 1990s, a diary appeared that seemed to be his confession. That sounds like the end of the mystery. But in 1995, a man named Michael Barrett said that he had forged the diary. Experts who studied it rejected it.

A prince, a painter, and a secret diary make exciting stories. But exciting is not the same as true. The police of 1888 had other names.

The names the police had

The first was Montague John Druitt, a lawyer and teacher. He was found dead in the River Thames on December 31, 1888. A senior police officer, Sir Melville Macnaghten, named him as one of three likely suspects in a report in 1894. But there is a problem. On the day of the first murder, Druitt was in Dorset, far from Whitechapel.

The second was George Chapman, who was born in Poland. Years later, he poisoned three women who lived with him as his wives, and he was hanged in 1903. Detective Inspector Frederick Abberline came from Scotland Yard to help with the hunt. He reportedly thought that Chapman was the Ripper. But critics say that poison does not fit the Ripper’s methods.

The third was Aaron Kosminski, a Polish Jewish barber who lived in Whitechapel. Two senior police officers named him as a suspect. He was never charged. In 1891, he was sent to an asylum, which was a hospital for people with serious mental illness.

In 2014, the writer Russell Edwards made a bold claim. He said that DNA on an old shawl proved that Kosminski was the killer. DNA is the code inside every living cell, and it is different for each person. But many experts doubt this claim. Nobody can prove where the shawl came from, and the tests have been strongly criticized.

So each name has a problem. No theory has enough evidence to settle the case.

Why was he never caught?

You may wonder how a killer could escape in the middle of a great city. Think about the tools that detectives have today, such as fingerprints, DNA, and computer maps. The police of 1888 could not use them.

So they did what they had always done. They talked to witnesses. They knocked on doors, house after house. They looked for men who fitted a description. History books say that they had too little evidence, some wrong information, and false stories from witnesses.

There was a second problem. London had two police forces. Four of the murders happened in the area of the Metropolitan Police. But Mitre Square belonged to the City of London Police. So two forces had to work on the same case.

The people of Whitechapel tried to help. Local men formed a group called the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, and it offered a reward of 50 pounds.

At the same time, daily newspapers had become cheap, and many people could buy them. The papers printed frightening stories and copies of the letters. People in the East End were afraid. A rumor named a man called “Leather Apron.” A Jewish worker named John Pizer was arrested, but he was later let go.

The street today

The case is still open. Nobody was ever charged, put on trial, or found guilty. Much of the evidence is lost or too damaged to test, and every witness died long ago. A crime that the police could not solve is called a cold case. This is one of the best-known cold cases in history.

Buck’s Row has a new name now. It is called Durward Street, and new buildings stand where the old ones were. But at night, walking tours still go through Whitechapel. In 2015, a Jack the Ripper Museum opened on Cable Street.

Not everyone thinks that this is right. Some people ask a hard question. Is it fair to make money from the deaths of five poor women? Women protested at the museum. Other people answer that the tours teach visitors about the East End and about how hard life was there. The argument has not ended.

What do you think? Perhaps the fairest thing that we can do is to remember the five women. They were Mary Ann, Annie, Elizabeth, Catherine, and Mary Jane. We know their names. We will probably never know his.
