Jack the Ripper: The Victorian Killer Whose Identity Remains a Mystery

It was very early on August 31, 1888. A cart driver named Charles Allen Cross was walking to work in London. His path took him along Buck’s Row, a dark and narrow street in a poor area called Whitechapel. He saw a shape on the ground near a gate. He thought it was an old sheet of cloth.

It was not cloth. It was the body of a woman. Her name was Mary Ann Nichols, and she was 43 years old. Someone had killed her with a knife.

She was the first of five women that most experts link to one killer. They are known as the “Canonical Five.” All five died within about ten weeks. The killer was never caught. Today the world knows him by one name: Jack the Ripper.

The name came from a letter. Someone sent it to a London news office in September 1888. It was signed “Yours truly, Jack the Ripper.” The letter was probably a trick by a newspaper writer. But the name stayed. More than 130 years later, people still ask the same question. Who was he?

To understand the case, you need to know the place. In the 1880s, London was like two cities. The West End was rich and bright. The East End was very different. Whitechapel, Spitalfields, and Aldgate were crowded and poor.

About 900,000 people lived in the East End, and many had almost nothing. Some slept in cheap lodging houses. A bed for one night cost four pence, and people shared the room with strangers. The streets had few lamps and few police officers. Crime and sickness were common.

The five women were among the poorest people in this poor place. Most of them were widows or lived apart from their husbands. They did any work they could find to pay for a bed each night. They had no one to protect them. And the richer people of London mostly looked away.

The murders changed that. For the first time, many people in Britain had to look at how the East End lived. Arguments about poor housing, weak policing, and fairness went on for many years.

The first victim was Mary Ann Nichols. Her friends called her Polly. She was found in Buck’s Row at about 3:40 in the morning on August 31, 1888. Today that street is called Durward Street.

The second victim was Annie Chapman, who was 47. She was found at about 6:00 in the morning on September 8, in the yard behind 29 Hanbury Street. This time the killer had also cut the body open and taken a part of it away. That was a new and strange detail. Some doctors thought it showed that the killer knew something about the human body.

The third and fourth murders happened on the same night, September 30, 1888. People call it the “Double Event.” Elizabeth Stride, aged 44, was found at about 1:00 in the morning in Dutfield’s Yard, near Berner Street. She had only one wound. The police believed that someone had disturbed the killer, and he had run away.

Less than an hour later, a second woman was found about a mile away. She was Catherine Eddowes, aged 46. She lay in Mitre Square, a small, dark yard inside the old City of London. Her wounds were much worse than the others. Again, the killer had taken parts of the body with him.

The fifth victim was Mary Jane Kelly. She was about 25, the youngest of the five. She was also the only one killed indoors. She was found on November 9, 1888, at about 10:45 in the morning. She was in her small room at 13 Miller’s Court, near Dorset Street.

A man named Thomas Bowyer found her. He worked for her landlord, and he had come to collect rent that was late. This murder was the most violent of all. The killer had been alone in the room, with no one to stop him.

After Mary Jane Kelly, the killings seemed to stop. Nobody knows why. That question is one more part of the mystery.

During that autumn, hundreds of letters arrived at police stations and newspapers. Each one said it came from the killer. Most were jokes or lies. But three became famous.

The first was the “Dear Boss” letter. It reached the Central News Agency on September 27, 1888. It was written in red ink, and it was the first to use the name “Jack the Ripper.” Most experts now think a newspaper writer made it up to sell more papers.

The second was the “Saucy Jacky” postcard. It arrived on October 1, 1888. It talked about the Double Event and made fun of the police.

The third was the most frightening. It is called the “From Hell” letter. It arrived on October 16, 1888. It was sent to George Lusk, the leader of a group of local men who were hunting for the killer. With the letter came a small box. Inside was half of a human kidney.

A kidney had been taken from Catherine Eddowes, and it was never found. Some experts said the kidney in the box could have been hers. Many researchers think “From Hell” is the letter most likely to be real. But nobody can be sure.

The man who led the hunt on the streets was Inspector Frederick Abberline of Scotland Yard. He was a skilled detective, and he knew the East End well. Hundreds of police officers helped him. Local business owners formed their own group, the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee. It was one of the biggest searches of that age. Still, no one was ever charged.

Since then, people have named more than one hundred suspects. The list includes doctors, butchers, artists, teachers, and even a prince.

One suspect was Aaron Kosminski. He was a Polish Jewish barber who lived in Whitechapel. A witness pointed to him, but he was never charged. In 1891 he was sent to a hospital for people with serious mental illness.

In 2014, the writer Russell Edwards made a bold claim. He said that DNA on an old shawl proved Kosminski was the killer. DNA is the code inside every living cell, and it is different for each person. But many experts doubt the claim. Nobody can prove where the shawl came from, and the tests have been strongly criticized.

A second suspect was Montague John Druitt, a lawyer and teacher. He was found dead in the River Thames on December 31, 1888. A senior police officer, Sir Melville Macnaghten, named Druitt as his main suspect in a report in 1894. He wrote that Druitt’s own family believed he was the killer.

A third was George Chapman. He was born in Poland with the name Severin Kłosowski. He later poisoned three women who lived with him as his wives. Inspector Abberline is said to have believed that Chapman was the Ripper.

There were other famous names. Francis Tumblety was an American who sold fake medicine. He was arrested in London in 1888 for a different offence, and then he ran away to the United States.

Walter Sickert was a well-known painter. The crime writer Patricia Cornwell named him in her 2002 book Portrait of a Killer. She is said to have spent about $2 million on her search. But her case rests mostly on dark scenes in his paintings, and that is weak proof.

Some people even blamed Prince Albert Victor, a grandson of Queen Victoria. No serious historian accepts this idea, because there is no real evidence for it.

Then there is James Maybrick, a cotton trader from Liverpool. In 1992, a diary appeared that seemed to be his confession. Most experts think the diary is a modern fake.

So the suspects run from a poor barber to a prince. That shows the real problem. No theory has enough evidence to settle the case.

Why could the police not catch him? One reason is that the tools of a modern detective did not exist yet.

In 1888, the police did not use fingerprints. Scotland Yard only started to use them in 1901. DNA tests were more than a hundred years away. Nobody could yet tell one person’s blood from another’s. Cameras existed, but the pictures of crime scenes were poor.

So the police did what they had always done. They talked to witnesses. They knocked on doors, house after house. They searched for men who fitted a description.

There was another problem. London had two police forces. Four of the murders happened in the area of the Metropolitan Police. But Catherine Eddowes died in Mitre Square, which belonged to the City of London Police. The two forces had different bosses. They did not always work well together, and important facts may have been lost between them.

At the same time, the newspapers printed frightening stories every day. People in the East End were afraid. Some of them turned their anger on the Jewish families of Whitechapel. And politicians pushed the police to find an answer fast.

Did the killer have medical training? Experts have argued about this from the start, because he took parts of the body away from three of the victims.

Dr. Thomas Bond was a police surgeon who examined Kelly and studied the case of Eddowes. He said the killer had no training at all. He wrote that the cuts were rough and showed “no anatomical skill.” Anatomy is the study of how a body is built.

Dr. George Bagster Phillips examined Annie Chapman, and he did not agree. He thought the work showed real medical knowledge.

This is why people have guessed that the killer was a surgeon, a medical student, or a butcher. Most modern experts take a middle view. The killer knew enough to find what he wanted. But he was probably not a trained surgeon.

The murders happened at a special time for newspapers. In the 1880s, daily papers had become cheap, and millions of people could buy them. The Whitechapel murders gave those papers a story that nobody could stop reading. It had a hidden killer, helpless victims, and dark streets.

The papers printed every detail. They printed copies of the letters. They sent their own writers to look for clues. They demanded that the police act.

In this way, Jack the Ripper became the first criminal who was famous because of the news. The press made his name known within weeks. The picture of a man in a top hat and a black cloak, walking through the fog, also came from the newspapers. No witness ever described the killer that way.

All this attention did real harm. Crowds in the East End attacked innocent men because they thought they had found the Ripper. There was also a rumor about a Jewish worker called “Leather Apron.” It made life dangerous for Jewish people in Whitechapel.

The police looked foolish, and the government was embarrassed. In November 1888, the head of the Metropolitan Police, Sir Charles Warren, left his job.

The case also changed how we tell stories about crime. People still focus on the killer’s name, the clues, and the reasons. Today we call this kind of story “true crime.” You can find it in books, films, and podcasts everywhere. Much of it began in Whitechapel in 1888.

In London today, Jack the Ripper is big business. Dozens of walking tours go through Whitechapel at night. Guides lead visitors down the same lanes and yards. In 2015, the Jack the Ripper Museum opened on Cable Street. Hundreds of books tell the story, and so do films, television shows, and video games.

Not everyone thinks this is right. Some people ask a hard question. Is it fair to make money from the deaths of five poor women? They say the tours turn the victims into parts of a horror show and make the killer into a star.

Others answer that the tours teach visitors about the history of the East End and about how hard life was there. The argument has not ended.

The Jack the Ripper case is still unsolved. Nobody was ever charged, put on trial, or found guilty.

Science has changed a great deal since 1888. Detectives now use fingerprints, DNA, and computer maps of where crimes happen. But none of these tools has solved this case. New buildings stand on the old streets. Much of the evidence is lost or too damaged to test. Every witness died long ago.

So the best-known cold case in history stays open. A cold case is a crime that the police could not solve. Macnaghten’s report did not close it. Cornwell’s costly search did not close it. The DNA test on the shawl did not close it.

Perhaps that is why people still remember the Ripper. He is a shape in the fog and a name without a face. The fog over Whitechapel cleared a long time ago. The fog around his name never has. And the question is still the same. Who?
