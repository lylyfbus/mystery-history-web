The Wow! Signal: Did We Receive a Message From Aliens in 1977?

One word in red ink

Jerry Ehman sat at his kitchen table with a pile of computer paper. It was August 1977. Each page was full of numbers and letters, row after row. Most people would find it very boring.

Ehman was an astronomer, a scientist who studies space. He had lost his paid job on this project when the money was cut. But he still did the work in his spare time, as a volunteer.

Then he saw six characters that made him stop: 6EQUJ5. He took a red pen and drew a circle around them. In the margin, he wrote one word.

“Wow!”

What did Ehman see in those six characters? And why do scientists still talk about them today? To answer that, we must first visit a very strange telescope.

The telescope that could not move

The paper came from a radio telescope called Big Ear. It stood in the farmland of central Ohio, in the United States. A professor named John Kraus designed and built it for Ohio State University.

A radio telescope does not look at light. It listens to radio waves from space. In the 1970s, Big Ear had a special task. It listened for signals from other worlds. Scientists call this work SETI, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. In simple words, it is the hunt for a message from aliens.

Big Ear had one unusual feature. Its big reflector could not move. So how did it scan the sky? It let the Earth do the work. As the Earth turned, the sky drifted past the telescope. Each point in the sky stayed in view for about 72 seconds.

A computer printed what the telescope heard. A number from 0 to 9 meant a weak signal. For a stronger signal, the computer used letters, from A to Z.

Now you can read the code like Ehman did. The signal started at 6. It climbed to E, then Q, and then U. After that, it fell to J and to 5. The letter U meant a signal about 30 times stronger than the usual noise of space.

The telescope caught the signal on August 15, 1977, at 11:16 at night. Nobody saw it then. Ehman found it a few days later, at his kitchen table.

Why this signal was special

A strong signal is exciting. But three other things made this one special.

First, it lasted the right time. The signal rose and fell in about 72 seconds. That is the time that a point in the sky takes to drift past Big Ear. So the signal seemed to come from one fixed place in space.

Second, it was narrow. Think of a radio dial. A natural source, such as a cloud of gas, makes noise across a wide part of the dial. This signal sat on one thin slice of it. Transmitters made by people usually do that.

Third, it was on a famous channel. Hydrogen is the most common element in the universe, and it gives off a natural radio signal. Scientists call this the hydrogen line. In 1959, two scientists named Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison had an idea. If aliens wanted to call other worlds, they would use a channel near the hydrogen line. Every astronomer in the universe would know it. It is like a telephone number that everyone knows.

The Wow! Signal arrived very close to that channel. It came from the direction of Sagittarius, a group of stars in the direction of the center of our galaxy.

So the signal looked like the message that scientists had waited for. There was only one thing to do. They had to listen again.

Listening again, and again

Scientists have listened to that part of the sky for more than forty years. The signal has never come back.

An astronomer named Robert Gray tried many times. He searched in 1987 and 1989 with a telescope system in the United States. He searched in 1995 and 1996 with the Very Large Array in New Mexico, a very powerful group of radio telescopes. In 1999, he tried from Tasmania, in Australia. Each time, he found nothing.

In 2022, a project called Breakthrough Listen searched the same spot with two more telescopes. It found nothing, too.

This is a big problem. A key rule of science says that a result must be repeatable. If something happens only once, nobody can check it.

There is also a small puzzle inside Big Ear. The telescope had two antennas, called feed horns. Each point of the sky passed one horn first, and the other horn a few minutes later. A real signal from space should appear in both, one after the other. The Wow! Signal appeared in only one. And the printout cannot show which one.

So what was it? Let us look at three ideas.

Idea one: it came from Earth

Perhaps the signal was not from space at all. Perhaps it was a radio signal from people.

There is a problem with this idea. The hydrogen line is protected by international agreement. Transmitters on Earth may not broadcast on it or near it.

Ehman had another thought. In 1994, he told a newspaper that a signal from Earth had possibly bounced off a piece of space debris. Space debris is rubbish in orbit, such as parts of old rockets. But that object would need some very odd features. Later, Ehman himself moved away from the idea.

Others have suggested a satellite or some other machine made by people. Nobody has proved any of these ideas.

Idea two: it came from a comet

In 2017, a researcher named Antonio Paris suggested something new. A comet is a ball of ice and dust that travels around the Sun, and it has a cloud of hydrogen gas around it. Paris thought that two comets had made the signal.

It was a clever idea, but astronomers rejected it. The two comets were not in the view of the telescope at the right time. And comets do not make strong signals like this one.

Idea three: somebody sent it

This is the idea that everyone secretly hopes for. I do, too.

The signal was narrow. It was near the hydrogen line. It came from a part of the sky that is full of stars. In 2020, an amateur astronomer named Alberto Caballero looked for stars like our Sun in that area. He found three. The best studied one is about 1,800 light-years from Earth. A light-year is the distance that light travels in one year. So a signal from that star would be about 1,800 years old when it reached us.

But we must be honest here. Nobody knows if that star has planets with life. And the alien idea has the same problem as the others. A signal that appears once, and never again, cannot be confirmed.

Jerry Ehman was always careful about this. He did not say that the signal came from aliens. He said that without a second signal, any idea about its source is only a guess.

Still listening

So where are we now? No idea has been confirmed. Astronomers do not accept the comet idea. The signal possibly came from Earth, or possibly from very far away. More than forty years later, nobody can say.

Think again of that kitchen table in 1977. A man with a red pen found six characters that he could not explain. He wrote the most honest word that he knew.

Scientists have not found a better one. Perhaps one day, the signal will come back.
