The Lost Fabergé Eggs: Eight Imperial Treasures Still Waiting to Be Found

Somewhere in the world there may be a small, jeweled object worth tens of millions of dollars. It could be in a private collection, an attic, or a safety deposit box. It could be sitting on a dusty shelf. Its owner may have no idea what they have.

This is not a fantasy. It has already happened at least once. In 2014, a man in the American Midwest owned a small gold egg. He had bought it at a flea market for about $14,000, planning to sell it for scrap metal. Then he learned the truth about the object on his kitchen counter.

It was the Third Imperial Fabergé Egg of 1887. It was one of the famous jeweled Easter eggs that Peter Carl Fabergé made for the Russian royal family. It was worth about $33 million.

The egg had been bought at a junk market in the 1960s for about $500. It sat unnoticed for half a century. It was nearly melted down for its gold before anyone knew what it was.

The Third Imperial Egg was one of 50 Imperial Easter eggs. The House of Fabergé made them for the Romanov dynasty between 1885 and 1916. Each was a masterpiece of goldsmithing, enameling, and clever machinery. Each held a surprise, such as a tiny portrait, a mechanical animal, a working clock, or a small model of an imperial carriage or palace.

Of the 50 Imperial eggs, 43 are accounted for. Seven are missing. They were lost in the chaos of the Russian Revolution. A poor Soviet government sold some of them. Decades of private dealing scattered them around the globe. Some may be sitting in collections whose owners do not know what they hold.

The story begins with the Romanov dynasty. This royal family ruled Russia for over three centuries. Their rule ended with a violent overthrow in the Russian Revolution of 1917.

Giving eggs as Easter gifts was an old custom in Russian Orthodox culture. In that faith, an egg stands for resurrection and new life. In 1885, Tsar Alexander III asked the House of Fabergé to make a special Easter egg. The House of Fabergé was one of the best jewellery firms in Saint Petersburg. The egg was a gift for his wife, Tsarina Maria Fedorovna.

The result was the Hen Egg. It looked like a plain white enamel egg. It opened to show a gold yolk. Inside the yolk was a small gold hen with ruby eyes. The hen hid a tiny imperial crown and a small ruby pendant.

The Tsarina was delighted. Alexander III began a tradition that lasted over thirty years. Each Easter, the Tsar ordered a new Fabergé egg. Each was unique, each held a surprise, and each was the finest work a goldsmith could make.

Alexander died in 1894. His son Nicholas II kept the tradition going, with even more splendor. He ordered two eggs a year, one for his mother and one for his wife, Alexandra Fedorovna.

Each egg took nearly a year to make. A team of master craftsmen worked in gold, silver, enamel, diamonds, rubies, sapphires, and other precious materials.

The 50 Imperial Fabergé eggs are among the finest decorative art ever made. They are so detailed and so perfectly made that people compare them to the work of Renaissance goldsmiths. Renaissance goldsmiths worked in Europe from about the 1400s. People also compare them to the makers of medieval reliquaries, which are ornate boxes for holy objects.

Each egg had a different design. The surprises inside ranged from charming to astonishing. The Trans-Siberian Railway Egg (1900) marked the completion of the railway across Russia. It held a working miniature gold train. The train had a platinum headlight and a ruby lantern. You could wind it up and set it running along a tiny track.

The Coronation Egg (1897) marked the coronation of Nicholas II. It was made of gold and clear yellow enamel. Inside was an exact small copy of the imperial coronation carriage. It had working doors, turning wheels, and a tiny folding step. The model was so detailed that it took the craftsmen fifteen months.

The Winter Egg (1913) is perhaps the most beautiful of all. It was carved from a single piece of quartz. It was decorated with more than 1,300 diamonds set in platinum, to look like frost flowers. Its surprise was a tiny basket of spring flowers carved from quartz and gold.

The Winter Egg sold at Christie’s auction in 2002 for $9.6 million. It sold again in 2025 for $30.2 million. That makes it one of the most expensive decorative objects ever sold.

The art of the eggs went far beyond their materials. Carl Fabergé was famous for insisting that the eggs should be beautiful and surprising, not merely costly. The value lay in the clever design and the perfect work, not in the number of jewels.

The eggs held many mechanical marvels. There were tiny singing birds, spinning globes, miniature paintings on ivory, clockwork, and lifelike enamel portraits. In the Lilies of the Valley Egg (1898), three small portraits of Nicholas II and his daughters rose from the top when a pearl button was pressed. The Bay Tree Egg (1911) looked like a carved nephrite tree. Nephrite is a green stone. A singing mechanical bird came out of the top.

Each egg showed the unmatched skill of the House of Fabergé and its army of specialists. There were enamelers, jewellers, goldsmiths, engravers, and miniaturists. They worked in strict secrecy so the surprises would delight the imperial family.

The very first egg, the Hen Egg of 1885, set the pattern. It was a rich jeweled shell hiding a series of surprises that you found only by opening it. The Hen Egg survived the Russian Revolution. It is now in the collection of the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts.

The world of the Romanovs ended suddenly and violently in 1917. The February Revolution forced Nicholas II to give up the throne. Then the Bolsheviks took power in the October Revolution. They imprisoned the imperial family. In July 1918, the family was killed in the cellar of the Ipatiev House in Yekaterinburg.

The Bolsheviks took the Fabergé eggs and the other imperial treasures from the palaces. These were the Winter Palace, the Anichkov Palace, and the Alexander Palace. They carried them to the Kremlin Armory in Moscow for safekeeping.

For a few years the eggs sat in the Kremlin. They were listed but not used. The new Soviet government was struggling with civil war, a collapsing economy, and isolation from other countries.

The last Imperial egg, the Constellation Egg (1917), was never finished. It was found in the Fabergé workshop, in different stages of assembly. Its surprise, believed to be a mechanical turning globe, was not complete.

In the late 1920s and early 1930s, the Soviet government under Joseph Stalin made a fateful decision. It chose to sell many of the imperial treasures, including the Fabergé eggs. It wanted foreign money to pay for industry.

The sales went through a network of go-betweens and dealers. The best known was Armand Hammer, an American businessman who had made trade links with the Soviet Union. He later became famous as head of Occidental Petroleum.

Hammer and other dealers bought the eggs from the Soviet government at very low prices. They resold them to rich collectors in the United States and Europe. Few records were kept. In the process, the history of many eggs was blurred or lost.

Some eggs passed quickly through several owners. They vanished into private collections and stayed unseen for decades. The chaos of the revolution, the secret Soviet sales, and decades in private hands were perfect conditions for objects to be lost, misnamed, or forgotten.

Of the 50 Imperial Fabergé eggs, seven are still unaccounted for as of 2026. Nobody knows where they are or what happened to them. The first three are the Hen with Sapphire Pendant (1886), the Cherub with Chariot (1888), and the Necessaire (1889). The other four are the Empire Nephrite (1902), the Royal Danish (1903), the Alexander III Commemorative (1909), and the Constellation (1917).

Some may have been destroyed during the revolution or in the years since. Others may be in private collections whose owners do not know what they have. That was true of the Third Imperial Egg before it was found again in 2014.

The Necessaire Egg (1889) held a set of tiny gold and enamel toilet tools. It was last seen in 1952 at a dealer’s shop in London. It has not been seen since. The Alexander III Commemorative Egg (1909) held a gold bust of the late tsar. It vanished after the Bolsheviks took it, and it has never appeared on the art market.

The finding of the Third Imperial Egg is the most dramatic chapter in the modern Fabergé story. It is also the best sign that the missing eggs might still be found.

In the 1960s, someone bought a small gold egg at a junk market in the United States for about $500. The buyer had no idea what it was. It was just an attractive gold trinket that seemed worth the price. The egg stayed with the buyer for years and then passed to a family member.

Decades later, around 2012, a man bought the egg from the estate for about $14,000. He is described only as a resident of the American Midwest. He meant to sell it for the value of its gold, at a small profit. He contacted several buyers. But the gold was worth less than he had paid. Disappointed, he put the egg aside.

One evening he was browsing the internet and found an article about the missing Fabergé eggs. One egg in it, the Third Imperial Egg of 1887, looked a lot like the gold egg on his kitchen counter. He began to research. He compared his egg with photographs and descriptions of the missing treasure. He grew more and more sure that he had something extraordinary.

He contacted Kieran McCarthy, a director of Wartski, the famous London dealers in Fabergé. McCarthy flew to the United States to see the egg himself. He knew it at once as the Third Imperial Egg.

It was made of 18-karat gold in the Louis XVI style. It stood on gold legs with lion-paw feet and was decorated with sapphires and diamonds. It held its original surprise, a lady’s watch by Vacheron and Constantin with a white enamel dial.

The egg was confirmed as genuine. Wartski bought it for a private collector who has not been named. The man who nearly melted it down for scrap now owned an object worth about $33 million.

Fabergé eggs are among the most valuable decorative objects in the world. The most expensive Fabergé egg ever sold at auction was the Rothschild Egg. It fetched $18.5 million at Christie’s in 2007.

In 2004, the Russian oligarch Viktor Vekselberg bought a collection of 9 Imperial eggs. An oligarch is a very rich and powerful businessman. The American publisher Malcolm Forbes had put the collection together. Vekselberg paid a reported $90 to $120 million. It was the largest single purchase of Fabergé eggs in history.

His collection is now shown at the Fabergé Museum in St. Petersburg, which opened in 2013. The Kremlin Armory in Moscow has the largest museum collection, with 10 Imperial eggs. The Virginia Museum of Fine Arts owns 5, and the Royal Collection of the United Kingdom holds 3.

The Third Imperial Egg has made people wonder about the seven missing eggs. They might still turn up in private collections, antique shops, estate sales, or even flea markets. The same things that hid the Third Egg for over a century apply to the others. There was the chaos of the revolution and the secret Soviet sales. There were many owners who may not have known what they held. And not everyone can spot a Fabergé egg when they see one.

The lost Fabergé eggs are more than costly objects. They are physical links to a vanished world. Each egg stands for months of careful work by master craftsmen at the peak of their art. They made things of great beauty for a royal family that would be swept away by revolution within a few years.

The 43 surviving Imperial eggs are in museums, royal collections, and private hands around the world. Each is a treasure worth more than most houses. But the seven missing eggs are the ones that fire the imagination, because they might still be out there.

The Third Imperial Egg showed that great treasures can hide in very ordinary places. Their owners may not know for decades. The Necessaire Egg was seen in London in 1952. Someone, somewhere, may have it on a shelf. The Alexander III Commemorative Egg, with its gold bust of the slain tsar, may sit in a private vault. The Constellation Egg, the last Imperial egg ever begun, may survive in some form.

Until every egg is accounted for, the hunt will go on. It is a real treasure hunt, and the prizes are objects of priceless beauty. The next find might be waiting at a flea market near you. Where are the seven missing eggs?
