The Lost Fabergé Eggs: Six Imperial Treasures Still Waiting to Be Found

Somewhere in the world there may be a small, jeweled object worth tens of millions of dollars. It could be in a private collection, an attic, or a safety deposit box. It could be sitting on a dusty shelf. Its owner may have no idea what they have.

This is not a fantasy. It has already happened at least once. In 2014, the world learned that a man in the American Midwest had owned a small gold egg. He had bought it at a market for about $14,000, planning to sell it for scrap metal. Then he learned the truth about the object on his kitchen counter.

It was the Third Imperial Fabergé Egg of 1887. It was one of the famous jeweled Easter eggs that Peter Carl Fabergé made for the Russian royal family. Reports have valued it at tens of millions of dollars. One estimate was $33 million.

The egg had vanished after the Russian Revolution. It sat unnoticed for decades. It was nearly melted down for its gold before anyone knew what it was.

The Third Imperial Egg was one of 50 Imperial Easter eggs. The House of Fabergé made them for the Romanov dynasty between 1885 and 1917. Each was a masterpiece of goldsmithing, enameling, and clever machinery. Each held a surprise, such as a tiny portrait, a mechanical animal, a working clock, or a small model of an imperial carriage or palace.

Of the 50 Imperial eggs, 44 are accounted for. Six are missing. Counts differ a little between experts. The eggs were lost in the years after the Russian Revolution. A poor Soviet government sold some of them. Decades of private dealing scattered them around the globe. Some may be sitting in collections whose owners do not know what they hold.

The story begins with the Romanov dynasty. This royal family ruled Russia for over three centuries. Their rule ended with a violent overthrow in the Russian Revolution of 1917.

Giving eggs as Easter gifts was an old custom in Russian Orthodox culture. In that faith, an egg stands for resurrection and new life. In 1885, Tsar Alexander III asked the House of Fabergé to make a special Easter egg. The House of Fabergé was one of the best jewellery firms in Saint Petersburg. The egg was a gift for his wife, Tsarina Maria Fedorovna.

The result was the Hen Egg. It looked like a plain white enamel egg. It opened to show a gold yolk. Inside the yolk was a small gold hen with ruby eyes. The hen hid a tiny imperial crown and a small ruby pendant.

The Tsarina was delighted. Alexander III began a tradition that lasted over thirty years. Each Easter, the Tsar ordered a new Fabergé egg. Each was unique, each held a surprise, and each was the finest work a goldsmith could make.

Alexander died in 1894. His son Nicholas II kept the tradition going, with even more splendor. He gave eggs to both his mother and his wife, Alexandra Fedorovna.

Making an egg took a long time. A team of master craftsmen worked in gold, silver, enamel, diamonds, rubies, sapphires, and other precious materials.

The 50 Imperial Fabergé eggs are among the best-known pieces of decorative art ever made. They are so detailed that people compare them to the work of Renaissance goldsmiths. Renaissance goldsmiths worked in Europe from about the 1400s. People also compare them to the makers of medieval reliquaries, which are ornate boxes for holy objects.

Each egg had a different design. The surprises inside ranged from charming to astonishing. The Trans-Siberian Railway Egg (1900) marked the completion of the railway across Russia. It held a working miniature gold train. You could wind it up and set it running.

The Coronation Egg (1897) marked the coronation of Nicholas II. It was made of gold and clear yellow enamel. Inside was an exact small copy of the imperial coronation carriage. It had working doors and turning wheels.

The Winter Egg (1913) is one of the most admired of all. It was carved from rock crystal, a clear kind of quartz. It was decorated with thousands of diamonds set in platinum, to look like frost. Its surprise was a tiny basket of spring flowers made of quartz and gold.

The Winter Egg sold at Christie’s auction in 2002 for $9.6 million. It sold again in December 2025 for $30.2 million, a record price for a Fabergé object.

The art of the eggs went far beyond their materials. Carl Fabergé is said to have wanted the eggs to be beautiful and surprising, not merely costly. Much of their value lies in the clever design and the fine work.

The eggs held many mechanical marvels. There were tiny singing birds, spinning globes, miniature paintings on ivory, clockwork, and lifelike enamel portraits. In the Lilies of the Valley Egg (1898), small portraits rose from the top when a pearl button was pressed. The Bay Tree Egg (1911) looked like a tree with nephrite leaves. Nephrite is a green stone. A singing mechanical bird came out of the top.

Each egg showed the unmatched skill of the House of Fabergé and its army of specialists. There were enamelers, jewellers, goldsmiths, engravers, and miniaturists. They worked in strict secrecy so the surprises would delight the imperial family.

The very first egg, the Hen Egg of 1885, set the pattern. It was a rich shell hiding a series of surprises that you found only by opening it. The Hen Egg survived the Russian Revolution. It is now in the Fabergé Museum in Saint Petersburg.

The world of the Romanovs ended suddenly and violently in 1917. The February Revolution forced Nicholas II to give up the throne. Then the Bolsheviks took power in the October Revolution. They imprisoned the imperial family. In July 1918, the family was killed in the cellar of the Ipatiev House in Yekaterinburg.

The Bolsheviks took the Fabergé eggs and the other imperial treasures from the palaces. They carried them to the Kremlin Armory in Moscow.

For a few years the eggs sat in the Kremlin. They were listed but not used. The new Soviet government was struggling with civil war, a collapsing economy, and isolation from other countries.

The last Imperial egg, the Constellation Egg (1917), was never finished, because of the revolution.

In the late 1920s and early 1930s, the Soviet government under Joseph Stalin chose to sell many of the imperial treasures, including some Fabergé eggs. It wanted foreign money to pay for industry. The sales were called “treasures for tractors.”

The sales went through go-betweens and dealers. One of the best known was Armand Hammer, an American businessman who had made trade links with the Soviet Union. He later became famous as head of Occidental Petroleum.

Hammer and other dealers are said to have bought eggs from the Soviet government at low prices. They resold them to rich collectors in the United States and Europe. Records of these sales are poor, so the history of some eggs is blurred.

Some eggs passed quickly through several owners. They vanished into private collections and stayed unseen for decades. The chaos of the revolution, the secret Soviet sales, and decades in private hands were perfect conditions for objects to be lost, misnamed, or forgotten.

Of the 50 Imperial Fabergé eggs, six are still unaccounted for, according to one recent count. Nobody knows where they are or what happened to them. They are the Hen with Sapphire Pendant (1886), the Cherub with Chariot (1888), and the Necessaire (1889). The other three are the Mauve (1897), the Royal Danish (1903), and the Alexander III Commemorative (1909). Some lists differ on dates and on how many are missing.

Some may have been destroyed during the revolution or in the years since. Others may be in private collections whose owners do not know what they have. That was true of the Third Imperial Egg before it was found again in 2014.

The Necessaire Egg (1889) was sold by the London dealer Wartski in 1952 to a buyer listed only as “A Stranger.” It has not been seen since. The Alexander III Commemorative Egg (1909) was a gift in memory of the late tsar. Its whereabouts are unknown.

The finding of the Third Imperial Egg is the most dramatic chapter in the modern Fabergé story. It is also a sign that other missing eggs might still be found.

The egg was made in 1887 for Tsarina Maria Fedorovna. After 1917 it was seized by the Bolsheviks. Then it vanished from the record. In 1964 it was sold at a New York auction for only a few thousand dollars, as a gold watch in an egg-shaped case. Nobody knew it was a Fabergé egg.

Later, probably around 2004, a scrap dealer bought the egg at a market in the American Midwest for about $14,000. He is described only as a man from the Midwest. He meant to sell it for the value of its gold, at a small profit. But he could not find a buyer who would pay enough. Disappointed, he put the egg aside.

About 2012, he searched the internet for the words on the watch inside the egg. He found stories about a missing Fabergé egg. The Third Imperial Egg of 1887 looked a lot like the gold egg in his home. He began to research. He grew more and more sure that he had something extraordinary.

He contacted Kieran McCarthy, a director of Wartski, the famous London dealers in Fabergé. McCarthy is said to have known it at once as the Third Imperial Egg.

It was made of 18-karat gold in the Louis XVI style. It stood on gold legs with lion-paw feet and was decorated with sapphires and diamonds. It held its original surprise, a lady’s watch by Vacheron Constantin.

The egg was confirmed as genuine. Wartski bought it for a private collector who has not been named. It was shown in London in April 2014. The man who nearly melted it down for scrap had owned an object valued at tens of millions of dollars.

Fabergé eggs are among the most valuable decorative objects in the world. The Rothschild Egg fetched about $18.5 million at Christie’s in 2007. That was a record until the Winter Egg sold in 2025.

In 2004, the Russian oligarch Viktor Vekselberg bought the Forbes collection of 9 Imperial eggs. An oligarch is a very rich and powerful businessman. The American publisher Malcolm Forbes had put the collection together. Reports say Vekselberg paid between $90 million and more than $100 million.

His collection is now shown at the Fabergé Museum in St. Petersburg, which opened in 2013. The Kremlin Armory in Moscow has the largest museum collection, with 10 Imperial eggs. The Virginia Museum of Fine Arts owns 5, and the Royal Collection of the United Kingdom holds 3.

The Third Imperial Egg has made people wonder about the six missing eggs. They might still turn up in private collections, antique shops, estate sales, or even flea markets. The same things that hid the Third Egg for decades apply to the others. There was the chaos of the revolution and the secret Soviet sales. There were many owners who may not have known what they held. And not everyone can spot a Fabergé egg when they see one.

The lost Fabergé eggs are more than costly objects. They are physical links to a vanished world. Each egg stands for long, careful work by master craftsmen at the peak of their art. They made things of great beauty for a royal family that would be swept away by revolution within a few years.

The 44 surviving Imperial eggs are in museums, royal collections, and private hands around the world. Each is a treasure worth more than most houses. But the six missing eggs are the ones that fire the imagination, because they might still be out there.

The Third Imperial Egg showed that great treasures can hide in very ordinary places. Their owners may not know for decades. The Necessaire Egg was sold in London in 1952. Someone, somewhere, may have it on a shelf. The Alexander III Commemorative Egg may sit in a private vault.

Until every egg is accounted for, the hunt will go on. It is a real treasure hunt, and the prizes are objects of priceless beauty. The next find might be waiting at a market near you. Where are the six missing eggs?
