El Dorado: The Legendary City of Gold That Drove Conquistadors Mad

In 1541, a Spanish conquistador named Gonzalo Pizarro marched out of the city of Quito. A conquistador was a Spanish soldier who conquered new lands. Gonzalo was the brother of the man who had conquered the Inca Empire and taken its mountains of gold. He led an army of 4,000 men, with thousands of horses, dogs, and pigs.

They followed a shining dream. Local people had told him about a city made entirely of gold, deep inside South America. Its king, they said, covered himself in gold dust every morning. Every night he washed it off in a sacred lake.

Pizarro and his men pushed into the Amazon rainforest. The plants were so thick that sunlight never reached the ground. They crossed rivers full of piranhas. They suffered disease and hunger, and many men ran away. Local warriors attacked them, because they wanted nothing to do with the strangers in armor.

Within months, the expedition fell apart. Men died by the hundreds, from fever, hunger, poisoned arrows, drowning, and plain exhaustion. When the survivors stumbled out of the jungle, the dream of El Dorado, The Gilded One, had cost thousands of lives. It had given nothing in return.

Yet the legend lived on for another 80 years. It drove some of the boldest and worst-fated expeditions in the history of exploration. And it left a question behind. Was there ever a city of gold? Or was the greatest treasure hunt in history built on a ritual that the Spanish misunderstood?

In the early 1500s, Spain’s conquests in the Americas brought riches that Europe had never imagined. The fall of the Aztec Empire in 1521 brought huge amounts of gold and silver. The conquest of the Inca Empire in 1533 brought even more. The ransom of the Inca emperor Atahualpa alone filled a room that was 22 by 17 feet with gold objects.

These finds made the Spanish believe that the New World held almost endless precious metal. The other European powers watched with envy. Reports came back from the interior of Colombia, Venezuela, and the Amazon basin. They told of peoples who wore gold, used gold in rituals, and spoke of greater treasures deeper in the wilderness. It was the start of a long and destructive search. It would show as much about the seekers as about what they sought.

The truth behind El Dorado is more interesting than the myth, and more modest. The original “El Dorado” meant The Gilded Man. It was not a city or an empire. It was a person. He was the zipa, the king or chief of the Muisca people.

The Muisca were one of the four most advanced civilizations of the Americas before Europeans came. The others were the Aztec, Maya, and Inca. The Muisca lived in the highlands of present-day Colombia, on fertile high ground around the modern capital, Bogotá.

They were not one single state. They were a network of friendly chiefdoms, led by two main rulers. The zipa ruled in the south, and the zaque ruled in the north. The Muisca were skilled goldsmiths, farmers, and weavers. They were also astronomers, with a careful calendar and many gods.

The Spanish chronicles describe a remarkable ceremony at Lake Guatavita. These writings were, as always, shaped by the views and mistakes of the conquerors. The lake is a round crater lake in the hills about 57 kilometers (35 miles) northeast of Bogotá.

When a new zipa took power, he took off his clothes. His body was covered in fine gold dust. He floated out onto the sacred lake on a raft loaded with gold and emeralds. Priests and helpers followed in canoes. At the center of the lake, the golden king dived into the cold water and washed the dust from his body. It was a gift to the gods.

At the same moment, his helpers threw gold ornaments, emeralds, and other treasures into the lake. The ritual was a ceremony of crowning and offering. It joined the new ruler with the divine powers believed to live in the water.

It was not a show of endless wealth. It was a religious act. The Muisca believed gold was the sweat of the sun god Súa. They believed the lake was a doorway to the spirit world.

In 1969, two farm workers were in a cave near the town of Pasca, about 40 kilometers south of Bogotá. They found a small and wonderful gold object. It is called the Muisca Raft, or Balsa Muisca. It measures just 19.5 × 10.2 × 10.1 centimeters (7.7 × 4.0 × 4.0 inches).

The raft shows the El Dorado ceremony in miniature. A standing figure, the zipa, wears a headdress and a nose ornament. Smaller helpers stand around him. It was made by lost-wax casting, a method where the artist shapes a wax model and pours melted metal into the mold. The metal is a mix of gold, silver, and copper. The raft has been dated to between 1295 and 1410 CE. That is centuries before the Spanish came.

It is now the star of the Gold Museum (Museo del Oro) in Bogotá. The museum holds more than 55,000 pieces of goldwork from before the Spanish arrived. It is the largest such collection in the world. The raft has become a national emblem of Colombia and appears on its stamps.

There is a bitter irony here. The raft is the closest thing to El Dorado that has ever been found. It is not a city of gold. It is a small, beautiful model of a ritual that the Spanish badly misunderstood.

How did a Muisca ritual become one of the most lasting legends in history? It shows how misunderstanding, greed, and a lack of respect for another culture can build a myth strong enough to change a continent.

When the first Spanish explorers reached Muisca lands in the 1530s, they found a people rich in gold. But it was the way a deeply religious people is rich in sacred objects. It was not the way a mining empire is rich in gold bars. The Muisca got gold by trade and by panning rivers, not from mines. They used it for worship and ceremonies, not as money.

But the Spanish had already taken huge amounts of gold from Mexico and Peru. They heard about a king covered in gold dust, plunging into a lake full of treasure. Their imaginations did the rest.

The first Spanish explorer to reach the Muisca was Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada. In 1537 he led about 800 men up the Magdalena River. The march through the hot lowlands was brutal, and most of his men died of disease and hunger. Only 166 reached the Muisca highlands alive.

He found a civilization that deeply impressed him. He took a large amount of gold, including objects from Muisca temples and graves. But he did not find the legendary city of gold. Still, the stories he and his men carried back to the coast fed the legend.

Within a few years, the idea had grown. First it was a golden man performing a ritual. Then it was a whole city of gold. Then it was a kingdom, and finally an empire of unimaginable wealth. It lay somewhere in the wild center of South America. It lived in the gap between rumor and wishful thinking. It was always just past the next mountain ridge, the next bend in the river, the next expedition.

For about 80 years, from the 1530s to the 1610s, one European expedition after another set out to find El Dorado. Each one went worse than the last. In 1541, Gonzalo Pizarro marched east from Quito with 4,000 soldiers and local porters. They plunged into the Amazon rainforest in search of the golden city.

Conditions were terrible, and the jungle swallowed men whole. Pizarro’s lieutenant, Francisco de Orellana, was sent ahead with a small group to find food. He never came back. Instead he became the first European to travel the whole length of the Amazon River, from its streams in the Andes to the Atlantic Ocean. The journey was more than 6,000 kilometers (3,700 miles) and took nearly eight months. Orellana’s account described large settlements and hinted at wealth, and it fed the legend even more.

In Venezuela, a German explorer named Philipp von Hutten searched for El Dorado from 1541 to 1545. He was backed by the Welser banking family of Augsburg. He explored the Venezuelan plains, called the llanos. He reportedly found signs of large communities but no golden city. A rival Spanish expedition led by Juan de Carvajal eventually killed him. The greatest danger in the hunt for El Dorado was often other Europeans.

Another German, Nikolaus Federmann, also worked for the Welser family. He led a separate expedition across the Venezuelan plains in the same years. He also failed.

Many people thought Lake Guatavita held huge amounts of sunken gold. This idea was almost as powerful as the legend of the city. In the 1540s, Spanish settlers tried to drain the lake. They cut a notch in the crater rim, and the water level dropped. They got some gold objects from the shore, but not the treasure they expected.

In the 1580s, a merchant named Antonio de Sepúlveda hired many workers to deepen the notch. He found some ornaments and emeralds, but the effort left him bankrupt.

From 1898 to 1912, a business called the Company Ltda. del Tesoro de Colombia (the Colombian Treasure Company) actually drained the lake. It dug a tunnel through the crater wall. But the exposed mud dried as hard as concrete. Nobody could dig out whatever lay beneath. The company went bankrupt too.

In 1965, the Colombian government declared Lake Guatavita a protected archaeological site. All treasure hunting there was banned.

Modern archaeologists have studied the lake as well. A 2023 study in Latin American Antiquity, by the Gold Museum, found that people did leave offerings at the lake. But they were small gifts, not the huge piles of gold the Spanish imagined.

No one shows the sad pointlessness of the El Dorado quest better than Sir Walter Raleigh (1552–1618). He was an English courtier, soldier, explorer, and poet, and a favorite of Queen Elizabeth I. For decades, the legend held him in its spell. He believed the golden city lay at the headwaters of the Caroní River, in what is now Venezuela.

In 1595, he led his first expedition. He sailed up the Orinoco River with a small fleet and went hundreds of miles into the interior. He found no gold, no city, and no empire. But he wrote a bestselling book, The Discovery of Guiana (1596). It described the Orinoco region in rich, tempting words and kept the legend alive for another generation. Raleigh wrote that the golden city was real and that finding it was only a matter of time and resources. That claim would prove fatal.

After Elizabeth died in 1603, Raleigh lost favor with the new king, James I. The king put him in the Tower of London on various charges. Raleigh talked James into letting him out for one last trip to find El Dorado. He promised gold, glory, and a new English colony in South America.

In 1617, Raleigh set sail with 14 ships and over 1,000 men. He sent his son, Watt Raleigh, up the Orinoco with a group of soldiers. The trip was a disaster from the start. Watt was killed in a battle with Spanish forces at the Venezuelan town of San Tomé. The surviving officers came back to the ships empty-handed.

Raleigh returned to England in disgrace. King James had ordered him not to provoke the Spanish. The king had him beheaded on October 29, 1618. The official reason was crimes from years before. In truth it was for failing to find El Dorado. His death marked the end of the great European hunt for the golden city.

The search for El Dorado cost the lives of thousands of Europeans and local people over about 80 years. Pizarro’s 1541 expedition began with about 4,000 men, and most died in the rainforest of disease, hunger, and attacks. Jiménez de Quesada’s 1537 expedition began with about 800 men, and only 166 reached the Muisca highlands alive.

Philipp von Hutten was killed not by the jungle but by rival Spaniards. Watt Raleigh died fighting the Spanish in Venezuela, and his father was executed after returning home.

The peoples of these lands suffered terribly too. They included the Muisca, the peoples of the Orinoco, and those of the Amazon basin. European diseases, slavery, and violence killed an estimated 80 to 90 percent of people in some areas within decades of first contact.

The Spanish destroyed the Muisca civilization in their search for gold. But the Muisca left a legacy the conquistadors could never have imagined. The Gold Museum in Bogotá holds the world’s largest collection of such goldwork. Its more than 55,000 pieces include many Muisca works.

There are pendants, nose ornaments, and breastplates. There are tunjos, which are small figures offered to the gods. And there is the Muisca Raft. They show a people of great artistic skill and deep spiritual life.

The Muisca did not use gold as money or to measure personal wealth. It was a sacred material, the “sweat of the sun.” It was used in rituals that linked the human world to the divine. The gold was not meant to be stored away. It was meant to be offered, worn, and left in lakes and caves as gifts to the gods.

Study of Lake Guatavita goes on. The 2023 survey by Gold Museum researchers found a shrine site near the lake. Small offerings were made there. The study said that Spanish chronicles had made the ceremonies sound bigger than they were. That helped to feed the myth of El Dorado.

The real Muisca world was impressive enough. It was a group of chiefdoms with perhaps 500,000 to one million people when the Spanish arrived. They farmed with advanced methods and kept up long trade routes. Their goldwork was as beautiful and as skillful as anything made in the ancient Mediterranean world.

The irony is deep. The Spanish destroyed a real civilization while chasing an imaginary one. The gold they wanted was never as valuable as the culture they wiped out.

At heart, the story of El Dorado is about the power of a story. A ritual was changed by European greed into a city of impossible wealth. A king covered in gold dust, diving into a sacred lake, became a kingdom of fabled splendor. It became an empire that lived only in the space between what people wanted to believe and what was really there.

For 80 years, the legend drove men to their deaths in some of the hardest lands on Earth. They died in the Amazon rainforest, the Venezuelan plains, and the Colombian highlands. It cost the lives of thousands of Europeans. It helped destroy local civilizations whose true wealth was far greater than any golden city. That wealth was their art, their religion, their social order, and their knowledge.

The Muisca Raft in Bogotá is barely 20 centimeters long. Yet it is worth more than all the imaginary cities of gold together. It is a real object, made by real hands, showing a real ceremony of a real civilization. That civilization was destroyed by people who could not see the treasure in front of them. They were too busy chasing a mirage.

The words “El Dorado” have entered the language. They mean any imaginary place of great wealth. That fits a legend that was, from beginning to end, the greatest treasure hunt that never found treasure. Did a city of gold ever exist? Everything we know says no. But the Muisca raft shows that the legend began with something real.
