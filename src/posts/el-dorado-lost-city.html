El Dorado: The Legendary City of Gold That Drove Conquistadors Mad

A march after a golden dream

In 1541, a Spanish conquistador named Gonzalo Pizarro marched out of the city of Quito. A conquistador was a Spanish soldier who conquered new lands. Gonzalo was the brother of the man who had conquered the Inca Empire and taken its mountains of gold. He led about 220 Spaniards and about 4,000 Native porters, with horses and dogs.

They followed a shining dream. Stories told of a golden land deep inside South America. They spoke of a ruler who covered himself in gold dust and washed it off in a sacred lake.

Pizarro and his men pushed into the rainforest east of the Andes. The land was hard to cross, and the food ran short. Pizarro treated local people cruelly when they could not show him the way to El Dorado.

The expedition fell apart. By one account, about 140 Spaniards and about 3,000 Native people died, mostly from hunger. The dream of El Dorado, The Gilded One, had cost thousands of lives. It had given nothing in return.

So where did the dream come from? Let us find the real story.

A gilded man, not a city

The original “El Dorado” meant The Gilded Man. It was not a city or an empire. It was a person. He was the zipa, the king or chief of the Muisca people.

The Muisca were one of the important civilizations of the Americas before Europeans came. They lived in the highlands of present-day Colombia, around the modern capital, Bogotá. They were not one single state. They were a loose group of chiefdoms. The zipa ruled in the south, and the zaque ruled in the north. The Muisca were skilled goldsmiths, farmers, and weavers. They also kept a careful calendar.

The Spanish chronicles describe a remarkable ceremony at Lake Guatavita. These writings were shaped by the views and mistakes of the conquerors. The lake is a round crater lake in the hills about 57 kilometers (35 miles) northeast of Bogotá.

The story says that when a new zipa took power, his body was covered in mud and fine gold dust. He floated out onto the sacred lake on a raft. In the middle of the lake, he threw gold and emeralds into the water and washed the dust from his body. It was a gift to the gods.

It was not a show of endless wealth. It was a religious act. The Muisca honored the sun god Sué, and gold was very important in their worship.

Is there anything real that we can hold in our hands?

The tiny gold raft

In 1969, a farmer found a small and wonderful gold object in a cave near the town of Pasca, south of Bogotá. It is called the Muisca Raft. It measures just 19.5 × 10.2 × 10.1 centimeters (7.7 × 4.0 × 4.0 inches).

The raft shows the El Dorado ceremony in miniature. A standing figure, the zipa, wears a headdress and a nose ornament. Smaller helpers stand around him. It was made by lost-wax casting. The artist shapes a wax model and pours melted metal into the mold. The metal is a mix of gold, silver, and copper. The raft has been dated to between 1295 and 1410 CE. That is centuries before the Spanish came.

It is now the star of the Gold Museum in Bogotá. The raft has become a national emblem of Colombia and appears on its stamps.

There is a bitter irony here. The raft is one of the best clues to El Dorado that has ever been found. It is not a city of gold. It is a small model of a ritual that the Spanish badly misunderstood.

How a ritual became a city

When the first Spanish explorers reached Muisca lands in the 1530s, they found a people rich in gold. But it was the way a deeply religious people is rich in sacred objects. It was not the way a mining empire is rich in gold bars. The Muisca had no gold mines in their land, and got much of their gold by trade.

The Spanish had already taken huge amounts of gold from Mexico and Peru. The ransom of the Inca emperor Atahualpa alone filled a room with gold objects. They heard about a king covered in gold dust, plunging into a lake full of treasure. Their imaginations did the rest.

The first Spanish explorer to reach the Muisca was Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada. In 1537 he led an expedition up the Magdalena River. Many of his men died of disease and hunger. He found a civilization that deeply impressed him, and he took gold from Muisca temples. But he did not find a city of gold. Still, the stories that he and his men carried back fed the legend.

Within a few years, the idea had grown. First it was a golden man performing a ritual. Then it was a whole city of gold. Then it was a kingdom, and finally an empire of unimaginable wealth. It was always just past the next mountain ridge, the next bend in the river, the next expedition.

Eighty years of searching

For about 80 years, from the 1530s to the 1610s, one European expedition after another set out to find El Dorado. Many of them went badly.

On Pizarro’s march, his second-in-command, Francisco de Orellana, was sent down the Napo River with about 50 men to find food. The strong current made it too hard to come back. Orellana went on to become the first European to travel the length of the Amazon River. Some say he abandoned Pizarro.

A German explorer named Philipp von Hutten searched for El Dorado in the 1540s. He explored the plains east of the Andes and found no golden city. He was later killed by Spaniards. Sometimes the greatest danger in the hunt was other Europeans. Another German, Nikolaus Federmann, reached the Muisca highlands in 1539, at the same time as two Spanish captains. The three of them founded Bogotá together.

Many people also thought that Lake Guatavita held huge amounts of sunken gold. In 1545, Spaniards tried to drain the lake. They lowered the water enough to get hundreds of gold pieces from the shore, but not the treasure they expected. Later in the 1500s, a merchant named Antonio de Sepúlveda hired many workers to dig a canal. The water dropped about 20 meters. He found an emerald and some pots, but no gold.

In the early 1900s, English and German adventurers tried again. The last attempt, in 1912, failed too. Only a few gold and silver objects have ever come from the lake.

Raleigh’s last voyage

Sir Walter Raleigh (about 1552–1618) was an English courtier, soldier, explorer, and poet, and a favorite of Queen Elizabeth I. For decades, the legend held him in its spell. He believed the golden city lay at the headwaters of the Caroní River, in what is now Venezuela.

In 1595, he led his first expedition to the Orinoco region. He found no gold, no city, and no empire. But he wrote a famous book, The Discovery of Guiana (1596). It made big claims about the region and helped keep the legend alive for another generation.

After Elizabeth died in 1603, Raleigh lost favor with the new king, James I. He was arrested for treason that year and held in the Tower of London. Years later the king let him out for one last trip to Guiana. Raleigh promised to find gold.

In 1617, Raleigh set sail with a fleet. His son, Watt Raleigh, went with a group of soldiers to attack a Spanish outpost, Santo Tomé, on the Orinoco. Raleigh had told them not to fight the Spanish. Watt was killed in the attack, and the expedition came back empty-handed.

Raleigh returned to England in disgrace. The Spanish ambassador demanded that Raleigh’s old death sentence from 1603 be carried out. The king agreed. Raleigh was executed on October 29, 1618. His death is often seen as the end of the great European hunt for the golden city.

A real people, a made-up city

The search cost the lives of thousands of Europeans and local people. The peoples of these lands suffered terribly too. They included the Muisca, the peoples of the Orinoco, and those of the Amazon basin. European diseases, slavery, and violence killed very many of them.

The Muisca left a legacy that the conquistadors could never have imagined. The Gold Museum in Bogotá holds about 34,000 pieces of gold from before the Spanish arrived. It is described as the largest such collection in the world. There are pendants, nose ornaments, and breastplates. There are tunjos, which are small figures offered to the gods. The Muisca did not use gold as money. It was meant to be offered, worn, and left in lakes and caves as gifts to the gods.

So what do we know? Historians disagree about how much of the Lake Guatavita story is true. Some think the ceremony was real. Others think the Spaniards made much of it up. Estimates of the Muisca people range from one million to three million when the Spanish arrived.

Did a city of gold ever exist? Everything we know says no. Think again of the little raft in Bogotá, barely 20 centimeters long. It is a real object, made by real hands. The legend began with something real, and the Spanish could not see the treasure in front of them.

The words “El Dorado” have entered the language. They mean any imaginary place of great wealth.
