El Dorado: The Legendary City of Gold That Drove Conquistadors Mad

In 1541, a Spanish conquistador named Gonzalo Pizarro marched out of the city of Quito. A conquistador was a Spanish soldier who conquered new lands. Gonzalo was the brother of the man who had conquered the Inca Empire and taken its mountains of gold. He led about 220 Spaniards and about 4,000 Native porters, with horses and dogs.

They followed a shining dream. Stories told of a golden land deep inside South America. They spoke of a ruler who covered himself in gold dust and washed it off in a sacred lake.

Pizarro and his men pushed into the rainforest east of the Andes. The land was hard to cross, and the food ran short. Pizarro treated local people cruelly when they could not show him the way to El Dorado.

The expedition fell apart. By one account, about 140 Spaniards and about 3,000 Native people died, mostly from hunger. When the survivors came out of the jungle, the dream of El Dorado, The Gilded One, had cost thousands of lives. It had given nothing in return.

Yet the legend lived on for many more years. It drove some of the boldest and worst-fated expeditions in the history of exploration. And it left a question behind. Was there ever a city of gold? Or was this great treasure hunt built on a ritual that the Spanish misunderstood?

In the early 1500s, Spain’s conquests in the Americas brought riches that Europe had never imagined. The fall of the Aztec Empire in 1521 brought huge amounts of gold and silver. The conquest of the Inca Empire in 1533 brought even more. The ransom of the Inca emperor Atahualpa alone filled a room with gold objects. Most sources say the room was about 22 by 17 feet.

These finds made the Spanish believe that the New World held almost endless precious metal. The other European powers watched with envy. Reports came back from the interior of Colombia, Venezuela, and the Amazon basin. They told of peoples who wore gold, used gold in rituals, and spoke of greater treasures deeper in the wilderness. It was the start of a long and destructive search. It would show as much about the seekers as about what they sought.

The truth behind El Dorado is more interesting than the myth, and more modest. The original “El Dorado” meant The Gilded Man. It was not a city or an empire. It was a person. He was the zipa, the king or chief of the Muisca people.

The Muisca were one of the important civilizations of the Americas before Europeans came. They lived in the highlands of present-day Colombia, on fertile high ground around the modern capital, Bogotá.

They were not one single state. They were a loose group of chiefdoms, led by two main rulers. The zipa ruled in the south, and the zaque ruled in the north. The Muisca were skilled goldsmiths, farmers, and weavers. They were also astronomers, with a careful calendar and many gods.

The Spanish chronicles describe a remarkable ceremony at Lake Guatavita. These writings were, as always, shaped by the views and mistakes of the conquerors. The lake is a round crater lake in the hills about 57 kilometers (35 miles) northeast of Bogotá.

The story says that when a new zipa took power, his body was covered in mud and fine gold dust. He floated out onto the sacred lake on a raft. In the middle of the lake, he threw gold and emeralds into the water and washed the dust from his body. It was a gift to the gods.

Other chiefs threw in gold and emeralds too. The ritual was a ceremony of crowning and offering. It joined the new ruler with the divine powers believed to live in the water.

It was not a show of endless wealth. It was a religious act. The Muisca honored the sun god Sué, and gold was very important in their worship.

In 1969, a farmer found a small and wonderful gold object in a cave near the town of Pasca, south of Bogotá. It is called the Muisca Raft, or Balsa Muisca. It measures just 19.5 × 10.2 × 10.1 centimeters (7.7 × 4.0 × 4.0 inches).

The raft shows the El Dorado ceremony in miniature. A standing figure, the zipa, wears a headdress and a nose ornament. Smaller helpers stand around him. It was made by lost-wax casting, a method where the artist shapes a wax model and pours melted metal into the mold. The metal is a mix of gold, silver, and copper. The raft has been dated to between 1295 and 1410 CE. That is centuries before the Spanish came.

It is now the star of the Gold Museum (Museo del Oro) in Bogotá. The museum holds about 34,000 pieces of gold from before the Spanish arrived. It is described as the largest such collection in the world. The raft has become a national emblem of Colombia and appears on its stamps.

There is a bitter irony here. The raft is one of the best clues to El Dorado that has ever been found. It is not a city of gold. It is a small, beautiful model of a ritual that the Spanish badly misunderstood.

How did a Muisca ritual become one of the most lasting legends in history? It shows how misunderstanding, greed, and a lack of respect for another culture can build a myth strong enough to change a continent.

When the first Spanish explorers reached Muisca lands in the 1530s, they found a people rich in gold. But it was the way a deeply religious people is rich in sacred objects. It was not the way a mining empire is rich in gold bars. The Muisca had no gold mines in their land, and got much of their gold by trade. They used it for worship and ceremonies.

But the Spanish had already taken huge amounts of gold from Mexico and Peru. They heard about a king covered in gold dust, plunging into a lake full of treasure. Their imaginations did the rest.

The first Spanish explorer to reach the Muisca was Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada. In 1537 he led an expedition up the Magdalena River. The march through the hot lowlands was brutal, and many of his men died of disease and hunger.

He found a civilization that deeply impressed him. He took a large amount of gold, including objects from Muisca temples. But he did not find the legendary city of gold. Still, the stories he and his men carried back to the coast fed the legend.

Within a few years, the idea had grown. First it was a golden man performing a ritual. Then it was a whole city of gold. Then it was a kingdom, and finally an empire of unimaginable wealth. It lay somewhere in the wild center of South America. It lived in the gap between rumor and wishful thinking. It was always just past the next mountain ridge, the next bend in the river, the next expedition.

For about 80 years, from the 1530s to the 1610s, one European expedition after another set out to find El Dorado. Many of them went badly. In 1541, Gonzalo Pizarro marched east from Quito with about 220 Spaniards and about 4,000 Native porters. They went into the rainforest in search of the golden land.

Conditions were terrible. Pizarro’s second-in-command, Francisco de Orellana, was sent down the Napo River with about 50 men to find food. The strong current made it too hard to come back. Orellana went on to become the first European to travel the length of the Amazon River. Some say he abandoned Pizarro.

A German explorer named Philipp von Hutten searched for El Dorado in the 1540s. He was backed by the Welser banking family of Augsburg. He explored the plains east of the Andes and found no golden city. He was later killed by Spaniards. Sometimes the greatest danger in the hunt for El Dorado was other Europeans.

Another German, Nikolaus Federmann, also worked for the Welser family. In 1539 he reached the Muisca highlands, at the same time as two Spanish captains. The three of them founded Bogotá together.

Many people thought Lake Guatavita held huge amounts of sunken gold. This idea was almost as powerful as the legend of the city. In 1545, Spaniards tried to drain the lake. They lowered the water enough to get hundreds of gold pieces from the shore, but not the treasure they expected.

Later in the 1500s, a merchant named Antonio de Sepúlveda hired many workers to dig a canal. The water dropped about 20 meters. He found an emerald and some pots, but no gold.

In the early 1900s, English and German adventurers tried again. The last attempt, in 1912, failed too. Only a few gold and silver objects have ever come from the lake. Some are now in museums, including the Gold Museum in Bogotá and the British Museum.

No one shows the sad pointlessness of the El Dorado quest better than Sir Walter Raleigh (about 1552–1618). He was an English courtier, soldier, explorer, and poet, and a favorite of Queen Elizabeth I. For decades, the legend held him in its spell. He believed the golden city lay at the headwaters of the Caroní River, in what is now Venezuela.

In 1595, he led his first expedition. He explored the Orinoco region of what is now Venezuela and Guyana. He found no gold, no city, and no empire. But he wrote a famous book, The Discovery of Guiana (1596). It made big claims about the region and helped keep the legend alive for another generation. Those claims would prove fatal.

After Elizabeth died in 1603, Raleigh lost favor with the new king, James I. He was arrested for treason that year and held in the Tower of London. Years later the king let him out for one last trip to Guiana. Raleigh promised to find gold.

In 1617, Raleigh set sail with a fleet. His son, Watt Raleigh, went with a group of soldiers to attack a Spanish outpost, Santo Tomé, on the Orinoco. Raleigh had told them not to fight the Spanish. Watt was killed in the attack, and the expedition came back empty-handed.

Raleigh returned to England in disgrace. The Spanish ambassador demanded that Raleigh’s old death sentence from 1603 be carried out. The king agreed. Raleigh was beheaded on October 29, 1618. Many see it as a way for the king to calm Spain. His death is often seen as the end of the great European hunt for the golden city.

The search for El Dorado cost the lives of thousands of Europeans and local people over about 80 years. By one account, about 140 Spaniards and about 3,000 Native people died on Pizarro’s expedition, mostly from hunger. Jiménez de Quesada’s march up the Magdalena River also cost many lives.

Philipp von Hutten was killed not by the jungle but by Spaniards. Watt Raleigh died fighting the Spanish in Venezuela, and his father was executed after returning home.

The peoples of these lands suffered terribly too. They included the Muisca, the peoples of the Orinoco, and those of the Amazon basin. European diseases, slavery, and violence killed very many of them.

The Spanish conquered the Muisca in their search for gold. But the Muisca left a legacy the conquistadors could never have imagined. The Gold Museum in Bogotá holds the largest collection of pre-Hispanic goldwork in the world. Its pieces include many Muisca works.

There are pendants, nose ornaments, and breastplates. There are tunjos, which are small figures offered to the gods. And there is the Muisca Raft. They show a people of great artistic skill and deep spiritual life.

The Muisca did not use gold as money. It was a sacred material. It was used in rituals that linked the human world to the divine. The gold was not meant to be stored away. It was meant to be offered, worn, and left in lakes and caves as gifts to the gods.

Study of Lake Guatavita goes on. Historians disagree about how much of the story is true. Some think the ceremony was real. Others think the Spaniards made much of it up. Even so, the Spanish tales helped to feed the myth of El Dorado.

The real Muisca world was impressive enough. It was a group of chiefdoms with perhaps one million to three million people when the Spanish arrived. Estimates differ a great deal. They kept up long trade routes. Their goldwork was beautiful and very skillful.

The irony is deep. The Spanish destroyed a real civilization while chasing an imaginary one. The gold they wanted was never as valuable as the culture they harmed.

At heart, the story of El Dorado is about the power of a story. A ritual was changed by European greed into a city of impossible wealth. A king covered in gold dust, diving into a sacred lake, became a kingdom of fabled splendor. It became an empire that lived only in the space between what people wanted to believe and what was really there.

For about 80 years, the legend drove men to their deaths in some of the hardest lands on Earth. They died in the Amazon rainforest, the plains of Venezuela, and the Colombian highlands. It cost the lives of thousands of Europeans. It helped harm local civilizations whose true wealth was their art, their religion, their social order, and their knowledge.

The Muisca Raft in Bogotá is barely 20 centimeters long. Yet it is worth more than all the imaginary cities of gold together. It is a real object, made by real hands, showing a real ceremony of a real civilization. That civilization was conquered by people who could not see the treasure in front of them. They were too busy chasing a mirage.

The words “El Dorado” have entered the language. They mean any imaginary place of great wealth. That fits a legend that was, from beginning to end, the greatest treasure hunt that never found treasure. Did a city of gold ever exist? Everything we know says no. But the Muisca raft shows that the legend began with something real.
