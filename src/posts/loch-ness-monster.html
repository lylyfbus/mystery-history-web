Loch Ness Monster: 1,500 Years of Sightings, Sonar Contacts, and the Photograph That Fooled the World

It is the year 565 AD. An Irish monk named Saint Columba is traveling through the Scottish Highlands. By the River Ness he meets a group of men burying a friend. The man had been swimming, they say. A water beast attacked him and dragged him under.

Columba sends one of his own followers into the river to test the creature. The beast rises from the water, roaring, with its jaws wide open. Columba raises his hand and makes the sign of the cross. He commands the monster to leave. It flees.

An abbot named Adomnan of Iona wrote this story down a century later, in his Life of Columba. It is the earliest known account of a creature in Loch Ness. The legend has lasted for nearly fifteen hundred years.

Loch Ness is the largest body of fresh water in Britain by volume. It holds more water than all the lakes in England and Wales put together. It is 23 miles long and up to 754 feet deep. Glaciers carved it during the last Ice Age, and its depths are still mostly unexplored.

The water is so dark with peat that you cannot see more than a few feet below the surface. Peat is old, rotted plant matter that stains water brown. For centuries, people in the Highlands whispered that something ancient lived in the loch.

Then, in the 1930s, the whispers became a roar. A new road was built along the western shore of the loch, and tourists began to come. Sightings multiplied. Photographs appeared.

Nessie, the Loch Ness Monster, became the most famous cryptid in the world. A cryptid is a creature that some people say exists but that science has not proven. Nessie has led to thousands of sightings and dozens of scientific expeditions. Tourism linked to her is said to be worth 25 to 41 million pounds a year to the Scottish Highlands.

The question is not whether people have seen something in Loch Ness. They have, hundreds of times. The question is what they saw. And can the truth ever come out of waters so dark and so deep?

The modern story began in 1933. That year a new road was built along the western shore. Workers cleared trees, and views of the water that had been hidden were suddenly open.

In April, Aldie Mackay, the manageress of the Drumnadrochit Hotel, said she had seen a whale-like creature in the loch. The Inverness Courier printed her account, and it spread across Britain.

In July, a man named George Spicer and his wife were driving along the new road. They saw a large creature with a long neck cross the road in front of their car and plunge into the water. Spicer called it “the nearest approach to a dragon or prehistoric animal I ever saw in my life.” The national newspapers rushed to the loch.

On November 12, 1933, Hugh Gray took what is thought to be the first photograph of the monster. It was blurry. It showed a dark, long shape on the water.

Skeptics said it was a dog carrying a stick, an otter, or a swimming deer. Believers saw a creature. The photograph set a pattern that would repeat for ninety years. Unclear pictures convinced the people who already believed, and nobody else.

Then came the photograph that changed everything. On April 19, 1934, a London doctor named Robert Kenneth Wilson sent a picture to the Daily Mail. It showed a long, thin neck rising from the water of Loch Ness. The creature looked like a plesiosaur, a sea reptile from the age of the dinosaurs.

People called it “the Surgeon’s Photograph.” It became the most famous image of the monster. It appeared on postcards, book covers, and magazine fronts around the world.

It was also a hoax, as people learned sixty years later. In 1994, researchers traced the picture to Marmaduke Wetherell, a big-game hunter. The Daily Mail had hired him to find the monster.

Earlier, a fake monster trail had made Wetherell look foolish. So he asked his son Ian and a sculptor named Christian Spurling for help. They built a toy submarine, about twelve inches long, with a carved neck and head.

Wilson was a respected surgeon. He agreed to lend his name to the photograph so that people would believe it. The picture was taken from far enough away to make the tiny model look huge.

The truth came from Spurling himself, in a 1993 interview on his deathbed. For sixty years the photograph had been the best visual evidence for the monster. And it showed a twelve-inch toy.

If the photographs proved nothing, the sonar evidence was more interesting. But it was no more certain. Sonar uses sound waves to find objects under water.

From the 1960s on, scientific expeditions used sonar to scan the depths. Several found large, moving objects that nobody could name right away.

In 1960, an aeronautical engineer named Tim Dinsdale filmed a hump-shaped object moving across the surface. The Joint Air Reconnaissance Intelligence Centre studied the film. It said the object was “probably animate,” which means alive. It judged that the object moved at about 6 to 7 knots.

Dinsdale was so sure that he gave up his engineering career. He spent the rest of his life hunting the monster. Over the following decades he made several more films and photographs.

The biggest sonar survey was Operation Deepscan in 1987. Twenty boats with sonar sailed in a line across the loch. They found three large underwater contacts that known fish could not explain.

One of them was larger than a shark but smaller than a whale. It was about 600 feet down. The results were tantalizing but not final. The contacts could have been schools of fish, seals, or other known animals.

A BBC expedition in 2003 used 600 separate sonar beams. It found no sign of any large animal in the loch.

In 2019, Professor Neil Gemmell led the most thorough scientific search of Loch Ness so far. He is a geneticist at the University of Otago in New Zealand. His team took water samples from many places around the loch.

From the samples they pulled out environmental DNA, or eDNA. DNA is the code inside every living cell. All living things shed bits of it into the water around them.

The study found no reptile DNA at all. That rules out the plesiosaur idea, which had been the most popular explanation since the 1930s. It found no sturgeon DNA, no catfish DNA, and no shark DNA.

What it did find was a lot of eel DNA. These were European eels. They swim to Loch Ness from the Sargasso Sea, a journey of more than 3,100 miles.

Gemmell said the eel DNA did not prove that giant eels exist. But the data fit the idea that large eels could explain some sightings. The team listed more than 3,000 species from the loch’s waters. Nothing in decades had added more to the debate.

Loch Ness is unusual for its rocks as well as its water. It lies along the Great Glen Fault. That is a deep crack in the land that cuts Scotland in two, from coast to coast.

The loch is very deep, up to 754 feet. It holds about 7.4 billion cubic meters of water, more than all the lakes in England and Wales combined. The water is nearly opaque because of the peat, so you can see only a few feet below the surface. The temperature stays steady all year, at about 5 degrees Celsius.

Great depth, no visibility, and cold steady water make a full search almost impossible. They also give the imagination room to fill in what the eyes cannot see.

Two more things may explain some sightings. The loch has seiches, which are standing waves that make the water level rise and fall by several inches. It also has layers of water at different temperatures. These can make mirage-like effects on the surface.

The Loch Ness Monster is no longer just a mystery. It is a business. The Highlands are said to earn 25 to 41 million pounds a year from Nessie tourism. More than 500,000 visitors come to the loch each year, to a region that would otherwise attract far fewer.

The village of Drumnadrochit has about 1,100 people. It is home to two rival museums. One is the Loch Ness Centre and Exhibition. The other is the Loch Ness Monster Exhibition Centre. Each tells the story in its own way.

Boat tours cross the loch every day. Hotels, restaurants, gift shops, and petrol stations all sell the Nessie name.

This creates an odd puzzle. The monster is too valuable to be proven real, because that would end the mystery. It is also too valuable to be proven false, because that would kill the tourism.

When a study fails to find Nessie, the news says “Nessie still not found.” It does not say “Nessie does not exist.” A blurry photograph or a strange sonar echo is called “new evidence,” not an unclear result. So the cycle of guessing goes on. It has outlived every study, every skeptic, and every exposed hoax.

People have offered several ideas about what they saw. The first is the plesiosaur, a prehistoric sea reptile that somehow survived. The 2019 eDNA study found no reptile DNA, so this idea is now ruled out.

The second is the giant eel. Gemmell’s study raised it, because the loch holds so much European eel DNA. The third is the sturgeon, a big fish that can reach twelve feet or more. Its humps fit some descriptions, but no sturgeon DNA was found.

Next come swimming deer or elk. Their heads and necks above the water could look like a creature with a long neck. Then there are optical illusions. The loch’s layers of different temperatures and its seiches can make mirages and odd shapes on the surface.

The last idea is gas from the Great Glen Fault. It may release bubbles that disturb the surface in a way that looks like a large animal.

The Loch Ness Monster has appeared in almost every kind of popular culture. Nessie showed up in Doctor Who (“Terror of the Zygons,” 1975), Scooby-Doo, The Simpsons, and South Park. The 1996 film Loch Ness, with Ted Danson, took a thoughtful view of the legend. The animated film The Water Horse (2007) gave a family-friendly version.

Nessie is in countless books, video games, comic strips, and advertisements. She even has an official list. The Official Loch Ness Monster Sightings Register is kept by Gary Campbell, a chartered accountant. He has logged more than 1,100 sightings since 1996.

The Loch Ness Monster is not a question for science alone. It is a cultural phenomenon, a story people tell and love. The 2019 eDNA study came as close to a final answer as science is likely to give. There is no plesiosaur in Loch Ness, no sturgeon, and no unknown sea reptile.

There are eels, lots of them. And there is a vast, dark, cold lake that can create strange optical effects. There are also people, hundreds of them, who truly saw something they could not explain.

Were they all mistaken? Almost certainly. The human brain is a pattern-matching machine. It sees faces in clouds and animals in waves, especially when it expects them.

But the power of the legend does not depend on a monster. It depends on the possibility of one. In a world that is mapped, measured, and digitized down to the last square meter, there might still be something in the deep that we have not found. The human need for mystery is as real and as deep as Loch Ness itself. So what is down there?
