Loch Ness Monster: An Old Legend, Sonar Contacts, and the Photograph That Fooled the World

The old story begins around the year 565 AD. An Irish monk named Saint Columba is traveling through the Scottish Highlands. By the River Ness he meets a group of men burying a friend. The man had been swimming, they say. A water beast attacked him and dragged him under.

Columba sends one of his own followers into the river to test the creature. The beast rises from the water, roaring, with its jaws wide open. Columba raises his hand and makes the sign of the cross. He commands the monster to leave. It flees.

An abbot named Adomnan of Iona wrote this story down about a century later, in his Life of Columba. It is the earliest known written account of a creature in Loch Ness. It is a tale about a saint, not an eyewitness report. The legend has lasted for nearly fifteen hundred years.

Loch Ness is the largest body of fresh water in Britain by volume. It holds more water than all the lakes in England and Wales put together. It is 23 miles long and up to 754 feet deep. Glaciers carved it during the last Ice Age, and its dark depths are hard to search.

The water is so dark with peat that you can see very little below the surface. Peat is old, rotted plant matter that stains water brown. For centuries, people in the Highlands whispered that something ancient lived in the loch.

Then, in the 1930s, the whispers became a roar. A new road was built along the loch, and more visitors came. Sightings multiplied. Photographs appeared.

Nessie, the Loch Ness Monster, became one of the most famous cryptids in the world. A cryptid is a creature that some people say exists but that science has not proven. Nessie has led to many reported sightings and many searches. A 2018 study by Gary Campbell, who runs a Nessie fan club, said her tourism is worth about 41 million pounds a year to Scotland.

The question is not whether people have seen something in Loch Ness. They have, many times. The question is what they saw. And can the truth ever come out of waters so dark and so deep?

The modern story began in 1933. That year a new road was built along the loch.

On April 15, Aldie Mackay and her husband were driving beside the loch. She said she saw a whale-like creature in the water. The Inverness Courier printed the story on May 2, and it spread.

On July 22, a man named George Spicer and his wife were driving along the loch. They saw a large creature with a long neck cross the road in front of their car and plunge into the water. Spicer called it “the nearest approach to a dragon or pre-historic animal that I have ever seen in my life.” Some researchers now doubt that his story was true. But at the time, the newspapers took notice.

On November 12, 1933, Hugh Gray took what is said to be the first photograph of the monster. It was blurry. It showed a dark, long shape on the water. Some people think it shows a dog with a stick.

Skeptics said it was a dog, an otter, or a swimming deer. Believers saw a creature. The photograph set a pattern that would repeat for many years. Unclear pictures convinced the people who already believed, and nobody else.

Then came the photograph that changed everything. A London doctor named Robert Kenneth Wilson said he took a picture on April 19, 1934. The Daily Mail printed it two days later. It showed a long, thin neck rising from the water of Loch Ness. The creature looked like a plesiosaur, a sea reptile from the age of the dinosaurs.

People called it “the Surgeon’s Photograph.” It became the most famous image of the monster. It appeared on postcards, book covers, and magazine fronts around the world.

It was also a hoax, as people learned about sixty years later. In 1994, a report named Marmaduke Wetherell, a big-game hunter, as the man behind it. The Daily Mail had hired him to find the monster.

Earlier, in December 1933, Wetherell had found big footprints on the shore. Experts said they were made with a dried hippo foot, and he looked foolish. The story says he then asked his son Ian and his stepson Christian Spurling, a sculptor, for help. They built a toy submarine with a carved neck and head.

Wilson was a respected surgeon. The story says he agreed to lend his name to the photograph so that people would believe it. A small model on the water, seen from far away, can look huge.

The tale came from Spurling himself, near the end of his life. Sources disagree on exactly when he told it. For about sixty years the photograph had been the best visual evidence for the monster. And it may have shown a toy.

If the photographs proved nothing, the sonar evidence was more interesting. But it was no more certain. Sonar uses sound waves to find objects under water.

From 1958 onward, scientific expeditions used sonar to scan the depths. Several found large, moving objects that they could not explain.

In 1960, an aeronautical engineer named Tim Dinsdale filmed a hump-shaped object moving across the surface. In 1966, the Joint Air Reconnaissance Intelligence Centre studied the film. It said the object was “probably animate,” which means alive. Experts now think its estimates were too bold.

Dinsdale was so sure that he gave up his engineering career. He spent the rest of his life hunting the monster.

The biggest sonar survey was Operation Deepscan in 1987. About twenty boats with sonar sailed in a line across the loch. Some reports say twenty boats and others say twenty-four. They found three underwater targets that nobody could explain right away.

One strong echo came from about 590 feet down, near Urquhart Bay. The results were interesting but not final. The contacts could have been schools of fish, seals, or other known animals.

A BBC expedition in 2003 used 600 separate sonar beams. It found no sign of any large animal in the loch.

In 2019, Professor Neil Gemmell led a large DNA survey of Loch Ness. He is a geneticist at the University of Otago in New Zealand. His team took 250 water samples from many places around the loch.

From the samples they pulled out environmental DNA, or eDNA. DNA is the code inside every living cell. All living things shed bits of it into the water around them.

The study found no reptile DNA at all. Gemmell said a giant reptile like a plesiosaur is very unlikely. It found no sturgeon DNA, no catfish DNA, and no shark DNA.

What it did find was a lot of eel DNA, at almost every place they tested.

Gemmell said the eel DNA did not prove that giant eels exist. But he said giant eels remain a possible idea. Reports say the team found DNA from about 3,000 species in all.

Loch Ness is unusual for its rocks as well as its water. It lies along the Great Glen Fault. That is a long crack in the rock that runs across Scotland.

The loch is very deep, up to 754 feet. It holds about 7.5 billion cubic meters of water, more than all the lakes in England and Wales combined. The water is very murky because of the peat.

Great depth and dark water make a full search very hard. They also give the imagination room to fill in what the eyes cannot see.

The Loch Ness Monster is no longer just a mystery. It is a business. One 2018 study said Nessie tourism is worth about 41 million pounds a year. Hundreds of thousands of visitors come to the loch area each year.

Boat tours, hotels, restaurants, and gift shops all use the Nessie name.

This creates an odd puzzle. Some people say the monster is worth more as a mystery than as an answer.

When a study fails to find Nessie, the news says “Nessie still not found.” It does not say “Nessie does not exist.” A blurry photograph or a strange sonar echo is called “new evidence,” not an unclear result. So the cycle of guessing goes on. It has outlived every study, every skeptic, and every exposed hoax.

People have offered several ideas about what they saw. The first is the plesiosaur, a prehistoric sea reptile that somehow survived. The 2019 eDNA study found no reptile DNA, so this idea is now very unlikely.

The second is the giant eel. Gemmell’s study raised it, because the loch holds so much European eel DNA. The third is the sturgeon, a big fish. Its humps fit some descriptions, but no sturgeon DNA was found.

Next come swimming deer. Their heads and necks above the water could look like a creature with a long neck. Then there are waves, floating logs, and tricks of the light. These can make odd shapes on the surface.

The Loch Ness Monster has appeared in many kinds of popular culture. Nessie shows up in TV shows, films, books, video games, comic strips, and advertisements.

She even has an official list. Gary Campbell, a chartered accountant, keeps a register of reported sightings.

The Loch Ness Monster is not a question for science alone. It is a cultural phenomenon, a story people tell and love. The 2019 eDNA study is the closest thing to an answer so far. It found no sign of a plesiosaur, a sturgeon, or an unknown sea reptile.

There are eels, lots of them. And there is a vast, dark, cold lake that can create strange optical effects. There are also people, hundreds of them, who truly saw something they could not explain.

Were they all mistaken? Most scientists think so. The human brain is a pattern-matching machine. It sees faces in clouds and animals in waves, especially when it expects them.

But the power of the legend does not depend on a monster. It depends on the possibility of one. In a world that is mapped, measured, and digitized down to the last square meter, there might still be something in the deep that we have not found. The human need for mystery is as real and as deep as Loch Ness itself. So what is down there?
