The Great Emu War: When Australia's Military Lost to 20,000 Birds

In November 1932, the country of Australia did something very strange. It sent soldiers with machine guns to fight birds. They were not enemy planes or drones. They were emus. Emus are large, flightless birds that live in Australia, and they are surprisingly fast.

These emus had walked into wheat fields in the Campion district of Western Australia. They were eating the crops. The government sent soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery, and Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence, agreed to the plan. The soldiers carried two Lewis machine guns and 10,000 bullets.

About 20,000 emus had moved into the farming district. They were ruining the wheat of poor farmers, many of them former soldiers. What followed was one of the silliest military campaigns in history. It lasted about five weeks. Trained soldiers with machine guns could not stop large, fast birds that were very good at running away.

The soldiers withdrew on December 10, 1932. The commander reported that 986 emus had been killed. He said that about 10,000 bullets had been used. Many people doubt his numbers. The campaign was widely laughed at. In Parliament, people asked about it. In the end, the army was not sent back to fight the birds.

The story begins after World War I. The war ended in 1918, and the Australian government had to help thousands of soldiers return to normal life. Its answer was the Soldier Settlement Scheme. It gave returned soldiers pieces of farmland. Often this was poor land that an experienced farmer would have avoided.

In Western Australia, large parts of the Wheatbelt were given to former soldiers. These included the Campion district. The farmers were told to grow wheat. The government promised them help with money.

Many of the promised payments never came. They struggled to grow wheat in dry, poor soil. On top of that, the Great Depression had begun in 1929. It pushed the price of wheat down. They were barely getting by when the emus arrived.

The emu has the scientific name Dromaius novaehollandiae. It is the second-largest living bird by height, after the ostrich. It lives across most of Australia. An adult can be up to 6.2 feet tall and weigh up to about 120 pounds.

Emus cannot fly, but they are not helpless. They can run at up to 30 miles per hour for a good distance. They are very agile for their size.

When in danger, emus do not stand and fight. They scatter and run in many directions. That makes them very hard targets for hunters. As the Australian Army would learn, it also made them hard targets for machine-gun crews.

In 1932, about 20,000 emus moved from the inland toward the coast of Western Australia. Emus do this each year after the breeding season, looking for food and water. This time their path crossed the wheat fields. The crops offered food and water.

The birds came down on the fields in huge flocks. They ate the wheat and trampled the young plants. They also damaged fences that farmers had put up.

Many of these farmers had survived the horrors of the Western Front, the battle lines of World War I in Europe. Now giant birds were ruining them. It was the last straw.

There was a funny irony here. The emu is on the Australian coat of arms, next to the kangaroo. Both animals are native to Australia. People often say that neither animal can easily walk backward. That is meant to show a nation that only moves forward.

King George V granted the current coat of arms in 1912. That was twenty years before the government sent soldiers to shoot the bird that stands for the nation.

The farmers’ complaints reached Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence. He agreed to a military operation, with some conditions. The farmers were a sympathetic group. They were men who had served their country in war and were now being ruined by birds.

Pearce sent soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery under Major G.P.W. Meredith. There were only three soldiers. They carried two Lewis guns, which were light machine guns used a lot in World War I. The guns worked well against enemy soldiers. Against emus, they did far worse than expected.

The first battle took place on November 2, 1932, near Campion. The soldiers saw about 50 emus. The first volley did little. The emus scattered and ran fast across open ground. Only about a dozen birds were killed later that day.

The soldiers were used to human enemies. They were not used to enemies who sprinted away at 30 miles per hour. It was hard to follow and hit the birds. One gun jammed after about twelve birds had been killed.

Major Meredith later made a famous remark. He said that the emus “can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks.”

On November 4, 1932, Meredith tried again near a local dam. More than 1,000 emus were spotted. But the emus scattered in every direction as soon as the firing began. The gun jammed again. A gun was mounted on a truck to chase the fleeing birds. But the truck could not keep up on the rough ground. The shaking made it impossible to aim.

By November 8, about 2,500 bullets had been fired. Few emus had been killed. On that day, Pearce withdrew the soldiers.

The soldiers went back to the farms on November 13. This second try lasted until December 10. Meredith claimed 986 confirmed kills in all. He said that he used 9,860 bullets, which is exactly ten for each bird. This is why many people doubt the figure. He also said that about 2,500 more birds had been wounded and would die. Nobody could check that.

The Great Emu War was a public relations disaster for the government. The campaign was widely mocked in the press. Many people saw it as a publicity stunt. Pearce faced questions about the wisdom of sending the army to fight birds.

In Parliament, a member named Rowley James asked the Prime Minister if a medal would be struck for the soldiers. This was a joke. But in the Emu War, the line between joke and plan was always thin.

After the army left, farmers had to deal with the emus themselves. A bounty system had begun in 1923. It paid hunters a set amount for each emu killed. In six months of 1934 alone, more than 57,000 bounties were claimed. Later, Western Australia also built a long emu-proof fence around its wheat areas.

Hunters on the ground, working alone or in small groups, could get close to the birds. That suited the land and the birds better than noisy machine-gun ambushes.

Dominic Serventy was an Australian bird expert. He later said that the emus acted a little like guerrilla fighters. They split into small groups and scattered. That made them nearly impossible targets for a planned military attack.

At heart, the Great Emu War of 1932 is a story about the limits of human power. The Australian government sent trained soldiers with machine guns to fight a flock of flightless birds, and the birds won. They did not win by courage or by planning. The emus simply did what emus do. They ran. They scattered. They took bullets and kept going.

They showed that a determined animal with strong legs, and no idea of army ranks, is very hard to stop with a machine gun. The Emu War is funny. It is hard not to laugh at soldiers waiting in ambush for giant birds, only to watch them sprint away while the guns jam.

But it also reminds us that people are not always the masters of nature that we think we are. Bounties and fences did more than the army did. Even so, there are almost three-quarters of a million emus in Australia today. Nature is not an enemy to defeat. It is a force to handle with respect, patience, and humility.
