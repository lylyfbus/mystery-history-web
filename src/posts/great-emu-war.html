The Great Emu War: When Australia's Military Lost to 20,000 Birds

Three soldiers meet fifty emus

It is November 2, 1932, near Campion, in Western Australia. Three soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery have arrived with two Lewis guns, which are light machine guns, and 10,000 bullets. Their commander is Major G.P.W. Meredith. In front of them stand about 50 emus.

Emus are large, flightless birds that live in Australia. These ones have walked into wheat fields and are eating the crops. The soldiers open fire. The first volley does little. The birds scatter and run fast across open ground.

These were not enemy planes or drones. They were birds, and surprisingly fast ones. About 20,000 of them had moved into the farming district, and they were ruining the wheat of poor farmers, many of them former soldiers.

This is the start of what people call the Great Emu War. It lasted about five weeks. Trained soldiers with machine guns could not stop large birds that were very good at running away. It sounds like a joke, but the story begins with real trouble. Let us go back and see how soldiers and birds ended up on the same fields.

Soldiers who became farmers

The story begins after World War I. The war ended in 1918, and the Australian government had to help thousands of soldiers return to normal life. Its answer was the Soldier Settlement Scheme. It gave returned soldiers pieces of farmland. Often this was poor land that an experienced farmer would have avoided.

In Western Australia, large parts of the Wheatbelt were given to former soldiers. These included the Campion district. The farmers were told to grow wheat, and the government promised them help with money.

Many of the promised payments never came. The farmers struggled to grow wheat in dry, poor soil. On top of that, the Great Depression began in 1929. It pushed the price of wheat down. They were barely getting by when the emus arrived.

A bird that does not fight

The emu has the scientific name Dromaius novaehollandiae. It is the second-largest living bird by height, after the ostrich. An adult can be up to 6.2 feet tall and weigh up to about 120 pounds. Emus cannot fly, but they are not helpless. They can run at up to 30 miles per hour for a good distance.

When in danger, emus do not stand and fight. They scatter and run in many directions. That makes them very hard targets for hunters. As the army would learn, it also made them hard targets for machine-gun crews.

In 1932, about 20,000 emus moved from the inland toward the coast of Western Australia. Emus do this each year after the breeding season, looking for food and water. This time their path crossed the wheat fields.

Giant birds on the wheat

The birds came down on the fields in huge flocks. They ate the wheat and trampled the young plants. They also damaged the fences that the farmers had put up. Many of these farmers had survived the Western Front, the battle lines of World War I in Europe. Now giant birds were ruining them. It was the last straw.

The farmers’ complaints reached Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence. The farmers were a sympathetic group. They were men who had served their country in war. Pearce agreed to a military operation, with some conditions.

There was a funny irony here. The emu is on the Australian coat of arms, next to the kangaroo. People often say that neither animal can easily walk backward, which is meant to show a nation that only moves forward. King George V granted the coat of arms in 1912. That was twenty years before the government sent soldiers to shoot the bird that stands for the nation.

Birds that would not hold still

Back to the fields. On November 2, the soldiers learned a hard lesson. They were used to human enemies. They were not used to enemies who sprinted away at 30 miles per hour. Only about a dozen birds were killed that day.

Meredith later made a famous remark. He said that the emus “can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks.”

On November 4, he tried again near a local dam. More than 1,000 emus were spotted. But the emus scattered in every direction as soon as the firing began. One gun jammed after about twelve birds had been killed. A gun was mounted on a truck to chase the fleeing birds. But the truck could not keep up on the rough ground. The shaking made it impossible to aim.

By November 8, about 2,500 bullets had been fired. Few emus had been killed. On that day, Pearce withdrew the soldiers.

One more try

The soldiers went back to the farms on November 13. This second try lasted until December 10. Meredith claimed 986 confirmed kills in all. He said that he used 9,860 bullets, which is exactly ten for each bird. This is why many people doubt the figure. He also said that about 2,500 more birds had been wounded and would die. Nobody could check that.

The campaign was widely mocked in the press. Many people saw it as a publicity stunt. Pearce faced questions about the wisdom of sending the army to fight birds. In Parliament, a member named Rowley James asked the Prime Minister if a medal would be struck for the soldiers. It was a joke. But in the Emu War, the line between joke and plan was always thin.

In the end, the army was not sent back to fight the birds.

Who really won?

After the army left, farmers had to deal with the emus themselves. A bounty system had begun in 1923. It paid hunters a set amount for each emu killed. In six months of 1934 alone, more than 57,000 bounties were claimed. Later, Western Australia also built a long emu-proof fence around its wheat areas.

Hunters on the ground, working alone or in small groups, could get close to the birds. That suited the land and the birds better than noisy machine-gun ambushes.

Dominic Serventy, an Australian bird expert, later said that the emus acted a little like guerrilla fighters. They split into small groups and scattered. That made them nearly impossible targets for a planned military attack.

So what do we know? Trained soldiers with machine guns could not stop a flock of flightless birds. The emus did not win by planning. They did what emus do. They ran, they scattered, and they kept going. What do we not know? The real number of birds that fell, because Meredith’s figures cannot be checked.

Think again of those three soldiers at Campion, facing 50 birds. It is hard not to laugh at guns that jam while giant birds sprint away. But the story also reminds us that people are not always the masters of nature that we think we are. Bounties and fences did more than the army did. Even so, there are almost three-quarters of a million emus in Australia today.
