The Great Emu War: When Australia's Military Lost to 20,000 Birds

In November 1932, the country of Australia did something that no other country had done before or since. It sent its army to fight birds. They were not enemy planes or drones. They were emus. Emus are large, flightless birds that live in Australia, and they are surprisingly fast.

These emus had walked into wheat fields in the Campion district of Western Australia. They were eating the crops. The government sent soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery, and Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence, directed the plan. The soldiers carried Lewis machine guns that could fire 500 to 600 bullets a minute.

About 20,000 emus had moved into the farming district. They were ruining the wheat of poor farmers who were former soldiers. What followed was one of the silliest military campaigns in human history. It lasted a month. Trained soldiers with automatic guns could not hit large, fast birds that were very good at running away.

The soldiers withdrew on December 10, 1932. The official count was 986 emus killed out of about 20,000. It had cost about 10,000 bullets. The newspapers laughed. Parliament argued about it. In the end, the plan was dropped for a simple bounty system, which worked much better. A bounty is money paid for each animal killed.

The story begins after World War I. The war ended in 1918, and the Australian government had to help thousands of soldiers return to normal life. Its answer was the Soldier Settlement Scheme. It gave returned soldiers pieces of farmland. Often this was poor land that an experienced farmer would have avoided.

In Western Australia, large parts of the Wheatbelt were given to former soldiers. These included the Campion district near the town of Walgoolan. The farmers were told to grow wheat. The government promised money to help, farming support, and fences to protect the crops.

The money came slowly. The farming help was small. The fences were never built. Many of the veterans had never farmed. They struggled to grow wheat in dry, poor soil. On top of that, the Great Depression had begun in 1929. It pushed the price of wheat to record lows. They were barely getting by when the emus arrived.

The emu has the scientific name Dromaius novaehollandiae. It is the second-largest living bird by height, after the ostrich. It lives only in Australia. An adult can be up to 6.2 feet tall and weigh up to 130 pounds.

Emus cannot fly, but they are not helpless. They can run at up to 30 miles per hour for a long time. They can go even faster in short bursts. They are very agile for their size.

They are also tough. They can live in the harsh Australian outback for weeks without food or water. Their mood is, to put it kindly, “unbothered.” When in danger, emus do not stand and fight. They scatter and run in many directions at high speed. That makes them very hard targets for hunters. As the Australian Army would learn, it also makes them hard targets for machine-gun crews.

In late 1932, about 20,000 emus moved inland from the coast of Western Australia. They were following their usual seasonal pattern, looking for food and water. The long, dry summer had cut down their usual food. Then they found something better. The wheat fields of Campion were full of ripe grain and had plenty of water from the farmers’ irrigation systems.

The birds came down on the fields in huge flocks. They ate the wheat and trampled the young plants. Maybe worst of all, they broke the rabbit-proof fences that farmers had built with great effort. Rabbits then poured into the fields and made things worse.

Many of these farmers had survived the horrors of the Western Front, the battle lines of World War I in Europe. Now giant birds were ruining them. It was the last straw.

There was a funny irony here. The emu is on the Australian coat of arms, next to the kangaroo. Both were chosen because they are native to Australia. Popular belief says that neither animal can walk backward. That is meant to show a nation that only moves forward.

King George V granted the coat of arms in 1912. That was twenty years before the government sent soldiers to shoot the bird that stands for the nation.

The farmers’ complaints reached Sir George Pearce, the Minister of Defence. He approved a military operation. The farmers were a sympathetic group. They were men who had served their country in war and were now being ruined by birds. Farm groups also pressed the government to act.

Pearce sent soldiers of the Royal Australian Artillery under Major G.P.W. Meredith. They carried Lewis Guns, which were light machine guns used a lot in World War I. A Lewis Gun fired 500 to 600 rounds a minute. It was usually fed from a drum holding 47 rounds. It worked well against enemy soldiers. Against emus, it did far worse than expected.

The first battle took place on November 2, 1932, in the Campion district. Meredith and his men set up near a group of emus and opened fire. The result was disappointing. The noise startled the emus, and they scattered almost at once. They broke into small groups and ran fast across open ground.

The soldiers were used to human enemies, who usually took cover. They were not used to enemies who sprinted away at 30 miles per hour. It was hard to follow and hit the birds. Many bullets were fired, but few emus were killed. The Lewis Guns often jammed in the dust. While a gun was stuck, the surviving emus simply ran out of range.

Major Meredith seems to have been a professional soldier with a dry sense of humor. He later made a famous remark. He said: “If we had a military division with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds it would face any army in the world... They can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks.”

After the first failures, Meredith changed his tactics. On November 4, 1932, he tried a big attack near a local dam. About 1,000 emus had gathered there. The plan was to ambush them from close by and use the combined fire of the Lewis Guns to bring down many birds.

But the emus fooled the soldiers again. As soon as the firing began, they scattered in every direction. The guns jammed again and again. At one point, a gun was mounted on a truck to chase the fleeing birds. But the truck could not keep up on the rough ground. The shaking made it impossible to aim. The idea of hunting emus by truck was dropped quickly.

By the end of November 4, about 2,500 bullets had been used. Few kills were confirmed. The operation was paused on November 8 while Meredith reported to his superiors.

The Great Emu War was a public relations disaster for the government. Newspapers had a field day. They gave it the name “The Great Emu War.” Headlines mocked the army for being unable to beat a flock of birds. Australian papers printed cartoons of emus dodging machine-gun fire and soldiers running from huge birds.

In Parliament, people debated the operation with a mix of anger and laughter. Sir George Pearce, who had approved it, faced sharp questions about the wisdom of sending the army to fight birds. One member of Parliament reportedly asked if a “war medal” would be given for the campaign. Another suggested that the soldiers should be sent back with “greater firepower.” This was probably a joke. But in the Emu War, the line between joke and plan was always thin.

The army withdrew on December 10, 1932. The government then gave farmers ammunition, reportedly 500,000 rounds. It urged them to shoot the emus themselves. More importantly, it strengthened the bounty system. This had begun in 1923. It paid farmers a set amount for each emu scalp.

The bounty system worked much better than the army, for a simple reason. Single farmers, hunting alone or in small groups, could creep up quietly and shoot with rifles at close range. That suited the land and the birds far better than noisy machine-gun ambushes.

From 1945 to 1960, the bounty system led to 284,794 emu scalps being handed in in Western Australia. That is far more than the army’s 986 birds. The lesson was clear. For handling wild animals, patient hunters with rifles do better than soldiers with machine guns.

The Great Emu War was mostly forgotten for decades. People remembered it only as an odd story from Australian history. In the 2010s, it suddenly came back as an internet meme. The picture of soldiers with machine guns fighting giant flightless birds, and losing, was too good to resist. The Emu War became popular in history jokes, Reddit posts, YouTube videos, and social media.

Dominic Serventy was one of Australia’s leading bird scientists. He helped explain how emus escaped. He noted that the birds’ natural response to danger is to scatter in many directions at high speed. That made them nearly impossible targets for a planned military attack.

The Emu War has also appeared in video games, television shows, and comedy acts. People often cite it as one of the funniest examples of conflict between humans and wildlife.

At heart, the Great Emu War of 1932 is a story about the limits of human power. The Australian government sent trained soldiers with automatic guns to fight a flock of flightless birds, and the birds won. They did not win by courage or by planning. The emus simply did what emus do. They ran. They scattered. They took bullets and kept going.

They proved that a determined animal with strong legs, and no idea of army ranks, is very hard to wipe out with a machine gun. The Emu War is funny. It is hard not to laugh at soldiers crouching behind Lewis Guns, waiting in ambush for giant birds, only to watch them sprint away while the guns jam.

But it also reminds us that people are not always the masters of nature that we think we are. The farmers of the Campion district did find a solution in the end. The bounty system worked where the army did not. The emu numbers were slowly brought under control by patient, steady work, not by dramatic military operations. Nature is not an enemy to defeat. It is a force to handle with respect, patience, and humility.
