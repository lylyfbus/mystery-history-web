Thutmose II Tomb: The Lost Pharaoh Found

Dr. Piers Litherland led a team that dug in a dry, rocky valley in Egypt. Deep in a cliff, they reached a dark room. Pieces of painted plaster showed a blue sky covered with yellow stars.

In ancient Egypt, a ceiling like that was a sign of a king's tomb. Litherland has said that finding such clues moved him deeply. He had searched for years and had never truly expected to find them.

In February 2025, the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities told the world the news. The lost tomb of Pharaoh Thutmose II had been found. A pharaoh was a king of ancient Egypt. Thutmose II ruled during the 18th Dynasty. Some sources date his reign from about 1493 to 1479 BCE. A dynasty is a line of rulers from one family.

The news surprised experts everywhere. Egypt has had many pharaohs, so a pharaoh alone was not the surprise. The surprise was the long wait. The Ministry called it the first pharaoh's tomb found since 1922. In that year, Howard Carter found the tomb of Tutankhamun. Some experts say this wording is too strong, because other royal tombs have been found since then. But for the early New Kingdom, the find is rare.

That is a gap of about one hundred years. In that time, people split the atom and walked on the Moon. They mapped the human genome, the full set of instructions inside our cells. They joined the world together with the internet. Yet the grave of a king who ruled about three and a half thousand years ago stayed hidden under the desert.

Most pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty were buried in the famous Valley of the Kings. This tomb was not there. It lay about 2.45 kilometers away, in a wild and rocky area called the Western Wadis. A wadi is a dry valley in the desert. The exact place is known as Wadi Gabbanat el-Qurud.

The team that found it was a joint Egyptian and British mission. Its leader, Dr. Litherland, is an archaeologist linked to the University of Cambridge. Archaeology is the study of the past through the things people left behind.

The tomb they found was in a sad state. Floods had wrecked it. Its contents were gone or scattered, and its wall paintings were in small pieces. But the signs were clear. This was the tomb of a king.

The find matters for more than one man's grave. Thutmose II stood at an important turning point in the early New Kingdom. His wife was Hatshepsut, who took the throne after he died. She became one of the most powerful and famous women ever to rule Egypt as pharaoh.

His son was Thutmose III, a brilliant general. Some historians call him the "Napoleon of Egypt." He led at least 16 military campaigns. His empire reached from the Euphrates River to Nubia.

The tomb also fills a hole in what we know. In the early 18th Dynasty, the custom of burying pharaohs near the city of Thebes was still taking shape. The burial ground there is called the Theban Necropolis. The tomb shows us how kings may have been buried at that early time.

It raises new questions too. Why was Thutmose II buried so far from the Valley of the Kings? What happened to the things inside his tomb? And what can the broken pieces tell us about a king whose wife and son became far more famous?

So who was he? Thutmose II was the fourth pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty. His name is sometimes spelled Thutmosis or Tuthmosis. Experts disagree about how long he ruled. Some say about thirteen years. Others say only a few.

His father was Thutmose I, a strong warrior king. His mother was a lesser wife named Mutnofret. Thutmose II received a huge and rich kingdom from his father. But his rule is usually seen as modest next to the kings before and after him.

He put down a revolt in Nubia. He may also have sent armies toward Syria. Generals led most of these campaigns. Still, the records suggest he was not a great fighter like his father and his son. He was a builder, though. He began a stone gateway at the great temple of Karnak, and Thutmose III finished it.

His family life was tied closely to the question of who would rule next. He married Hatshepsut, who was his half-sister. She was the daughter of Thutmose I and his Great Royal Wife, Ahmose. Such marriages were common in the Egyptian royal family. They were meant to keep power, and the royal bloodline, inside the family.

Thutmose II and Hatshepsut had a daughter, Neferure. But he had a son with a lesser wife named Iset. That boy was Thutmose III.

Thutmose II died young. Thutmose III was still a small child. At first, Hatshepsut ruled for her stepson as regent. A regent is a person who rules while the real king is too young. Later she took the full title of pharaoh for herself.

A mummy thought to be Thutmose II was found in 1881. It lay in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahari, which experts call cache DB320. The mummy shows signs of poor health, such as skin diseases and a weak body. That may explain his rather short life and his quiet rule.

The story of the discovery is a story of patience and careful work. The Egyptian and British mission had been working across the Western Wadis. This rough area lies west of Luxor. Archaeologists had studied it far less than the nearby Valley of the Kings.

The team was not hunting for the tomb of Thutmose II. They were exploring a different tomb cut into the cliff.

The Western Wadis were known as a burial place for royal women. Queens and princesses of the 18th Dynasty lay there. So the area was interesting. But nobody expected to find a pharaoh.

The great moment came in October 2022. The team found the entrance and the passage of an unknown tomb. They gave it the name Wadi C-4. At first, they thought it belonged to a royal wife.

Floods had filled its main passage with hardened mud and stones. The floods had also weakened the ceilings, and parts had fallen in. That made the digging slow, dangerous, and difficult. Litherland and his team spent almost three years taking out the hard fill. They recorded every piece and every clue.

In 2023, Litherland published a first report in the magazine Egyptian Archaeology. He asked whether the tomb could be that of Thutmose II. The official answer came from the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities in February 2025. It said yes.

The key proof was in the way the tomb was built and decorated. Part of the ceiling had survived as pieces of plaster. They were blue with yellow stars. This design is a mark of royal tombs. Small jars made of alabaster, a soft white stone, also carried the names of Thutmose II and his wife Hatshepsut.

The starry ceiling stood for the night sky. It may have shown the pharaoh's journey through the underworld to join the stars. The Egyptians called the stars that never set below the horizon the "imperishable ones."

The team found more signs. There were traces of a khekher frieze. This is a painted band of simple flower shapes, and it is another mark of a royal tomb.

They also found small bits of plaster with parts of the Amduat. The name means "What is in the Netherworld." The Amduat is a text for the dead. It tells how the sun god Ra travels through the twelve hours of the night. It was a text used in royal tombs. Together with the jars, these signs led the team to say this was the tomb of a pharaoh.

Tomb Wadi C-4 is cut into the limestone cliffs of Wadi Gabbanat el-Qurud. It has two corridors and four rooms. The rooms are roughly rectangular, and they are different sizes.

The tomb is "bent to the left." This means its main line turns to the left as you go in. Early royal tombs of the 18th Dynasty often have this shape. A second corridor was added later. It may have served as an emergency way out after floods blocked the first passage.

The place of the tomb is one of its biggest puzzles. Thutmose III and later pharaohs of the dynasty were buried in the Valley of the Kings. Why was Thutmose II placed in the Western Wadis?

The answer may be that the rules were not yet fixed. In the early New Kingdom, the Valley of the Kings was not yet the settled royal burial ground. The first kings of the 18th Dynasty seem to have tried different places. The floods may also explain why Hatshepsut and Thutmose III later went back to the Valley.

There may be a second reason. The area was linked with royal women. Nearby are the tombs of three queens, known as wives of Thutmose III. So the place may have been chosen to connect the king with the women of his family. Hatshepsut's famous temple at Deir el-Bahari is close by.

What the archaeologists found inside was sad. Floods had ruined the tomb soon after the burial. The hard fill blocked the main passage, and part of the ceiling had come down.

Most of the tomb's contents were gone. Officials said they were taken away in ancient times. Litherland thinks they were never stolen. He thinks they were moved to a second tomb nearby. That tomb has not been dug yet.

Only pieces were left. There were broken bits of pottery and the alabaster jars. There were pieces of painted plaster with scenes from the Amduat. And there were the traces of the blue ceiling with its stars.

Much of the wall painting was destroyed. Yet even as a ruin, the tomb taught experts a great deal. It showed how early 18th Dynasty kings may have been buried and how tomb building grew.

The tomb was empty. The mummy of the pharaoh was not there. Nobody was surprised by that.

A mummy had been found long before, in 1881. It was in the famous Deir el-Bahari Royal Cache, DB320. This was a hidden tomb. Ancient priests had moved the mummies of many pharaohs there to keep them safe from tomb robbers.

The cache held some of the most famous rulers of the New Kingdom. Among them were Ramesses II, Ramesses III, Seti I, Thutmose III, and the mummy named for Thutmose II.

Many of the mummies had lost their rich grave goods. They had been wrapped again. It was a humble end for kings who were once seen as gods on Earth.

But is the mummy really Thutmose II? Experts have argued about it. The coffin and the mummy bear his name. Still, Litherland has said the body looks too old for him. Egyptologists are experts who study ancient Egypt. Dr. Chris Naunton, an Egyptologist, finds this unlikely because of the name.

For now, the mummy rests in the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo. Many experts accept it as Thutmose II. The new discovery may lead them to check again.

Why does the tomb matter so much? There are three main reasons.

First, it helps answer an old puzzle of Egyptian archaeology. Where were the early pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty buried? The Valley of the Kings holds tombs of many rulers of that dynasty. But the burial places of some early kings, such as Thutmose I and Amenhotep I, are still debated.

The new find suggests that early kings may have been buried in different areas. The Valley of the Kings may have become the main royal burial ground only later in the dynasty.

Second, the tomb gives clues about how those early burials were done. It has a simple plan, the Amduat on its walls, and a line that bends to the left. These belong to an early stage of royal tomb design. Until now, experts could only guess at that stage by looking at later and richer tombs.

Third, the tomb lies near the tombs of royal women. That gives new clues about how the burials of pharaohs were linked to the burials of queens and princesses. It matters most when we think of Hatshepsut and her great power in the early 18th Dynasty.

The discovery shows that the sands of Egypt still keep secrets. For about a century, no tomb like this one was found. A patient team found it by searching an area that others had passed over, step by careful step.

The tomb is not whole like Tutankhamun's. It has been emptied and broken by time. But it is royal, and it is real.

Many questions are still open. Why did this king lie so far from the others? Is the mummy in Cairo truly his? And how many more pharaohs are still waiting under the sand?
