Thutmose II Tomb: The Lost Pharaoh Found

A dip in the desert ground

In October 2022, a joint British and Egyptian team was at work in a rocky valley in Egypt. They were exploring a different tomb in the cliff above. Then they found the entrance of another tomb that nobody knew. It lay in the Western Wadis, a wild area west of Luxor. Archaeologists had studied this area far less than the nearby Valley of the Kings. A wadi is a dry valley in the desert.

The leader of the team was Dr. Piers Litherland, an archaeologist linked to the University of Cambridge. Archaeology is the study of the past through the things people left behind. At first, the team thought that the new tomb belonged to a royal wife.

They gave it the name Wadi C-4. They did not yet know whose it was. Let us follow the clues.

Blue ceiling, yellow stars

Floods had filled the main passage with hardened mud and stones. Parts of the ceiling had fallen in. The digging was slow, dangerous, and difficult. Litherland and his team spent almost three years taking out the hard fill. They recorded every piece.

Slowly, small signs came out of the dust. Pieces of painted plaster showed a blue sky with yellow stars. This is a mark of royal tombs. The team also found small jars made of alabaster, a soft white stone. The jars carried the names of Thutmose II and his wife Hatshepsut.

There was more. They found traces of a khekher frieze, a painted band of simple flower shapes. They also found pieces of the Amduat, a text for the dead that was used in royal tombs.

Together, these clues pointed to a king. But which king? The jars gave the answer.

The news from Egypt

In 2023, Litherland wrote a first report in the magazine Egyptian Archaeology. He asked whether the tomb could be the tomb of Thutmose II. In February 2025, the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities gave the official answer. It said yes.

A pharaoh was a king of ancient Egypt. The Ministry called this the first pharaoh’s tomb found since 1922. In that year, Howard Carter found the tomb of Tutankhamun. Some experts say that this is too strong, because other royal tombs have been found since. But the news still surprised people everywhere.

Most pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty were buried in the Valley of the Kings. A dynasty is a line of rulers from one family. This tomb was not there. It lies about 2.45 kilometers away from the Valley.

So who was the king in this lonely tomb?

A short reign and a famous family

Thutmose II was the fourth pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty. His father was Thutmose I, and his mother was Mutnofret. Sources disagree about how long he ruled. Some date his reign from about 1493 to 1479 BCE.

He put down a revolt in Nubia. Generals led most of his campaigns. He also began a stone gateway at the great temple of Karnak, and Thutmose III finished it.

His family life shaped Egypt’s future. He married his half-sister Hatshepsut. They had a daughter named Neferure. With a lesser wife named Iset, he had a son. That boy was Thutmose III.

Thutmose II died young. His son was still a small child.

At first, Hatshepsut ruled for her stepson as regent. A regent is a person who rules while the real king is too young. Later, she took the full title of pharaoh for herself. She became one of the most powerful and famous women ever to rule Egypt.

Thutmose III grew up to be a great general. He led at least 16 military campaigns. Some historians call him the “Napoleon of Egypt.” His empire reached from the Euphrates River to Nubia. So the king in the new tomb stood between two famous people. His wife and his son were far better known than he was.

But another question remains. Why was his tomb so far from the Valley of the Kings?

A tomb in the wrong place?

Wadi C-4 is cut into the cliffs of a place called Wadi Gabbanat el-Qurud. It has two corridors and four rooms. The tomb is “bent to the left.” This means that its main line turns left as you go in. Royal tombs of the early 18th Dynasty often have this shape.

A second corridor was added later. It may have served as an emergency way out when floods blocked the first passage.

So why was a king buried here, and not in the Valley of the Kings? We can say plainly that nobody knows. The tomb is new to science, and the work is not finished.

Now let us look at what was inside.

An empty room and a second tomb

What the team found inside was sad. Floods had ruined the tomb soon after the burial. Most of the contents were gone. Only pieces were left. There were broken pottery, the alabaster jars, and bits of painted plaster.

Where did everything go? Officials said that the contents were taken away in ancient times. Litherland thinks that they were never stolen. He thinks that they were probably moved to a second tomb. That tomb has not been dug yet.

Even as a ruin, the tomb taught experts a great deal. It showed what a royal tomb of that time looked like.

But a tomb needs a king. Was his body found long ago?

Is this his mummy?

A mummy thought to be Thutmose II was found in 1881. It lay in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahari. Experts call that place cache DB320. The mummy shows signs of skin disease and a thin body. That may explain why he died young.

But is the mummy really Thutmose II? Experts disagree. Litherland has said that the body looks too old for him. Dr. Chris Naunton, an Egyptologist, disagrees. An Egyptologist is an expert who studies ancient Egypt.

For now, the mummy rests in the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo. The new tomb may lead experts to check again.

So what do we know now, and what is still open?

A king found, and questions left

We know that a team found a royal tomb in the Western Wadis. We know about the blue ceiling, the jars with royal names, and the royal text. We know that floods ruined the place long ago.

We do not know why the king lay so far from the Valley of the Kings. We do not know where his things went. And we do not know for sure whether the mummy in Cairo is his.

Think again about October 2022. A team looking at one tomb found the door of another. They thought that they had found a royal wife. They had found a pharaoh.

The sands of Egypt still keep secrets. A patient team found this one by searching, step by careful step.
