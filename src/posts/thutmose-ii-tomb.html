Thutmose II Tomb: The Lost Pharaoh Found

Dr. Piers Litherland stepped into a dark room cut deep into an Egyptian cliff. He lifted his light and looked up. Part of the ceiling was still whole. It was painted blue, and it was covered with yellow stars.

He knew at once what that meant. In ancient Egypt, only the tomb of a king had a ceiling like that. Litherland climbed back out into the sun. His wife was waiting outside. He later said: “The only thing I could do was burst into tears.”

In February 2025, the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities told the world the news. The lost tomb of Pharaoh Thutmose II had been found. A pharaoh was a king of ancient Egypt. Thutmose II ruled during the 18th Dynasty, from about 1493 to 1479 BCE. A dynasty is a line of rulers from one family.

The news surprised experts everywhere. Egypt has had many pharaohs, so a pharaoh alone was not the surprise. The surprise was the long wait. This was the first royal Egyptian tomb found since 1922. In that year, Howard Carter found the tomb of Tutankhamun.

That is a gap of more than one hundred years. In that time, people split the atom and walked on the Moon. They mapped the human genome, the full set of instructions inside our cells. They joined the world together with the internet. Yet the grave of a king who ruled about three and a half thousand years ago stayed hidden under the desert.

Most pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty were buried in the famous Valley of the Kings. This tomb was not there. It lay about 2.45 kilometers away, in a wild and rocky area called the Western Wadis. A wadi is a dry valley in the desert. The exact place is known as Wadi Gabbanat el-Qurud.

The team that found it was a joint Egyptian and British mission. Its leader, Dr. Litherland, is an honorary research associate in the archaeology department of the University of Cambridge. Archaeology is the study of the past through the things people left behind. The mission worked with the support of the New Kingdom Research Foundation.

The tomb they found was in a sad state. Ancient floods had wrecked it. Its contents were scattered, and its wall paintings were in small pieces. But the signs were clear. This was the tomb of a king.

The find matters for more than one man’s grave. Thutmose II stood at an important turning point in the early New Kingdom. His wife was Hatshepsut, who took the throne after he died. She became one of the most powerful and famous women ever to rule Egypt as pharaoh.

His son was Thutmose III, a brilliant general. He is known as the “Napoleon of Ancient Egypt.” He led 17 military campaigns and made Egypt’s empire larger than it had ever been.

The tomb also fills a hole in what we know. In the early 18th Dynasty, the custom of burying pharaohs near the city of Thebes was still taking shape. The burial ground there is called the Theban Necropolis. The tomb shows us how kings were buried at that early time.

It raises new questions too. Why was Thutmose II buried so far from the Valley of the Kings? What happened to the things inside his tomb? And what can the broken pieces tell us about a king whose wife and son became far more famous?

So who was he? Thutmose II was the fourth pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty. His name is sometimes spelled Thutmosis or Tuthmosis. He ruled Egypt for about thirteen years.

His father was Thutmose I, a strong warrior king who fought in Nubia and Syria. His mother was a lesser wife named Mutnofret. Thutmose II received a huge and rich kingdom from his father. But his rule is usually seen as modest next to the kings before and after him.

He led at least one military campaign in Nubia. He may also have sent armies into the Levant, the lands on the eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea. Still, the records suggest he was not a great fighter like his father and his son. He was a builder, though. He added to the great temple at Karnak and ordered building work all over Egypt.

His family life was tied closely to the question of who would rule next. He married Hatshepsut, who was his half-sister. She was the daughter of Thutmose I and his Great Royal Wife, Ahmose. Such marriages were common in the Egyptian royal family. They were meant to keep power, and the royal bloodline, inside the family.

Thutmose II and Hatshepsut had a daughter, Neferure. They had no son who lived. But he did have a son with a lesser wife named Isis. That boy was Thutmose III.

Thutmose II died young, probably in his thirties. Thutmose III was still a small child. At first, Hatshepsut ruled for her stepson as regent. A regent is a person who rules while the real king is too young. Later she took the full title of pharaoh for herself.

A mummy thought to be Thutmose II was found in 1881. It lay in a hidden tomb at Deir el-Bahari, which experts call cache DB320. The mummy shows signs of poor health, such as skin diseases and a weak body. That may explain his rather short life and his quiet rule.

The story of the discovery is a story of patience and careful work. The Egyptian and British mission had been working across the Western Wadis. This rough area lies west of Luxor. Archaeologists had studied it far less than the nearby Valley of the Kings.

The team was not hunting for the tomb of Thutmose II. They were clearing ground and looking for foundation deposits. These are gifts that ancient builders buried under tombs and temples as part of a ritual.

The Western Wadis were known as a burial place for royal women. Queens and princesses of the 18th Dynasty lay there. So the area was interesting. But nobody expected to find a pharaoh.

The great moment came on October 31, 2022. Two team members, Ashraf Omar Ali and Heraji Sayed Ahmed, noticed a soft patch in the ground. It was a slight dip, and it hinted that something lay below. The team dug down and found the door of an unknown tomb. They gave it the name Wadi C-4.

The tomb had been sealed since the Third Intermediate Period, about 1069 to 664 BCE. Floods had filled its main passage again and again. The mud and stones had packed together and set as hard as concrete.

The floods had also weakened the ceilings, and parts had fallen in. That made the digging slow, dangerous, and difficult. Litherland and his team spent months taking out the hard fill. They recorded every piece and every clue.

In 2023, a first report came out in Issue 63 of the magazine Egyptian Archaeology. In it, Litherland asked a tempting question: “Has the Tomb of Thutmose II been found?” The official answer came from the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities in February 2025. It said yes.

The key proof was in the way the tomb was built and painted. In the burial chamber, part of the ceiling had survived. It was blue with yellow stars. Only the tombs of pharaohs had this design. No private person could have it, however rich or powerful.

The starry ceiling was a right that belonged to kings alone. It stood for the night sky. It showed the pharaoh’s journey through the underworld to join the stars near the pole of the sky. The Egyptians called these stars the “imperishable ones,” because they never set below the horizon.

The team found more signs. There were traces of a khekher frieze. This is a painted band of simple flower shapes, and it is another mark of a royal tomb.

They also found small bits of plaster with parts of the Amduat. The name means “What is in the Netherworld.” The Amduat is a text for the dead. It tells how the sun god Ra travels through the twelve hours of the night. Only royal tombs could hold it. Together, these signs left no doubt that this was the tomb of a pharaoh.

Tomb Wadi C-4 is cut into the limestone cliffs of Wadi Gabbanat el-Qurud. It has two corridors and four rooms. The rooms are roughly rectangular, and they are different sizes.

The tomb is “bent to the left.” This means its main line turns to the left as you go in. Early royal tombs of the 18th Dynasty often have this shape. The floor of the corridor was covered in plain white plaster. Later royal tombs had rich painted walls, so this was a simple finish.

The whole plan is simpler than the tombs of later 18th Dynasty kings, such as Thutmose III or Amenhotep III. That suggests the custom of building royal tombs in this area was still young.

The place of the tomb is one of its biggest puzzles. His father, Thutmose I, and later pharaohs of the dynasty were buried in the Valley of the Kings. Why was Thutmose II placed in the Western Wadis?

The answer may be that the rules were not yet fixed. In the early New Kingdom, the Valley of the Kings was not yet the settled royal burial ground. The first kings of the 18th Dynasty seem to have tried different places. This tomb may be a middle step between the older ways of the Middle Kingdom and the later custom of the Valley.

There may be a second reason. The area was linked with royal women. Nearby are the tombs of three queens, known as wives of Thutmose III. So the place may have been chosen to connect the king with the women of his family. Above all, it may point to his powerful wife. Hatshepsut’s famous temple at Deir el-Bahari is close by.

What the archaeologists found inside was heartbreaking. Over thousands of years, floods had ruined the tomb. The hard fill blocked the main passage, and part of the ceiling had come down.

Most of the tomb’s contents were gone. They were probably taken out in ancient times, when many royal mummies were moved to the hiding place at Deir el-Bahari.

Only pieces were left. There were broken bits of pottery and parts of burial equipment. There were pieces of painted plaster with scenes from the Amduat. And there were the traces of the blue ceiling with its stars.

Some parts of the tomb were completely destroyed. The pieces that survived were only a tiny share of its old beauty. Yet even as a ruin, the tomb taught experts a great deal. It showed how early 18th Dynasty kings were buried and how tomb building grew. It also showed how ideas about life after death were changing.

The tomb was empty. The mummy of the pharaoh was not there. Nobody was surprised by that.

The mummy had been found long before, in 1881. It was in the famous Deir el-Bahari Royal Cache, DB320. This was a hidden tomb. Ancient priests had moved the mummies of many pharaohs there to keep them safe from tomb robbers.

The cache held some of the most famous rulers of the New Kingdom. Among them were Ramesses II, Ramesses III, Seti I, Thutmose III, and Thutmose II.

The mummies had lost their rich grave goods. They had been wrapped again in plain linen. Their golden coffins had been swapped for plain wooden ones. It was a humble end for kings who were once seen as gods on Earth.

But is the mummy really Thutmose II? Experts have argued about it. The wrappings carried a label with his name. The body shows the poor health that the records describe. Still, some Egyptologists see a problem. Egyptologists are experts who study ancient Egypt. They say some features of the body do not match what they would expect from this royal family.

The tomb may one day give new clues to settle the question. For now, the mummy rests in the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in Cairo. Most experts accept it as Thutmose II. But the new discovery has made them eager to check again.

Why does the tomb matter so much? There are three main reasons.

First, it helps answer an old puzzle of Egyptian archaeology. Where were the early pharaohs of the 18th Dynasty buried? The Valley of the Kings holds tombs of most rulers of that dynasty. But the tombs of several early kings had never been found for certain. These include Thutmose I, Thutmose II, and possibly Amenhotep I.

The new find suggests that the early kings may have been buried in a different area. The Valley of the Kings may have become the main royal burial ground only later in the dynasty.

Second, the tomb gives solid proof of how those early burials were done. It has a simple plan, white plaster floors, the Amduat on its walls, and a line that bends to the left. All of these belong to an early stage of royal tomb design. Until now, experts could only guess at that stage by looking at later and richer tombs.

Third, the tomb lies near the tombs of royal women. That gives new clues about how the burials of pharaohs were linked to the burials of queens and princesses. It matters most when we think of Hatshepsut and her great power in the early 18th Dynasty.

The discovery shows that the sands of Egypt still keep secrets. For more than a century, no new royal tomb was found. For decades, people guessed where the missing pharaohs might lie. Then a patient team found one. It was not luck. They searched an area that others had passed over, step by careful step.

The tomb is not whole like Tutankhamun’s. It has been emptied and broken by time. But it is royal, and it is real.

Dr. Litherland described the moment he understood as one of “extraordinary bewilderment.” It was the strong feeling of meeting something he had sought for years and never truly expected to find.

Many questions are still open. Why did this king lie so far from the others? Is the mummy in Cairo truly his? And how many more pharaohs are still waiting under the sand?
