The London Beer Flood of 1814: A Wave of Destruction

It is the afternoon of Monday, October 17, 1814, in London. Suddenly a wall of dark beer rushes down the street. It is said to be 15 feet high. This is one of the strangest industrial disasters in history.

The beer was a dark ale called porter. It poured through one of the most notorious slums in the city. At least eight people died. Homes were wrecked, and cellars filled with thousands of gallons of fermenting brew.

It began at Meux & Company’s Horse Shoe Brewery. The brewery stood where Tottenham Court Road meets Great Russell Street. Inside was a huge wooden fermentation vat, 22 feet tall. A vat is a big tank, and fermentation is the process that turns the brew into beer. This one held the equivalent of more than 3,500 barrels of porter.

One of the vat’s iron hoops snapped. The vat burst. The beer knocked out the valves of other vats and broke open the vats around it. In a few minutes, 323,000 imperial gallons of beer escaped. That is about 1.47 million liters.

The black flood smashed through the back wall of the brewery. It rushed into the St. Giles rookery. A rookery was a crowded, very poor part of town, full of tenements, narrow alleys, and packed cellars.

Two houses collapsed. A pub wall fell and trapped a teenage servant girl. An Irish family was holding a wake for a two-year-old child. A wake is a gathering to remember someone who has died. The flood swept the family away.

London in 1814 was changing fast because of the Industrial Revolution. The city was booming, crowded, and very unequal. More than one million people lived there. It was the largest city in Europe and one of the largest in the world.

The streets around the brewery were called the St. Giles rookery. Conditions there were among the worst in the city. It was a maze of narrow courts, dark alleys, and crumbling tenements. Its people were some of the poorest in London. They included Irish immigrants, farm workers who had lost their land, and the destitute.

Whole families crammed into single rooms. Many lived in basements. These cellars were damp, had little fresh air, and could flood very easily.

The artist William Hogarth had made St. Giles famous in his 1751 print Gin Lane. The picture shows the horrors of addiction to cheap gin. Beer, by comparison, was seen as the healthy drink. It was safer than the city’s often dirty water.

Men, women, and children drank it in huge amounts. The average Londoner is said to have drunk several pints a day. Porter was the most popular kind. It is a dark, rich ale made with brown malt.

Brewing had become an industry. Brewers built bigger and bigger vats to meet the demand. The vats at Meux’s brewery were among the largest ever made.

The Horse Shoe Brewery had stood at the corner of Great Russell Street and Tottenham Court Road for decades. Sir Henry Meux bought it in 1809. He came from a rich brewing family. His father, Sir Richard Meux, had been one of London’s best-known brewers.

Sir Henry set out to make the brewery as big as the largest in London. At its center were enormous wooden vats. The biggest was 22 feet high and 60 feet around.

The vats were made of huge oak planks called staves. Heavy iron hoops held the staves together. Each vat could hold about 135,000 imperial gallons of porter. One vat held enough beer to give a pint to more than one million people.

The brewery was proud of these giants, and people were curious about them. Before the flood the vats were a kind of tourist attraction. Visitors could go inside the empty vats. The brewery sometimes held dinners inside the huge casks. Dozens of guests sat at long tables within the wooden walls. It was a show of industrial pride, and it would soon prove too early.

The vats had caused trouble before. In 1810, a few years earlier, one of the big vats at the Horse Shoe Brewery had burst. This was a warning, but it seems nobody took it seriously enough.

Inside each vat the pressure was very strong. The fermenting beer gave off carbon dioxide gas. The wooden staves swelled and shrank as the weather and the damp changed. The iron hoops suffered too. Metal can tire, rust, and crack under strain.

On the afternoon of October 17, 1814, one of the iron hoops on the largest vat snapped. A worker saw the broken hoop and told a supervisor. But about an hour later, before anyone acted, the whole vat burst.

The sudden release of 135,000 gallons of porter made an explosive blast of pressure. It knocked out the valve of the vat next to it. That vat burst too and let out its beer. The chain went on as the beer hit more vessels and barrels.

In the end, between 128,000 and 323,000 imperial gallons escaped. That is enough to fill more than five Olympic swimming pools. The force brought down the back wall of the brewery. The black, foaming flood poured into the streets of St. Giles.

It was not a slow leak. It was a violent burst. The pressure wave was strong enough to blow out the whole back wall of the building.

Witnesses heard a deafening boom that echoed through the narrow streets. A moment later, a wall of dark, foaming porter rushed through the lanes of St. Giles. It was like a black tidal wave. In places it was said to be 15 feet high. It moved with frightening speed.

The lay of the land made things worse. The slum lay lower than the brewery, which stood on slightly higher ground. The beer ran downhill into a maze of narrow courts and dead-end alleys. Many were no wider than six or seven feet.

These narrow passages squeezed the flood and made it stronger. Side streets turned into raging rivers of beer, foam, and rubble. The cellars were the cheapest and most desperate homes in the area. They filled fast.

Whole families lived in rooms below ground. Thousands of gallons of porter poured through broken windows and fallen walls. In minutes it rose to the ceilings.

The damage was swift and cruel. Two houses on New Street were completely knocked down by the wave. The front wall of a third building, the Tavistock Arms pub on Great Russell Street, fell inward. A teenage servant girl named Eleanor Cooper was working in the pub’s cellar. She was only 14 years old, and she died.

One of the saddest stories is about an Irish woman named Mary Mulvey. She was holding a wake for her two-year-old son in a basement home when the flood hit. Mary and another of her children were swept away and died.

Hannah Banfield was a young mother at home with her baby daughter Sarah. She died when the flood tore through their home. Ann Saville died in one of the wrecked houses on New Street. Elizabeth Smith was caught in the flood as it surged through the basements. William Reymond is another victim named in the records of the time.

In total, eight people lost their lives. Some historians think the true number may have been a little higher. Many of the poorest people lived in cellars that nobody had recorded. Those cellars might never have been searched fully.

Many of the victims were Irish immigrants. They had come to London looking for work in the troubled years of the Napoleonic Wars. They were the invisible poor of Georgian London. In any other case, their deaths might have gone unnoticed. It was the strange cause, drowning in beer, that brought their deaths to the public’s attention.

The first hours were chaotic. Survivors told of wading through beer up to the waist. It was mixed with broken timber, shattered glass, and the wreckage of fallen buildings. The rich, malty, yeasty smell of porter hung in the air for blocks.

The rescue was disorganized. Neighbors and bystanders did most of it. There were no emergency services in London in 1814. People dug through rubble with their bare hands, looking for survivors in the fallen buildings and flooded cellars.

The St. Giles workhouse opened its doors. A workhouse was a place where poor people could live and work. It gave shelter for a time to survivors who had lost everything.

There was also a grim irony. Some local people were seen scooping up the spilled beer. They used pots, pans, buckets, and even their cupped hands. Many of them could rarely afford to buy beer in any quantity, so the flood was a strange windfall. Some are said to have drunk until they were stupefied while their neighbors were still being found in the wreckage.

It took days to find everyone who had died. The flood had spread rubble and victims over a wide area. The tangled ruins of the wrecked houses had to be taken apart with care.

A coroner’s inquest was held in the days that followed. A coroner is an official who investigates sudden deaths, and an inquest is the hearing he holds. It took place at the Workhouse of the Parish of St. Giles-in-the-Fields.

The inquest heard from brewery workers, neighbors, and witnesses. It asked whether this was an unavoidable accident or the result of carelessness by the brewery’s managers. There was evidence that a worker had noticed the broken hoop about an hour before the vat burst. He had reported it to a supervisor.

Even so, the inquest ruled that the deaths were “by casualty.” It found that the flood was an Act of God. That means an accident nobody could have foreseen, so nobody could be blamed in a criminal court.

This was not unusual for industrial disasters in the early nineteenth century. The idea that a company could be blamed for carelessness was still very new. The law favored owners and employers over the poor.

The brewery’s owners also faced lawsuits from survivors and from the families of victims. These cases were reportedly settled for fairly small sums. The total damage was thought to be about £23,000. That was a large sum then, equal to several million pounds today. But most of it was for damaged property, not for lives lost.

The era’s priorities showed in another way. Parliament debated the excise duty owed on the lost beer. Excise duty is a tax on goods like beer. Parliament decided to waive the tax. This saved the brewery from ruin and let Meux & Company go on working.

The Horse Shoe Brewery was rebuilt and ran for decades after the flood. Sir Henry Meux even expanded the business. The Meux family stayed one of London’s best-known brewing families through the nineteenth century.

The brewery was torn down in 1922. The Dominion Theatre on Tottenham Court Road now stands on the site. Today there is no memorial to the eight victims at the spot. This shows how invisible the poor of St. Giles were. Their lives and deaths were rarely thought worth remembering.

The flood did help to change attitudes toward safety in industry, though slowly. No single law came directly from the disaster. But people grew more aware that huge industrial vessels could endanger the people living around them. That awareness shaped later reforms in factory and workplace safety in the Victorian era.

Brewers kept building large vats. But the rules for materials, inspection, and repair grew stricter. The flood also became part of popular culture as one of London’s strangest disasters. Many books, documentaries, and articles tell the story. It often appears in lists of history’s oddest accidents.

In 2014, on the 200th anniversary of the flood, a local pub called the Holborn Whippet brewed a special beer. It was made in honor of the victims.

The story still fascinates people, and it has many themes. There is the oddness of drowning in beer. There is the weakness of the poor, and the pride of an age of industry. There is also the thin line between ordinary life and sudden disaster in the world’s first industrial city. Could anyone have stopped it?
