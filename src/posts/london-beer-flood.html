The London Beer Flood of 1814: A Wave of Destruction

It is the afternoon of Monday, October 17, 1814, in London. Suddenly a wall of dark beer rushes down the street. It is said to be 15 feet high. This is one of the strangest industrial disasters in history.

The beer was a dark ale called porter. It poured through one of the most notorious slums in the city. Eight people died. Homes were wrecked, and cellars filled with thousands of gallons of fermenting brew.

It began at Meux & Company’s Horse Shoe Brewery. The brewery stood where Tottenham Court Road meets Oxford Street. Inside was a huge wooden fermentation vat, 22 feet tall. A vat is a big tank, and fermentation is the process that turns the brew into beer. On that day it held more than 3,500 barrels of porter.

One of the vat’s iron hoops slipped off. The vat burst. The beer knocked out the valve of another vat, and other vessels burst too. Sources disagree on how much beer escaped. The estimates run from 128,000 to 323,000 imperial gallons. The top figure is about 1.47 million liters.

The black flood smashed through the back wall of the brewery. It rushed into the St. Giles rookery. A rookery was a crowded, very poor part of town, full of tenements, narrow alleys, and packed cellars.

Two houses collapsed. A pub wall fell and trapped a teenage servant girl. An Irish family was holding a wake for a small boy. A wake is a gathering to remember someone who has died. The flood swept the mourners away.

London in 1814 was changing fast because of the Industrial Revolution. The city was booming, crowded, and very unequal. More than one million people lived there. It was the largest city in Europe and one of the largest in the world.

The streets around the brewery were called the St. Giles rookery. Conditions there were among the worst in the city. It was a maze of narrow courts, dark alleys, and crumbling tenements. Its people were some of the poorest in London. They included Irish immigrants and many other poor families.

Whole families crammed into single rooms. Many lived in basements. These cellars were damp, had little fresh air, and could flood very easily.

The artist William Hogarth had made this area famous in his 1751 print Gin Lane. The picture shows the horrors of addiction to cheap gin. Beer, by comparison, was seen as the healthy drink. It was safer than the city’s often dirty water.

Men, women, and children drank it in huge amounts. Porter was the most popular kind. It is a dark, rich ale made with brown malt.

Brewing had become an industry. Brewers built bigger and bigger vats to meet the demand. The vats at Meux’s brewery were among the largest ever made.

The Horse Shoe Brewery had stood at the corner of Tottenham Court Road and Oxford Street for decades. Sir Henry Meux bought it in 1809. He came from a rich brewing family. His father, Sir Richard Meux, had been one of London’s best-known brewers.

Sir Henry set out to make the brewery as big as the largest in London. At its center were enormous wooden vats. The biggest was 22 feet high.

The vats were made of huge oak planks called staves. Heavy iron hoops held the staves together. One account says the biggest vat could hold about 135,000 imperial gallons of porter. That is enough to give a pint to more than one million people.

The brewery was proud of these giants, and people were curious about them. Visitors paid to come and see the vats. It was a show of industrial pride, and it would soon prove too early.

Inside each vat the pressure was very strong. The fermenting beer gave off carbon dioxide gas. Metal hoops can tire and crack under strain.

On the afternoon of October 17, 1814, one of the iron hoops on the largest vat slipped. A clerk named George Crick saw it and told a supervisor. The supervisor said no harm would come of it. About an hour later, the whole vat burst.

The sudden release of so much porter made an explosive blast of pressure. It knocked out the valve of the vat next to it. That vat burst too and let out its beer. The beer then hit more vessels and barrels.

The estimates run from 128,000 to 323,000 imperial gallons. Even the lower figure is a huge amount. The top figure would fill about half of an Olympic swimming pool. The force brought down the back wall of the brewery. The black, foaming flood poured into the streets of St. Giles.

It was not a slow leak. It was a violent burst. The pressure wave was strong enough to blow out the whole back wall of the building.

Reports say people heard a loud crash. A moment later, a wall of dark, foaming porter rushed through the lanes of St. Giles. It was like a black tidal wave. In places it is said to have been 15 feet high.

The slum was made of narrow courts and dead-end alleys. These narrow passages squeezed the flood and made it stronger. Side streets turned into rivers of beer, foam, and rubble. The cellars were the cheapest and most desperate homes in the area. They filled fast.

Whole families lived in rooms below ground. Thousands of gallons of porter poured through broken windows and fallen walls. In minutes it rose to the ceilings.

The damage was swift and cruel. Two houses on New Street were knocked down by the wave. The Tavistock Arms pub stood on Great Russell Street. A teenage servant girl named Eleanor Cooper was washing pots there. She was only 14 years old. A wall fell on her, and she died.

Two young children died in the houses on New Street. They were Hannah Bamfield, who was four years old, and Sarah Bates, who was three.

Several mourners died at the wake in another house. Records name Mary Mulvey, her young son Thomas Murry, Ann Saville, Elizabeth Smith, and Catherine Butler. The records give their ages as anything from three to sixty-five.

In total, eight people lost their lives. Different sources spell some of the names in different ways.

The first hours were chaotic. Survivors told of wading through beer. It was mixed with broken timber, shattered glass, and the wreckage of fallen buildings. The smell of porter hung over the area for weeks.

The rescue was disorganized. Neighbors and bystanders did most of it. There were no emergency services in London in 1814. People dug through rubble with their bare hands, looking for survivors in the fallen buildings and flooded cellars.

Later stories tell of a grim irony. They say that local people scooped up the spilled beer in pots, pans, and buckets. Some tales say a man even died from drinking too much of it. But one historian points out that newspapers of the time did not mention any of this. So these stories may be legend.

A coroner’s inquest was held in the days that followed. A coroner is an official who investigates sudden deaths, and an inquest is the hearing he holds.

The inquest heard from brewery workers, neighbors, and witnesses. It asked whether this was an unavoidable accident or the result of carelessness by the brewery’s managers. The clerk had noticed the slipped hoop about an hour before the vat burst. He had reported it to a supervisor.

Even so, the inquest ruled that the eight people died “casually, accidentally and by misfortune.” It found that the flood was an Act of God. That means an accident nobody could have foreseen, so nobody could be blamed. As a result, no one was held responsible and the company paid nothing to the families.

The disaster cost the brewery about £23,000. That is roughly 1.25 million pounds in today’s money, by one estimate. Meux & Company asked Parliament for help. It got back about £7,250 of the excise duty it had paid on the lost beer. Excise duty is a tax on goods like beer. This helped save the brewery from ruin.

The Horse Shoe Brewery went back into business soon afterward. It closed in 1921, when Meux moved its brewing to another site. It was torn down the following year. The Dominion Theatre on Tottenham Court Road now stands on the site. One source says there is no plaque or memorial to the victims there.

The flood is still remembered as one of London’s strangest disasters. No single law came directly from it. Many books, documentaries, and articles tell the story. It often appears in lists of history’s oddest accidents.

The story still fascinates people, and it has many themes. There is the oddness of drowning in beer. There is the weakness of the poor, and the pride of an age of industry. There is also the thin line between ordinary life and sudden disaster in the world’s first industrial city. Could anyone have stopped it?
