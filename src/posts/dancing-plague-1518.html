The Dancing Plague of 1518: When Hundreds Danced Themselves to Death

It was the summer of 1518 in Strasbourg. This was a busy trading city in the Holy Roman Empire. In mid-July, a woman known as Frau Troffea stepped out of her timber-framed house onto a narrow cobbled street. And she began to dance.

There was no music. She had no partner. She had no reason that anyone could see. She just danced, with her arms flung about and her feet pounding the stones. She could not control it.

She danced for six days without stopping. By the end of the first week, thirty-four other people had joined her. By August, about 400 people were jerking and spinning through the streets. It was like a strange carnival that nobody had chosen to join.

Some danced until their feet bled. Some danced until they dropped from tiredness and never got up. According to old chronicles, as many as fifteen people a day were dying at the worst point. The strain of nonstop movement was too much for their hearts.

The city leaders, doctors, and priests could not explain it. Their attempts to cure the dancers only made things worse.

The Dancing Plague of 1518 is one of the strangest and most frightening events in the history of medicine. It has never been fully explained. It makes us ask how the mind, the body, and society are linked.

It was not the only case. Between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries, dozens of similar outbreaks of dancing swept through Europe. They hit Germany, France, the Netherlands, and Switzerland. They are known as “dancing mania” or “St. Vitus’s Dance.”

They all followed a pattern. Normal people suddenly began to dance without control, often for days or weeks. They seemed unable to stop. Some screamed, begged for help, or wept as their bodies kept moving. Many collapsed and died.

The 1518 outbreak in Strasbourg is the best recorded. We have detailed city records, doctors’ notes, and the writings of local historians. It is also the deadliest. And it is the most disturbing, because the way the city reacted shows a view of the world very different from ours. To understand it, we must know what people feared and believed at the time.

The city records say the outbreak began on or around July 14, 1518. That was when Frau Troffea began dancing in the street outside her home. She danced alone for about six or seven days. Neighbors, passersby, and finally the city authorities noticed.

She was not performing or celebrating or exercising. She seemed to be in a trance, unable to stop her own movement. Within a week, thirty-four people had joined her. It was not their choice. They too were seized by the same urge to dance. By early August, there were hundreds. Most of the dancers were poor women, but men and people of higher rank were also affected.

The sick danced day and night. Often they danced outdoors. Sometimes they danced in the guildhalls and marketplaces that the city opened for them. They did not answer shouts, touches, or begging from their families. Some tore their clothes. Some foamed at the mouth. Some collapsed and were carried away, only to start dancing again when they woke.

Accounts from the time describe the scene as chaotic and deeply upsetting. A city had been taken over by a strange, uncontrolled shaking that no one could explain or stop. As the weeks passed, the number of deaths grew. Dancers died of heart attacks, strokes, and complete exhaustion.

The exact number of deaths is debated. Some sources say as many as fifteen per day at the peak. Modern historians such as John Waller argue that the total was probably in the dozens, not the hundreds. But people did die. They danced until their bodies gave out, and they could not resist.

One of the earliest dancing outbreaks happened in Erfurt, Germany, in 1237. A group of children danced wildly through the streets. Some historians link this to the later legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin.

In 1374, a huge outbreak hit the cities along the Rhine River. It affected thousands of people in dozens of towns. The dancers writhed, screamed, and said they saw visions. Some walked on their hands. Some tore at their clothes. Priests and doctors were baffled. More outbreaks were recorded in 1463, 1510, and 1531, among other dates.

All of these followed the same pattern. They began suddenly, with a need to dance and a trance-like state. A few people died. After weeks or months, they faded away. They kept happening from the 14th to the 17th centuries. So whatever caused them was probably deep in the social and mental life of medieval Europe. People feared supernatural forces. They had lived through terrible plagues. And they faced the constant threat of hunger.

How the Strasbourg authorities reacted is one of the most astonishing and tragic chapters in the history of medicine. Hundreds of people were dancing themselves to death. The city council asked doctors, priests, and learned experts.

They agreed on an answer based on the medical ideas of the time. The dancers had “hot blood.” This meant that their humors, the four fluids doctors thought controlled the body, were out of balance. The imbalance was making their bodies overheat and their limbs move on their own. The cure they chose was more dancing. The idea was that the sick needed to dance the illness out of their bodies, until the extra heat and bad humors were gone.

To help with this “cure,” the city took several unusual steps. It built a wooden stage in the heart of the city, so the dancers had a place to move. It hired professional musicians to play for them. The idea was that music would guide and calm their movements. The city also gave extra food and shelter to keep the dancers alive, though many refused to eat or could not.

The result was bad, as one might have feared. The music and the stage did not cure the dancers. They made the outbreak stronger. The music gave a beat that drew in new dancers. The stage became a focus that gathered the sick together and encouraged onlookers to join in.

By backing the dancing in public and paying for it, the city turned a troubling local event into a full public health disaster.

The outbreak began to fade only weeks later. The dancers were taken away from the public eye to religious shrines, where they prayed and did penance. The city then banned dancing and music in public places. The outbreak slowly died down, though single cases were reported for months afterward.

The famous Renaissance doctor and alchemist Paracelsus (Theophrastus von Hohenheim) visited Strasbourg several years after 1518. He wrote his own account. He did not accept the supernatural explanations the priests liked. Instead he thought the dancing came from mental and social causes.

He sorted the dancers into three groups. Some danced out of anger. Some danced out of frustrated desires. Some danced out of religious ecstasy, a state of overwhelming joy. His groups seem old-fashioned today. But he was right to see that the dancing was mainly a problem of the mind, not a physical disease.

He argued that the dancers were not possessed by demons or cursed by saints. They were people whose minds had cracked under unbearable stress. His ideas came nearly five centuries before the modern theory of mass psychogenic illness. That is a sickness that starts in the mind and spreads through a group. But he lacked the words and the medical tools to explain it fully.

To understand the Dancing Plague, we must understand the world around it. Alsace in 1518 was under great strain. The years before had brought famine, disease, and terrible unrest. A severe drought had ruined the crops. Syphilis, brought to Europe from the New World, was ravaging the people. Leprosy and the bubonic plague were always a threat.

Peasants were crushed by heavy taxes from the church and the nobles. The religious upheavals of the early Reformation were breaking up the old spiritual certainties that had held medieval society together.

For the poor, the people most hit by the dancing plague, life was a daily struggle with hunger, disease, unfair treatment, and fear. Modern researchers argue that this pressure made the perfect setting for mass psychogenic illness. The dancing was not random. It was a bodily sign of shared despair, the way a body reacts when a mind can no longer cope.

What caused the Dancing Plague of 1518? People have debated it for centuries. No single theory has won everyone over.

The most widely accepted idea is mass psychogenic illness, also called conversion disorder. It is championed by the historian John Waller of Michigan State University. He argues that the plague was a form of mass hysteria. In this, great stress passes between people through suggestion and produces real physical symptoms. The dancers were not faking. Their bodies really moved without their control.

This idea explains why the outbreak hit the most stressed and powerless people. It explains why it spread by contact. And it explains why it reacted to changes around it, such as taking the dancers out of public view.

A second idea is ergot poisoning. Ergot is a fungus (Claviceps purpurea) that grows on rye grain, especially in wet weather. It contains chemicals related to LSD, a strong drug. Ergot poisoning can cause convulsions, hallucinations, jerking movements, and mental breakdowns. The theory, first suggested in the twentieth century, says the dancers had eaten bread made from rye with ergot in it.

But critics point out that ergot poisoning usually causes more symptoms than just dancing. And the outbreak spread from person to person in a particular social setting. That fits better with a mental cause than with food poisoning.

Some medical historians suggest the dancers had a kind of viral encephalitis. This is swelling of the brain that can cause involuntary movements, confusion, and changed awareness. This idea explains the nerve symptoms. But it struggles to explain the very specific form of the outbreak, which was shaped by culture.

Many scholars now think it was a perfect storm of body and mind pressures. Ergot or a mild brain infection may have caused the first cases. Then mass psychogenic illness drove the spread and growth of the outbreak. The culture of the time gave the hysteria its form. People believed in the curse of St. Vitus and in demon possession.

The most thorough modern study is by John Waller, a historian of medicine at Michigan State University. His 2008 book A Time to Dance, A Time to Die is the standard English-language book on the event. Waller says the key is the power of belief.

In Strasbourg in the early sixteenth century, many people believed in a folk tale about St. Vitus, a Sicilian martyr. They believed he could curse sinners with a need to dance without control. The people lived in fear of this curse.

When Frau Troffea began to dance, Waller argues, she was not having a random brain event. She was showing, through her body, a deep fear her culture shared. Others who shared the fear, and who were already under great stress, could react in the same way. The outbreak spread not by a germ but by social suggestion. This is the same way modern outbreaks of mass psychogenic illness spread in schools, factories, and army barracks around the world.

The Dancing Plague is not the only case of mass psychogenic illness. It is just one of the most dramatic. One modern example is the Tanganyika Laughter Epidemic of 1962. Uncontrollable laughing began at a girls’ school in Tanzania and spread to hundreds of people. It forced fourteen schools to close.

In 2011, more than a dozen teenage girls in Le Roy, New York, suddenly developed tics. These are quick movements or sounds that cannot be controlled. The tics spread through contact and drew intense media attention. They were finally diagnosed as conversion disorder.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, outbreaks of mass psychogenic illness were reported among factory workers and schoolchildren on several continents. In each case, the pattern is the same. There is great stress, spread among people, and real physical symptoms caused by mental distress. The forces behind the Dancing Plague of 1518 are still with us. They are part of being human.

The Dancing Plague is a story about the great power of the human mind, especially the shared mind of a group under strain. A community was so stressed, so afraid, and so trapped that its people’s bodies began to show what their minds could not say.

Frau Troffea and the hundreds who followed her were not mad, not poisoned, and not cursed by a saint. They were people, mostly poor, mostly female, mostly powerless. Their mental suffering grew so heavy that it burst out in the most dramatic way possible. It came through their bodies, in the streets, in front of everyone.

The city leaders made things worse because they acted on beliefs we now know were false. But the same pattern is still at work today. Mental distress turns into physical symptoms. The symptoms spread by contact. And well-meant but wrong help makes the crisis grow.

The Dancing Plague shows that the line between mind and body is not as firm as we like to think. People are very open to the power of suggestion. And the most dangerous epidemics are not always caused by viruses or bacteria. Sometimes the plague comes from within, a dance of despair that no medicine can stop. Was that what happened in Strasbourg? No one can say for certain.
