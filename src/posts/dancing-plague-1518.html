The Dancing Plague of 1518: When Hundreds Danced Themselves to Death

A woman in the street

It was the summer of 1518 in Strasbourg, a busy trading city in the Holy Roman Empire. Around July 14, a woman known as Frau Troffea stepped onto a cobbled street. And she began to dance.

There was no music. She had no partner. Nobody could see any reason for it. She just danced, and chroniclers say that she could not stop.

She danced for days. Some accounts say four days, and others say six. According to the historian John Waller, thirty-four other people had joined her within a week. By late August, one chronicler said that about 400 people had been affected. Modern writers give a range of 50 to 400.

Some danced until they dropped from tiredness. Waller says that the chronicles agree that dozens died. A later account says that as many as fifteen people a day died at the worst point. But some writers from the time do not mention deaths at all. The true number is not known.

What was happening to these people? To see why this story matters, we must look at what came before.

Not the first dance

Strasbourg was not alone. Between the 14th and 17th centuries, dozens of outbreaks of dancing swept through Europe. They hit Germany, France, the Netherlands, and Switzerland. People called them “dancing mania” or “St. Vitus’s Dance.”

One of the earliest happened in Erfurt, Germany, in 1237. A group of children is said to have danced all the way to the town of Arnstadt. Some link this to the legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin. In 1374, a big outbreak began in Aachen. It spread to cities such as Cologne and Strasbourg. The dancers screamed, cried, and said that they saw visions.

They all followed one pattern. Normal people suddenly began to dance, and they seemed unable to stop. After weeks or months, the dancing faded away. Experts do not agree on the cause.

The 1518 outbreak is one of the best recorded. We have city records and the writings of local chroniclers. They also tell us what the city did about it.

A cure that made things worse

The city council asked doctors for advice. The doctors said that the dancers had “hot blood.” This meant that their humors were out of balance. Humors were the four fluids that doctors then thought controlled the body. The extra heat made the limbs move on their own.

The cure they chose was more dancing. The sick would dance the illness out of their bodies. So the city cleared two guildhalls and a grain market. It built a wooden stage. It paid musicians and professional dancers to keep the dancers going.

It did not work. Waller argues that the music and the stage made the outbreak worse, because public dancing drew in more people.

So the council changed its mind. It banned public dancing. The dancers were sent to a shrine of St. Vitus. They wore red shoes, and holy water was sprinkled on them. The outbreak ended around September.

Why did it start at all? Part of the answer lies in the world around the dancers.

A hard time to be poor

Alsace in 1518 was under great strain. Writers list crop failures, famine, floods, plague, and syphilis. They also list peasant revolts and religious upheaval. Many of the dancers were poor. For them, life was a daily struggle with hunger, disease, and fear.

The famous doctor Paracelsus bought citizenship in Strasbourg in 1526, a few years after the outbreak. He did not accept explanations about spirits. He wrote that one kind of dancing came from imagination, “a mere opinion and idea,” in people who believed in it.

That idea is close to a modern theory. But first, let us look at a different idea about the bread.

Was it the bread?

Ergot is a fungus that grows on rye grain, especially in wet weather. It contains chemicals related to LSD, a strong drug. Ergot poisoning can cause convulsions and hallucinations. So one theory says that the dancers had eaten bread made from rye with ergot in it.

But Waller calls this idea “absolutely untenable.” He says that you simply cannot dance if you suffer from ergot poisoning. Ergot cuts the blood flow to the arms and legs. That would make coordinated movement very hard.

So the weakest idea is the one about the bread. Is there a better answer?

A fear that spread

The historian Waller favors mass psychogenic illness. This is a sickness that starts in the mind and spreads through a group. In it, great stress passes between people through suggestion and produces real physical symptoms. The dancers were not faking. Their bodies really moved without their control.

In Strasbourg in the early sixteenth century, many people believed in St. Vitus. They believed that he could seize a person’s mind and make that person dance. They lived in fear of this curse. When Frau Troffea began to dance, Waller argues, she may have shown a deep fear that her culture shared. Others who shared that fear, and who were under great stress, could react in the same way. The outbreak spread through social suggestion, not through a germ.

Supporters say that this explains why the outbreak hit stressed people and why it spread by contact. Waller wrote the book A Time to Dance, A Time to Die about it. Not every expert agrees, and no single theory has won everyone over.

Laughter and tics

The Dancing Plague is not the only case of this kind. In 1962, uncontrollable laughing began at a girls’ school in Tanzania. It spread to about a thousand people, and fourteen schools were shut down.

In 2011, more than a dozen students in Le Roy, New York, mostly girls, suddenly developed tics. Tics are quick movements or sounds that cannot be controlled. A neurologist diagnosed conversion disorder, though some of the girls and their parents disagreed. In such cases, there is great stress, spread among people, and real physical symptoms. Some experts think that the same forces were at work in Strasbourg.

The street in Strasbourg

So what do we know? A woman began to dance in a street in July 1518, and many others joined her. The city leaders tried to help, but they acted on beliefs that most experts now reject. The dancing ended around September.

What is open? Nobody knows how many people danced or how many died. The cause is still argued. If Waller is right, a community under great strain showed through its bodies what its minds could not say. And the line between mind and body is not as firm as we like to think.

Frau Troffea and the others were probably not poisoned. They were people, many of them poor, who danced in the streets in front of everyone. Was it fear, as Waller says? No one can say for certain.
