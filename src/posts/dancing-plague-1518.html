The Dancing Plague of 1518: When Hundreds Danced Themselves to Death

It was the summer of 1518 in Strasbourg. This was a busy trading city in the Holy Roman Empire. In mid-July, a woman known as Frau Troffea stepped onto a cobbled street. And she began to dance.

There was no music. She had no partner. She had no reason that anyone could see. She just danced. Chroniclers say she could not stop.

She danced for days. Some accounts say four days, and others say six. According to the historian John Waller, thirty-four other people had joined her within a week. By late August, one chronicler said that about 400 people had been affected. Modern writers give a range of 50 to 400.

Some danced until their feet bled. Some danced until they dropped from tiredness. A later account says that as many as fifteen people a day died at the worst point. The true number of deaths is not known.

The city leaders, doctors, and priests could not explain it. Their attempts to cure the dancers only made things worse.

The Dancing Plague of 1518 is one of the strangest events in the history of medicine. It has never been fully explained. It makes us ask how the mind, the body, and society are linked.

It was not the only case. Between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries, dozens of similar outbreaks of dancing swept through Europe. They hit Germany, France, the Netherlands, and Switzerland. They are known as “dancing mania” or “St. Vitus’s Dance.”

They all followed a pattern. Normal people suddenly began to dance without control, often for days or weeks. They seemed unable to stop. Some screamed, laughed, or cried as their bodies kept moving. Some are said to have died.

The 1518 outbreak in Strasbourg is one of the best recorded. We have city records and the writings of local chroniclers. The way the city reacted shows a view of the world very different from ours. To understand it, we must know what people feared and believed at the time.

The city records say the outbreak began on or around July 14, 1518. That was when Frau Troffea began dancing in the street outside her home. She danced alone for about six or seven days. Neighbors, passersby, and finally the city authorities noticed.

She was not performing or celebrating or exercising. She seemed to be in a trance, unable to stop her own movement. Within a week, thirty-four people had joined her. It was not their choice. They too were seized by the same urge to dance. By August, there were dozens or hundreds. Historians say many of the dancers were poor.

The sick danced day and night. Often they danced outdoors. Sometimes they danced in the guildhalls and the grain market that the city opened for them.

Waller says the chronicles agree that dozens died of exhaustion. Later writers say dancers died of heart attacks, strokes, and tiredness.

The exact number of deaths is debated. Some sources say as many as fifteen per day at the peak. But some sources from the time do not mention deaths at all. The total was probably in the dozens, not the hundreds.

One of the earliest dancing outbreaks happened in Erfurt, Germany, in 1237. A group of children is said to have danced all the way to the town of Arnstadt. Some link this to the legend of the Pied Piper of Hamelin.

In 1374, a big outbreak began in Aachen. It spread to cities such as Cologne and Strasbourg. The dancers screamed, cried, and said they saw visions.

All of these followed the same pattern. They began suddenly, with a need to dance and a trance-like state. After weeks or months, they faded away. They kept happening from the 14th to the 17th centuries. Experts do not agree on the cause. Some think it lay in the social life of the time. People feared supernatural forces, and many faced hunger and disease.

How the Strasbourg authorities reacted is one of the most astonishing and tragic chapters in the history of medicine. Many people were dancing without rest. The city council asked doctors and other experts.

They agreed on an answer based on the medical ideas of the time. The dancers had “hot blood.” This meant that their humors, the four fluids doctors thought controlled the body, were out of balance. The imbalance was making their bodies overheat and their limbs move on their own. The cure they chose was more dancing. The idea was that the sick needed to dance the illness out of their bodies, until the extra heat and bad humors were gone.

To help with this “cure,” the city took several unusual steps. It cleared two guildhalls and a grain market. It also built a wooden stage. It paid musicians and professional dancers to keep them going. The idea was that the dancers would recover their minds only if they kept dancing day and night. The result was bad. The music and the stage did not cure the dancers. Waller argues that they made the outbreak worse, because public dancing drew in more people.

The council then banned public dancing. Dancers were sent to a shrine of St. Vitus. They wore red shoes, and holy water was sprinkled on them. The outbreak ended around September.

The famous Renaissance doctor Paracelsus (Theophrastus von Hohenheim) later wrote about dancing mania. He bought citizenship in Strasbourg in 1526, a few years after the outbreak. He did not accept the supernatural explanations. He wrote that one kind of dancing came from imagination, “a mere opinion and idea,” in people who believed in it.

That idea is close to the modern theory of mass psychogenic illness. That is a sickness that starts in the mind and spreads through a group. But Paracelsus lacked the words and the medical tools to explain it fully.

To understand the Dancing Plague, we must understand the world around it. Alsace in 1518 was under great strain. Writers list crop failures, famine, floods, plague, and syphilis. They also list peasant revolts and religious upheaval.

For the poor, the people most hit by the dancing plague, life was a daily struggle with hunger, disease, unfair treatment, and fear. Modern researchers argue that this pressure made the perfect setting for mass psychogenic illness. Waller suggests the dancing was a bodily sign of shared despair.

What caused the Dancing Plague of 1518? People have debated it for centuries. No single theory has won everyone over.

A popular idea is mass psychogenic illness. It is favored by the historian John Waller. In this, great stress passes between people through suggestion and produces real physical symptoms. The dancers were not faking. Their bodies really moved without their control.

Supporters say this idea explains why the outbreak hit stressed people and why it spread by contact.

A second idea is ergot poisoning. Ergot is a fungus (Claviceps purpurea) that grows on rye grain, especially in wet weather. It contains chemicals related to LSD, a strong drug. Ergot poisoning can cause convulsions and hallucinations. The theory says the dancers had eaten bread made from rye with ergot in it.

But Waller calls this idea “absolutely untenable.” He says you simply cannot dance if you suffer from ergot poisoning. Ergot cuts the blood flow to the arms and legs, so coordinated movement would be very hard.

No single theory has won everyone over. The culture of the time may have given the outbreak its form. Many people believed in the curse of St. Vitus and in demon possession.

The best-known modern study is by John Waller, a historian of medicine. He wrote the book A Time to Dance, A Time to Die. Waller says the key is the power of belief.

In Strasbourg in the early sixteenth century, many people believed in St. Vitus. They believed he could seize people’s minds and make them dance without control. The people lived in fear of this curse.

When Frau Troffea began to dance, Waller argues, she may have been showing, through her body, a deep fear her culture shared. Others who shared the fear, and who were already under great stress, could react in the same way. The outbreak spread not by a germ but by social suggestion. Waller says this is the same way modern outbreaks of mass psychogenic illness spread.

The Dancing Plague is not the only case of mass psychogenic illness. It is just one of the most dramatic. One modern example is the Tanganyika Laughter Epidemic of 1962. Uncontrollable laughing began at a girls’ school in Tanzania. It spread to about a thousand people, and fourteen schools were shut down.

In 2011, more than a dozen students in Le Roy, New York, mostly girls, suddenly developed tics. These are quick movements or sounds that cannot be controlled. The tics drew intense media attention. A neurologist diagnosed conversion disorder, though some of the girls and their parents disagreed.

In such cases, there is great stress, spread among people, and real physical symptoms. Some experts think the same forces were at work in Strasbourg.

The Dancing Plague may be a story about the power of the human mind, especially the shared mind of a group under strain. A community may have been so stressed and afraid that its people’s bodies began to show what their minds could not say.

Frau Troffea and the hundreds who followed her were probably not poisoned or cursed by a saint. They were people, many of them poor and powerless. Their suffering may have grown so heavy that it burst out in the most dramatic way possible. It came through their bodies, in the streets, in front of everyone.

The city leaders may have made things worse because they acted on beliefs most experts now reject. Some think the same pattern still appears today. Mental distress turns into physical symptoms. The symptoms spread by contact. And well-meant but wrong help can make the crisis grow.

If Waller is right, the line between mind and body is not as firm as we like to think. Not every epidemic is caused by viruses or bacteria. Sometimes the cause may come from within, a dance of despair that no medicine can stop. Was that what happened in Strasbourg? No one can say for certain.
