The Franklin Expedition: Arctic Ice, Lost Ships, and the Inuit Who Knew Where They Were

On May 19, 1845, two of the most advanced ships in the world sailed from Greenhithe, in England. They were HMS Erebus and HMS Terror. They were heading for the Northwest Passage. This was a legendary sea route linking the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the frozen islands of the Canadian Arctic.

Sir John Franklin, a veteran Arctic explorer, was in command. He was fifty-nine years old. The ships carried 129 officers and men and food for three years. They also had early steam engines, made from railway engines. England was at the height of its power, and everyone was confident.

The Royal Navy had been exploring the Arctic for decades. The Northwest Passage was the last great prize of sea exploration. Franklin’s expedition was the best-equipped and best-supplied ever sent into the ice. People expected it to succeed where others had failed.

Instead, it became the worst disaster in the history of Arctic exploration. Not one man came back. The ships vanished. For many years, the fate of the Franklin expedition was one of the great mysteries of the age. It only began to be truly solved in 2014 and 2016. Then the wrecks of Erebus and Terror were found on the Arctic seabed. Inuit oral history helped the searchers to find them. The Inuit are the native people of the Arctic.

The story is a tragedy of pride. It is also a detective story that lasted two centuries. And it reminds us that the knowledge of native peoples held truths that official searchers could not find for decades. Over the years, Inuit testimony, Victorian scandal, modern science, and underwater archaeology all played a part. Bit by painful bit, they showed what happened to 129 men in the harshest place on Earth.

Sir John Franklin was not the Admiralty’s first choice. The Admiralty is the group that ran the Royal Navy. Franklin was fifty-nine. He had not led an Arctic trip for many years.

His earlier trip over land, to map the Arctic coast of Canada, had ended in disaster. It lasted from 1819 to 1822. Eleven of his twenty men had died of hunger, and Franklin himself barely survived. But the first choice, Sir James Clark Ross, said no. Franklin got the job.

The ships were remarkable. Erebus and Terror were bomb vessels. These were heavy warships built to survive the kick of huge mortar guns. They had been changed a lot for Arctic work. Their hulls were strengthened with thick oak planks and iron covering.

Each had a steam engine taken from a railway locomotive. It could push the ship at about four knots, which helped a great deal in tight ice. They had hot-water heating pipes through the ship. They had machines to make fresh water from seawater. They carried a huge library.

The food supply was vast. It included 8,000 tins of canned food. The tins were sealed with solder that contained lead.

Stephen Goldner, a food processor, won the contract for the tins. He had only about seven weeks to fill them before the ships sailed. Some people say that the work was done in a hurry.

In 1981, Owen Beattie of the University of Alberta began to study remains from the expedition. He found much more lead in the bones than in the bones of local Inuit. One reading was 226 parts per million, compared with 26 to 36 for the Inuit. Some people think that the canned food was the cause.

Lead poisoning causes tiredness, confusion, and poor judgment. In the end, it can cause death. That is a terrible mix for men trying to survive in the Arctic. But newer research asks if lead alone could have doomed the expedition. Other causes may have played a part too.

The expedition sailed first to the Orkney Islands. Then it crossed the Atlantic to Disko Bay on the west coast of Greenland. It anchored there in late June 1845. Five men were sent home to England. That left 129.

On July 26, 1845, two whaling ships saw Erebus and Terror tied to an iceberg in Baffin Bay. They were waiting for the ice to clear. The whaling captains spoke briefly with Franklin’s officers. Then the two ships sailed west toward Lancaster Sound, the gateway to the Arctic islands. No European ever saw any member of the expedition alive again.

For three years, nothing was heard. At first the Admiralty was not worried, because Franklin had food for three years. In 1847 it began to organize search parties. Over the next years, dozens of expeditions went to look for Franklin and his men. It was one of the biggest searches of its time.

The first solid clue came in August 1850. Searchers found the ruins of the expedition’s first winter camp at Beechey Island, at the entrance to Wellington Channel. They found the graves of three crew members. They were John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine. All had died during the first winter, in 1845 and 1846.

The permafrost, which is ground frozen all year, had preserved the bodies. In the 1980s they were dug up and studied. They showed high lead levels and signs of tuberculosis, a lung disease. But Beechey Island showed only where the expedition had been. It did not show where it went or what happened next.

The most important document in the whole mystery was found on May 6, 1859. A search party led by Francis Leopold McClintock found it. Lady Franklin had sent them. The place was a stone pile, called a cairn, at Victory Point on the northwest coast of King William Island.

McClintock’s second-in-command, William Hobson, found a standard Admiralty form there. It had two messages, written a year apart. The first was dated May 28, 1847. It said that Erebus and Terror had spent the winter in the ice off Beechey Island in 1845 and 1846. Then they had sailed on. It said: “Sir John Franklin commanding the Expedition. All Well.”

The second message was written in the margins of the same form. Captain Fitzjames of Erebus wrote it on April 25, 1848. It told a very different story. Sir John Franklin had died on June 11, 1847, just two weeks after the first message. By April 1848, nine officers and fifteen men had died. The ships had been stuck in the ice since September 12, 1846, for nineteen months.

The surviving officers had decided to leave the ships. They planned to march south to the Great Fish River, now called the Back River, on the Canadian mainland. The Victory Point note is the last written message from the expedition that has been found.

In 1854, a Scottish explorer, Dr. John Rae, was mapping the Arctic coast for the Hudson’s Bay Company. Near Pelly Bay, he met a group of Inuit. They had objects that came from the expedition. One of them was a small silver plate with Franklin’s name on it.

The Inuit told Rae a grim story. They had seen a large party of white men, at least forty of them, pulling a boat south on King William Island. Later, the Inuit had found the bodies of about thirty men. The Inuit said that the men had eaten human flesh in their last desperate days. That is called cannibalism.

Rae told the Admiralty, and the London Times printed the story. The public was furious. Many people in Victorian England refused to believe that officers and gentlemen of the Royal Navy could do such a thing.

Charles Dickens was the most famous writer in English. He wrote an angry two-part essay in his magazine, Household Words. He attacked Rae’s report and the Inuit who had told the story. Lady Franklin was also horrified. She did not want to believe that her husband’s men had acted in this way.

Rae was attacked for telling the story. It took more than a century for scientific evidence to support it.

The Inuit of the central Arctic had detailed oral accounts. The Netsilik Inuit of King William Island and the Inuinnait of nearby lands passed them down for generations. They told of meeting Franklin’s men and of where the ships ended up. For a long time, many European researchers ignored or dismissed this knowledge.

Inuit stories said that one ship sank on the west coast of King William Island. They said that the other sank at a place called Ugjulik, off the Adelaide Peninsula. Parks Canada worked with Inuit researchers such as Louie Kamookak of Gjoa Haven. The search used this testimony for years.

Erebus was found in September 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, near O’Reilly Island. It lay in about eleven meters of water. Terror was found in September 2016 in Terror Bay, off King William Island, in about 24 meters of water. An Inuk hunter named Sammy Kogvik had told the search crew about a tall piece of wood that he had once seen sticking out of the ice there. Inuit elders had known of these ships for generations. Many Europeans had not listened.

Parks Canada’s team has studied the wrecks under water. Their finds have been remarkable. On Terror, the team saw a mess table, a food tin, wine bottles, and the ship’s bell.

The cold water kept the ships in good shape. Terror was not crushed by the ice, as people had long believed. Its windows are still whole. Inside there may be papers that could finally answer the open questions.

Modern science has studied bones from King William Island and from the graves on Beechey Island. It suggests that several problems piled up together. Lead poisoning may have damaged the nerves and judgment. Scurvy, a sickness from lack of vitamin C, may also have weakened the men. The ships carried lemon juice, but cooking can destroy vitamin C.

Tuberculosis was found in the Beechey Island remains. Crowded ships would have helped it spread. Nobody knows for sure which of these problems did the most harm.

The final killers were hunger and cold. After leaving the ships in April 1848, the survivors faced a march of hundreds of miles across barren, frozen land. They had too few supplies. They did not know the Inuit ways of surviving.

John Rae’s report about cannibalism was later supported. In the 1990s, scientists who study old bones examined remains on King William Island. One was Anne Keenleyside of Trent University. They found cut marks on the bones. These are signs of survival cannibalism.

It was not a ritual or a crime. It was the final, desperate act of starving men trying to stay alive. Many Victorian leaders had condemned Rae and ignored Inuit testimony. In 2014, a plaque was put up at Rae’s birthplace in Orkney. It honored his achievements. In the same year, the wreck of one of the ships he had sought was found.

The Franklin expedition teaches about the limits of technology and the pride of empire. It also teaches about the wisdom of people who were once dismissed as primitive. Sir John Franklin and his men sailed into the Arctic with the best equipment the Industrial Revolution could make. The Arctic killed them all.

For more than 160 years, the official searchers, scientists, and historians could not find the ships. Inuit oral history pointed to where they might lie. The discovery of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror is a great success of modern archaeology. It belongs as much to Inuit oral history as to sonar and underwater robots.

The study of the wrecks is still going on. The remote location makes the work slow. One day it may reveal written records that tell, in the men’s own words, what really happened in those last terrible months.

But even if the logs stay sealed forever, the outline of the story is clear. There was lead, ice, hunger, and a doomed march into nowhere. One hundred and twenty-nine men sailed into the ice. The ice kept them for more than 160 years. The Inuit never forgot. What the men themselves wrote in their final days may still be waiting in the wrecks.
