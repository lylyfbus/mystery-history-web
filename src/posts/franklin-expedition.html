The Franklin Expedition: Arctic Ice, Lost Ships, and the Inuit Who Knew Where They Were

Two ships tied to an iceberg

On July 26, 1845, the crews of two whaling ships saw HMS Erebus and HMS Terror tied to an iceberg. The two navy ships were waiting for the ice to clear. The whaling captains spoke briefly with Franklin’s officers. Then the two ships sailed west toward Lancaster Sound, the gateway to the Arctic islands.

No European ever saw any member of the expedition alive again.

The ships had left Greenhithe, in England, on May 19, 1845. They were looking for the Northwest Passage. This was a legendary sea route that links the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the frozen islands of northern Canada. After a stop in Greenland, five men were sent home. That left 129 men on board.

Not one of them came back. It is the worst disaster in the history of Arctic exploration. For many years, it was one of the great mysteries of the age. Let us follow the clues, as the searchers did.

A second-choice leader, a fine pair of ships

Sir John Franklin was in command. He was fifty-nine years old, and he was a veteran Arctic explorer. But he was not the Admiralty’s first choice. The Admiralty is the group that ran the Royal Navy. The first choice, Sir James Clark Ross, said no. So Franklin got the job.

His earlier trip over land, to map the Arctic coast of Canada, had ended in disaster. It lasted from 1819 to 1822. Eleven of his twenty men died of hunger, and Franklin himself barely survived.

Still, the Royal Navy had been exploring the Arctic for decades. The Northwest Passage was the last great prize of sea exploration.

The ships, at least, were remarkable. Erebus and Terror were bomb vessels. These were heavy warships built to survive the kick of huge mortar guns. For the Arctic, they were changed a lot. Their hulls were strengthened with thick oak planks and iron covering.

Each had a steam engine taken from a railway locomotive. It could push the ship at about four knots, which helped a great deal in tight ice. They had hot-water heating pipes, machines to make fresh water from seawater, and a huge library.

The food supply was vast. It included 8,000 tins of canned food. A food processor named Stephen Goldner won the contract, and he had only about seven weeks to fill the tins. They were sealed with solder that contained lead. Some people say that the work was done in a hurry.

That small detail would matter much later. First, three years of silence had to pass.

Three years of silence

At first the Admiralty was not worried, because Franklin had food for three years. In 1847 it began to organize search parties. Over the next years, dozens of expeditions went to look for Franklin and his men. It was one of the biggest searches of its time.

The first solid clue came in August 1850. Searchers found the ruins of the expedition’s first winter camp at Beechey Island. They found the graves of three crew members: John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine. All had died during the first winter, in 1845 and 1846.

The ground there is permafrost, which means that it is frozen all year. It had kept the bodies well. In the 1980s they were dug up and studied. They showed high lead levels and signs of tuberculosis, a lung disease.

But Beechey Island showed only where the expedition had been. It did not show where it went next.

The note under the stones

The most important paper in the whole mystery was found on May 6, 1859. A search party led by Francis Leopold McClintock found it. Lady Franklin had sent them. The place was a stone pile, called a cairn, at Victory Point on King William Island.

McClintock’s second-in-command, William Hobson, found a standard Admiralty form there. It had two messages, written a year apart. The first was dated May 28, 1847. It said that the ships had spent the winter off Beechey Island and then sailed on. It said: “Sir John Franklin commanding the Expedition. All Well.”

The second message was written in the margins of the same form. Captain Fitzjames of Erebus wrote it on April 25, 1848. It told a very different story. Sir John Franklin had died on June 11, 1847. By April 1848, nine officers and fifteen men had died. The ships had been stuck in the ice since September 12, 1846.

The survivors had decided to leave the ships. They planned to march south to the Great Fish River, now called the Back River. This is the last written message from the expedition that has been found. What happened on that march?

What the Inuit told Rae

In 1854, a Scottish explorer, Dr. John Rae, was mapping the Arctic coast. Near Pelly Bay, he met a group of Inuit. The Inuit are the native people of the Arctic. They had objects from the expedition, including a small silver plate with Franklin’s name on it.

The Inuit told Rae a sad story. They had seen a large party of white men, at least forty of them, pulling a boat south on King William Island. Later, the Inuit had found the bodies of about thirty men. They said that in their last desperate days, the men had eaten human flesh.

Rae told the Admiralty, and the London Times printed the story. The public was furious. Many people in Victorian England refused to believe it. The famous writer Charles Dickens wrote an angry essay in his magazine, Household Words. He attacked Rae’s report and the Inuit who had told the story. Lady Franklin did not want to believe it either.

Rae was attacked for telling the truth as he heard it. It took more than a century for science to support him.

Listening at last

The Netsilik Inuit of King William Island and the Inuinnait of nearby lands had passed down detailed accounts for generations. They told of meeting Franklin’s men and of where the ships ended up. For a long time, many European researchers dismissed this knowledge.

Inuit stories said that one ship sank on the west coast of King William Island. They said that the other sank at a place called Ugjulik, off the Adelaide Peninsula. Parks Canada worked with Inuit researchers such as Louie Kamookak of Gjoa Haven, and the search used this testimony for years.

Erebus was found in September 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, in about eleven meters of water. Terror was found in September 2016 in Terror Bay, in about 24 meters of water. An Inuk hunter named Sammy Kogvik had told the search crew about a tall piece of wood that he had once seen sticking out of the ice there.

The cold water kept the ships in good shape. Terror was not crushed by the ice, as people had long believed. Its windows are still whole. On board, the team saw a mess table, a food tin, wine bottles, and the ship’s bell. The wrecks may still hold papers that could answer the open questions.

Lead, sickness, and cold

So why did 129 men die? Modern science has studied bones from King William Island and from the graves on Beechey Island. It suggests that several problems piled up together.

Remember the tins with lead in the solder. In 1981, Owen Beattie of the University of Alberta found much more lead in the bones than in the bones of local Inuit. One reading was 226 parts per million, compared with 26 to 36 for the Inuit. Lead poisoning causes tiredness, confusion, and poor judgment. Some people think that the canned food was the cause. But newer research asks if lead alone could have doomed the expedition.

Scurvy, a sickness from lack of vitamin C, may also have weakened the men. The ships carried lemon juice, but cooking can destroy vitamin C. Tuberculosis was found in the Beechey Island remains, and crowded ships would have helped it spread. Nobody knows for sure which problem did the most harm.

The final killers were hunger and cold. After leaving the ships in April 1848, the survivors faced a march of hundreds of miles across frozen land. They had too few supplies.

In the 1990s, scientists who study old bones examined remains on King William Island. One was Anne Keenleyside of Trent University. They found cut marks on the bones, which support what the Inuit had told Rae. Some experts think that it was a last, desperate act of starving men trying to stay alive.

Still waiting in the wrecks

Franklin and his men sailed with the best equipment that the age could make. The Arctic took them all. For more than 160 years, the official searchers could not find the ships. Inuit oral history pointed to where they might lie. The discovery of the wrecks belongs as much to that history as to sonar and underwater robots.

The study of the wrecks is still going on. The remote place makes the work slow. One day it may reveal written records that tell, in the men’s own words, what happened.

We know the outline now. There was lead, ice, hunger, and a long march into nowhere. We do not know the last details. Think again of those two ships, tied to an iceberg in the summer of 1845, with 129 men waiting for the ice to clear. What they wrote in their final days may still be waiting in the wrecks.
