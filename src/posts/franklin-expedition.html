The Franklin Expedition: Arctic Ice, Lost Ships, and the Inuit Who Knew Where They Were

On May 19, 1845, two of the most advanced ships in the world sailed from Greenhithe, in England. They were HMS Erebus and HMS Terror. They were heading for the Northwest Passage. This was a legendary sea route linking the Atlantic and Pacific oceans through the frozen islands of the Canadian Arctic.

Sir John Franklin, a veteran Arctic explorer, was in command. He was fifty-nine years old. The ships carried 129 officers and men and food for three years. They also had early steam engines, made from railway engines. England was at the height of its power, and everyone was confident.

The Royal Navy had been exploring the Arctic for decades. The Northwest Passage was the last great prize of sea exploration. Franklin’s expedition was the best-equipped and best-supplied ever sent into the ice. People expected it to succeed where others had failed.

Instead, it became the worst disaster in the history of Arctic exploration. Not one man came back. The ships vanished. For fifteen years, the fate of the Franklin expedition was the most gripping mystery in the English-speaking world. It only began to be truly solved in 2014 and 2016. Then the wrecks of Erebus and Terror were found on the Arctic seabed. They lay exactly where Inuit oral tradition had said they were. The Inuit are the native people of the Arctic.

The story is a tragedy of pride. It is also a detective story that lasted two centuries. And it reminds us that the knowledge of native peoples held truths that official searchers could not find for decades. Over the years, Inuit testimony, Victorian scandal, modern science, and underwater archaeology all played a part. Bit by painful bit, they showed what happened to 129 men in the harshest place on Earth.

Sir John Franklin was not the Admiralty’s first choice. The Admiralty is the group that ran the Royal Navy. Franklin was fifty-nine and overweight. He had not been to the Arctic for nearly twenty years.

His earlier trip over land, to map the Arctic coast of Canada, had ended in disaster. It lasted from 1819 to 1822. Eleven of his twenty men had died of hunger, and Franklin himself barely survived. But the first choice, Sir James Clark Ross, said no, and other candidates were not free. Franklin got the job. Perhaps it was because his strong-willed wife, Jane, Lady Franklin, worked tirelessly for him.

The ships were remarkable. Erebus and Terror were bomb vessels. These were heavy warships built to survive the kick of huge mortar guns. They had been changed a lot for Arctic work. Their hulls were strengthened with thick oak planks and iron covering.

Each had a steam engine taken from a railway locomotive. It could push the ship at about four knots, which helped a great deal in tight ice. They had hot-water heating pipes through the ship. They had machines to make fresh water from seawater. They carried a huge library.

The food supply was vast. It included over 120,000 pounds of flour and nearly 34,000 pounds of preserved meat. It also included 8,000 tins of canned food. The tins were sealed with solder that contained a lot of lead.

Stephen Goldner, a food processor in London, provided the food. He won the Admiralty contract with the lowest bid. His tins were large, made in a hurry, and badly soldered. The lead solder was put on so carelessly that it often dripped into the food.

When the tins were examined decades later, many had faulty seals. These let in bacteria. In 1981, Owen Beattie of the University of Alberta began to study remains from the expedition. He found levels of lead in the bones hundreds of times higher than normal. This fits long-term lead poisoning from the canned food.

Lead poisoning causes tiredness, confusion, and poor judgment. In the end, it can cause death. That is a terrible mix for men trying to survive in the Arctic. But newer research asks if lead alone could have doomed the expedition. The crew of Terror had eaten from similar tins on an earlier trip to Antarctica, from 1839 to 1843. They showed no clear harm.

The expedition sailed first to the Orkney Islands. Then it crossed the Atlantic to Disko Bay on the west coast of Greenland. It anchored there in late June 1845. Five men were sent home to England as unfit. That left 129.

On July 26, 1845, two whaling ships saw Erebus and Terror tied to an iceberg in Baffin Bay. They were waiting for ice to clear in Lancaster Sound. The whaling captains spoke briefly with Franklin’s officers. Then the two ships sailed west into Lancaster Sound, the gateway to the Arctic islands. No European ever saw any member of the expedition alive again.

For three years, nothing was heard. At first the Admiralty was not worried, because Franklin had food for three years. In 1847 it began to organize search parties. Over the next ten years, more than thirty expeditions went to look for Franklin and his men. It was the largest search and rescue in history.

The first solid clue came in August 1850. Searchers found the ruins of the expedition’s first winter camp at Beechey Island, at the entrance to Wellington Channel. They found the graves of three crew members. They were John Torrington, John Hartnell, and William Braine. All had died during the first winter, in 1845 and 1846.

The permafrost, which is ground frozen all year, had preserved the bodies. In the 1980s they were dug up and studied. They showed high lead levels and signs of tuberculosis, a lung disease. But Beechey Island showed only where the expedition had been. It did not show where it went or what happened next.

The most important document in the whole mystery was found on May 6, 1859. A search party led by Francis Leopold McClintock found it. Lady Franklin had sent them. The place was a stone pile, called a cairn, at Victory Point on the northwest coast of King William Island.

McClintock’s second-in-command, William Hobson, found a standard Admiralty form there. It had two messages, written a year apart. The first was dated May 28, 1847. It said that Erebus and Terror had spent the winter in the ice off Beechey Island in 1845 and 1846. Then they had sailed south through Wellington Channel. It said: “Sir John Franklin commanding the Expedition. All Well.”

The second message was written in the margins of the same form. Captain Fitzjames of Erebus wrote it on April 25, 1848. It told a very different story. Sir John Franklin had died on June 11, 1847, just two weeks after the first message. By April 1848, nine officers and fifteen men had died. The ships had been stuck in the ice since September 12, 1846, for nineteen months.

The surviving officers had decided to leave the ships. They planned to march south to the Great Fish River, now called the Back River, on the Canadian mainland. The Victory Point note was the expedition’s last written word.

In 1854, a Scottish explorer, Dr. John Rae, was mapping the Arctic coast for the Hudson’s Bay Company. Near Pelly Bay, he met a group of Inuit. They had a remarkable collection of objects. There was a silver fork with the Franklin family crest, a medal that belonged to one of Franklin’s officers, buttons, telescopes, and other items from the expedition.

The Inuit told Rae a grim story. They had met a large party of white men, perhaps forty of them. The men were pulling a boat on a sledge south along the west coast of King William Island. Later, the Inuit had found the bodies of about thirty men. Marks on the bodies showed that the men had eaten human flesh in their last desperate days. That is called cannibalism.

Rae told the Admiralty, and the London Times printed the story. The public was furious. Victorian England refused to believe that officers and gentlemen of the Royal Navy could do such a thing.

Charles Dickens was the most famous writer in English. He wrote an angry two-part essay in his magazine, Household Words. He attacked Rae’s report and cast doubt on whether Inuit could be trusted. Lady Franklin was also horrified. She was not upset only by the cannibalism. She could not bear the thought that her husband’s men had died in any way but heroically.

Rae was attacked for telling the truth. It took more than a century for scientific evidence to prove him right.

The Inuit of the central Arctic had detailed oral accounts. The Netsilik Inuit of King William Island and the Inuinnait of nearby lands passed them down for generations. They told of meeting Franklin’s men and of where the ships ended up. For over a century, European researchers ignored or dismissed this knowledge.

Inuit stories said one ship was crushed in ice near O’Reilly Island. Another drifted south to Terror Bay. Then Parks Canada and the Inuit community of Gjoa Haven began a serious search based partly on this testimony. The results were extraordinary.

Erebus was found in September 2014 in Wilmot and Crampton Bay, near O’Reilly Island. It lay in only eleven meters of water. Terror was found in September 2016 in Terror Bay, on the south coast of King William Island. That is exactly where Inuit accounts had placed it. The Inuit had known the answer for about 160 years. Nobody had thought to ask them properly.

Parks Canada’s team has studied the wrecks under water. They can only work in short summer windows. Their finds have been remarkable. Divers have entered the captain’s cabin of Erebus. They have brought up hundreds of objects. These include navigation instruments, china dishes, personal items marked with officers’ initials, medicine bottles, and science equipment.

The state of preservation is amazing. Leather boots, brass buttons, and even scraps of paper have survived in the cold Arctic water. On Terror, the hatches are closed. This suggests the ship was carefully shut before it was left. Inside there may be logbooks, maps, photographs, and written records that could finally answer the open questions.

Modern science has studied bones from King William Island and from the graves on Beechey Island. It shows several disasters piling up together. Lead poisoning from badly soldered cans damaged the nerves and judgment. Scurvy, a sickness from lack of vitamin C, may also have weakened the men. The ships carried lemon juice, but cooking can destroy vitamin C.

Tuberculosis was found in the Beechey Island remains. Crowded ships would have helped it spread. Some researchers say that badly sterilized canned food could have caused botulism. That is a poisoning that leads to fast paralysis and death.

The final killers were hunger and cold. After leaving the ships in April 1848, the survivors faced a march of hundreds of miles across barren, frozen land. They had too few supplies. They did not know the Inuit ways of surviving.

John Rae was right about cannibalism. In the 1990s and 2000s, scientists who study old bones examined remains from dozens of sites on King William Island. One was Anne Keenleyside of Trent University. They found clear marks of cutting and breaking. These are the clear signs of survival cannibalism.

It was not a ritual or a crime. It was the final, desperate act of starving men trying to stay alive. The Victorian leaders had condemned Rae and ignored Inuit testimony. They owed his memory a deep apology. In 2014, the Royal Geographical Society put up a plaque at Rae’s birthplace in Orkney. It honored his achievements. In the same year, the wreck of one of the ships he had sought was found.

The Franklin expedition teaches about the limits of technology and the pride of empire. It also teaches about the wisdom of people who were once dismissed as primitive. Sir John Franklin and his men sailed into the Arctic with the best equipment the Industrial Revolution could make. The Arctic killed them all.

For 160 years, the official searchers, scientists, and historians could not find the ships. The Inuit knew where they were. They had always known. The discovery of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror is among the greatest archaeological successes of our time. It belongs as much to Inuit oral history as to sonar and underwater robots.

The study of the wrecks is still at an early stage. The short Arctic diving season and the remote location limit it. One day it may reveal written records that tell, in the men’s own words, what really happened in those last terrible months.

But even if the logs stay sealed forever, the outline of the story is clear. There was lead, ice, hunger, and a doomed march into nowhere. One hundred and twenty-nine men sailed into the ice. The ice kept them for 170 years. The Inuit never forgot. What the men themselves wrote in their final days may still be waiting in the wrecks.
