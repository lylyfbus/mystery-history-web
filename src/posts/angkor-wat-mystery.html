Angkor Wat: The Largest Religious Monument Ever Built and the City That Vanished

Angkor Wat at dawn, the largest religious monument ever constructed, reflects in its ancient moat

A temple in the jungle

Imagine a dense jungle in northwestern Cambodia. In the middle of it, water-filled moats full of lotus flowers surround a huge stone temple. The walls around it are more than a kilometer long on each side. This is Angkor Wat, said to be the largest religious monument in the world. Its name means “City of Temples” in the Khmer language.

The temple covers about 162.6 hectares (401 acres). Five famous towers rise above the plain. The tallest is 65 meters high. They stand in a pattern like the five dots on a die.

The great city around the temple may have been the largest city in the world before the age of factories. Yet after some centuries, most of it was left empty, and the jungle grew over it. So the mystery of Angkor has two parts. How was it built? And why was it left empty? Let us start with the king who began it.

The king and his map of the world

King Suryavarman II came to power around 1113 CE. He was a king of huge ambition, in war and in building. His reign lasted until about 1150 CE. He led armies in wars with his neighbors, and he began the biggest building project of his reign. He made the temple for the Hindu god Vishnu.

Angkor Wat was much more than a temple. It was a map of the Hindu universe, drawn in stone. The five central towers stand for the five peaks of Mount Meru. This sacred mountain stands in the center of the Hindu world and is the home of the gods. The moat measures about 1,500 by 1,300 meters. It is said to stand for the cosmic ocean.

Most Khmer temples face east, toward the rising sun. Angkor Wat faces west. West is linked with death in Hindu belief. This is one reason some scholars think the temple was also built as the king’s tomb. It is a guess, and nobody has found his burial place there.

But a design is only a plan. What did it take to build it?

Millions of blocks of stone

Angkor Wat was built from an estimated 5 to 10 million sandstone blocks. Some weigh up to 1.5 tons each. The stone came from the Kulen Hills, about 40 kilometers to the northeast. Nobody is sure how it was moved. Canals may have helped.

Like the Great Pyramid of Giza, it was built without modern machines. But the pyramids are plain, and Angkor Wat is not. Almost every surface is carved. Walls, doorways, columns, roofs, and galleries show gods, dancers, warriors, and scenes from Hindu epics.

The walls carry almost 1,000 square meters of carvings that stand out from the stone. They are called bas-reliefs. They show scenes from Hindu myths, real battles, and the daily life of the Khmer Empire at its height.

The most famous carving is the “Churning of the Ocean of Milk.” It stretches along a long gallery wall. It shows 88 devas (gods) and 92 asuras (demons) pulling on the serpent Vasuki. They are churning the cosmic ocean to make the drink that gives life forever. Many figures have their own faces, jewelry, and clothes. It feels almost like a film.

The temple was only the center, though. Around it stood something far bigger.

A city under the trees

Angkor Wat did not stand alone. It was the center of a huge city called Angkor. This city was the capital of the Khmer Empire from the ninth to the fifteenth centuries CE. Its true size stayed hidden until the twenty-first century.

Then scientists used LIDAR, which stands for Light Detection and Ranging. The surveys began in 2012. Aircraft fired millions of laser pulses at the ground. The machines measured how long the light took to bounce back. From this, researchers made detailed 3D maps of the ground under the thick trees.

The Greater Angkor Project made the maps. It is led by the University of Sydney with the École française d’Extrême-Orient (EFEO) and Cambodian authorities. Under the jungle lay whole neighborhoods, roads, canals, ponds, and temples. Researchers on the ground had never seen them.

The city covered about 1,000 square kilometers. Experts say it is the largest known city from before the age of factories. It was a wide land of villages, rice fields, and temple groups, all joined by roads and waterways. A web of canals, reservoirs, and moats carried water for perhaps 750,000 people. The scans also showed temples that nobody knew about. One of them is Mahendraparvata. It lies hidden on the Kulen Hills, and it is older than Angkor Wat.

How could so many people live there? The secret was water.

Angkor has a tropical climate with a strong monsoon cycle. For months the rain pours down. Then for months it is dry. The Khmer engineers solved this problem with a clever system of water works. It had barays, which are huge man-made lakes. It also had canals, moats, and banks of earth.

They caught the monsoon rain and let it out in the dry season. So the rice fields had water all year. One of the biggest lakes was the West Baray, near Angkor Wat.

The extra rice fed a huge city, its armies, and the workers who built the temples. But the same system may have held the city’s weakness. We will come back to that. First, there is a change inside the temple itself.

A faith written over

You can still see a change of religion in the stone. Suryavarman II built the temple for Vishnu, a god of the Hindu faith. The earliest carvings show Hindu scenes. Vishnu rests on the serpent Ananta. The battle of Kurukshetra comes from the Mahabharata.

By the late thirteenth century, Theravada Buddhism was spreading from Sri Lanka and the Mon kingdoms. Under its influence, the temple slowly became a Buddhist place of worship. New carvings showed the Buddha and stories from Buddhist writings. Some Hindu sculptures were replaced with Buddhist ones.

It is like a page that has been written over, and it makes Angkor Wat a rare record of a spiritual change. The temple is still an active Buddhist place of worship today. Monks and pilgrims come to pray, as people have done for more than 800 years.

But by the fifteenth century, the city around it was in deep decline.

Why did the city fall silent?

People still argue about the exact causes. It seems that problems with nature, politics, and money all came together.

The first answer is war. In 1431, the Siamese (Thai) kingdom of Ayutthaya attacked Angkor and robbed it. This was a heavy blow to the already weak Khmer state. The Khmer people later moved south, away from Angkor, and toward the sea and trade routes. Some experts think trade by sea was becoming more important than the land routes that had kept Angkor rich.

But the Siamese attack was probably the last blow, not the root cause. Nature played a big part too. Scientists studied tree rings from trees in Vietnam. These showed long droughts in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. After each drought came very heavy monsoon floods.

The Khmer water system was built for a steady, predictable monsoon. It may not have coped with these extremes. In droughts, the lakes may have run dry. In floods, floodwater carved the canals and filled them with sand and gravel. Then the damage may have spread through the whole system. Experts think climate was one cause among several, together with trouble in politics and in the economy.

The temple that was never lost

Of all the temples of Angkor, Ta Prohm shows best how nature takes back what people build. King Jayavarman VII built it. Modern experts have left it half in ruins on purpose. Huge silk-cotton trees and strangler figs grow right out of its walls and roofs. Their roots pour over the doorways and slowly crush the stone blocks.

The French naturalist Henri Mouhot is often said to have “discovered” Angkor Wat in 1860. But this is a myth. The temple was never truly lost. Local Cambodian communities had kept it as a Buddhist place of pilgrimage for centuries. Earlier European visitors had also seen it, such as a Portuguese friar in 1586.

Today Angkor Wat is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1992. It is one of the most visited ancient sites in the world, with about 2.6 million foreign visitors in 2018. It appears on the Cambodian flag, and it has been there since the first version in 1863.

Stone that outlasts its builders

So what do we know? A king built a temple for Vishnu in the first half of the twelfth century. A huge city grew around it, fed by a clever water system. Later the faith changed from Hindu to Buddhist. And the city was left mostly empty after attacks, droughts, and floods. Experts still argue about how much each of these counted.

Angkor Wat is a paradox, which means two opposite things are true at once. The Khmer Empire was a leading power in Southeast Asia for hundreds of years. Now that empire is gone from the world. The lakes of its water system have silted up into meadows.

And yet the temple lives on. Millions of visitors stand in its galleries. They look at carvings made by hands that have been dust for eight centuries. The engineers, the stone masons, and the kings are all gone. But their work remains.

So when we look at Angkor today, one question remains. Why did such a great city fall silent?
