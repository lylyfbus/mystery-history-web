Angkor Wat: The Largest Religious Monument Ever Built and the City That Vanished

Angkor Wat at dawn, the largest religious monument ever constructed, reflects in its ancient moat

Imagine a dense jungle in northwestern Cambodia. In the middle of it, water-filled moats full of lotus flowers surround a huge stone temple. The walls around it are more than a kilometer long on each side. This is Angkor Wat, said to be the largest religious monument in the world. Its name means “City of Temples” in the Khmer language.

The temple covers about 162.6 hectares (401 acres). Five famous towers rise above the plain. The tallest is 65 meters high. They stand in a pattern like the five dots on a die. This pattern stands for the sacred peaks of Mount Meru.

The walls carry almost 1,000 square meters of carvings that stand out from the stone. These carvings are called bas-reliefs. They show scenes from Hindu myths, real battles, and the daily life of the Khmer Empire at its height.

King Suryavarman II built the temple in the first half of the twelfth century. He was a very ambitious king. He made the temple for the Hindu god Vishnu. Some scholars also think it was meant to be the king’s own tomb.

The great city around the temple may have been the largest city in the world before the age of factories. Yet after some centuries, most of it was left empty, and the jungle grew over it. The temple itself was never fully forgotten, but the outside world mostly forgot the city.

This is a story of human ambition pushed to the limit, and then pulled back by nature and history. It holds great engineering, changes of religion, and a mighty empire that reached too far. It also holds the strange beauty of ruins taken back by the forest.

Modern scientists have scanned the land with lasers. This method is called LIDAR. It showed that the Angkor city complex was far larger than anyone had imagined. It was a spread-out “megacity” of about 1,000 square kilometers. A web of canals, reservoirs, and moats carried water for perhaps 750,000 people.

So the mystery of Angkor has two parts. How was it built? And why was it left empty? The answers tell us how fragile even the greatest human work can be.

Suryavarman II came to power around 1113 CE. He was a king of huge ambition, in war and in building.

His reign lasted until about 1150 CE. He led armies in wars with his neighbors. He also began the biggest building project of his reign.

Angkor Wat was much more than a temple. It was a map of the Hindu universe, drawn in stone. It was so huge that it needed very many workers, craftsmen, and engineers. They worked for many years.

The design is full of meaning. The five central towers stand for the five peaks of Mount Meru. This sacred mountain stands in the center of the Hindu world and is the home of the gods. The moat measures about 1,500 by 1,300 meters. It is said to stand for the cosmic ocean.

Most Khmer temples face east, toward the rising sun. Angkor Wat faces west. West is linked with death in Hindu belief. This is one reason some scholars think the temple was built as Suryavarman’s tomb. It is a guess, and nobody has found his burial place there.

The builders were very exact. Some studies say the main tower lines up with the sunrise on the spring equinox. Some people also think the sizes of the temple hold number patterns from the stars and the calendar.

Angkor Wat was built from an estimated 5 to 10 million sandstone blocks. Some weigh up to 1.5 tons each. The stone came from the Kulen Hills, about 40 kilometers to the northeast. Nobody is sure how it was moved. Canals may have helped.

Like the Great Pyramid of Giza, it was built without modern machines. But the pyramids are plain, and Angkor Wat is not. Almost every surface is carved. Walls, doorways, columns, roofs, and galleries show gods, dancers, warriors, and scenes from Hindu epics.

The most famous carving is the “Churning of the Ocean of Milk.” It stretches along a long gallery wall. It shows 88 devas (gods) and 92 asuras (demons) pulling on the serpent Vasuki. They are churning the cosmic ocean to make the drink that gives life forever.

Many figures have their own faces, jewelry, and clothes. The whole scene is full of movement and energy. It feels almost like a film.

Angkor Wat did not stand alone. It was the center of a huge city called Angkor. This city was the capital of the Khmer Empire from the ninth to the fifteenth centuries CE.

The true size of the city stayed hidden until the twenty-first century. Then scientists used LIDAR, which stands for Light Detection and Ranging. Lasers on aircraft can see through the leaves of the jungle. They showed the outline of a city of amazing size.

The Greater Angkor Project made the maps. It is led by the University of Sydney with the École française d’Extrême-Orient (EFEO) and Cambodian authorities. They found that the city covered about 1,000 square kilometers. Experts say it is the largest known city from before the age of factories.

The secret of this huge city was water. Angkor has a tropical climate with a strong monsoon cycle. For months the rain pours down. Then for months it is dry.

The Khmer engineers solved this problem with a clever system of water works. It had barays, which are huge man-made lakes. It also had canals, moats, and banks of earth. They caught the monsoon rain and let it out in the dry season. So the rice fields had water all year.

One of the biggest lakes was the West Baray, near Angkor Wat.

This water system gave the Khmer extra rice. The extra rice fed a huge city. It fed the armies that built the empire. It also fed the workers who built the temples.

The LIDAR surveys began in 2012. They changed what we know about the site. Aircraft fired millions of laser pulses at the ground. The machines measured how long the light took to bounce back. From this, researchers made detailed 3D maps of the ground under the thick trees.

The results were surprising. Under the jungle lay whole neighborhoods, roads, canals, ponds, and temples. Researchers on the ground had never seen them.

Angkor turned out to be a low-density megacity. It was a wide land of villages, rice fields, and temple groups, all joined by roads and waterways. It was very different from the tight, walled towns of medieval Europe.

The scans also showed temples that nobody knew about. One of them is Mahendraparvata. It lies hidden under the jungle on the Kulen Hills, and it is older than Angkor Wat.

One fascinating thing about Angkor Wat is how its religion changed over time. You can still see the changes in the stone.

Suryavarman II built the temple for Vishnu, a god of the Hindu faith. The earliest carvings show Hindu scenes. Vishnu rests on the serpent Ananta. The battle of Kurukshetra comes from the Mahabharata. The Churning of the Ocean of Milk is a famous Hindu story.

By the late thirteenth century, Theravada Buddhism was spreading from Sri Lanka and the Mon kingdoms. Under its influence, the temple slowly became a Buddhist place of worship. New carvings showed the Buddha and stories from Buddhist writings.

Some older Hindu carvings were changed. Some Hindu sculptures were replaced with Buddhist ones. The change of faith can still be read in the stone itself. It is like a page that has been written over, and it makes Angkor Wat a rare record of a spiritual change.

The temple is still an active Buddhist place of worship today. Monks and pilgrims come to pray, as people have done for more than 800 years.

By the fifteenth century, the great city of Angkor was in deep decline. People still argue about the exact causes. It seems that problems with nature, politics, and money all came together.

In 1431, the Siamese (Thai) kingdom of Ayutthaya attacked Angkor and robbed it. This was a heavy blow to the already weak Khmer state. The Khmer people later moved south, away from Angkor, and toward the sea and trade routes. Some experts think trade by sea was becoming more important than the land routes that had kept Angkor rich.

But the Siamese attack was probably the last blow, not the root cause. Nature played a big part too. Scientists studied tree rings from trees in Vietnam. These showed long droughts in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. After each drought came very heavy monsoon floods.

The Khmer water system was built for a steady, predictable monsoon. It may not have coped with these extremes. In droughts, the lakes may have run dry. In floods, floodwater carved the canals and filled them with sand and gravel. Then the damage may have spread through the whole system. Experts think climate was one cause among several, together with trouble in politics and in the economy.

Of all the temples of Angkor, Ta Prohm shows best how nature takes back what people build. King Jayavarman VII built it. Modern experts have left it half in ruins on purpose.

Huge silk-cotton trees and strangler figs grow right out of its walls and roofs. Their roots pour over the doorways and slowly crush the stone blocks. The sight is hauntingly beautiful. It seems to tell the whole story of Angkor.

French experts from the EFEO began conservation work at Angkor in the early twentieth century. They found that the jungle had done much damage. Tree roots had pushed stones apart. Monsoon rains had worn away carvings.

They decided to leave Ta Prohm mostly as it was. They wanted to keep the powerful picture of nature and architecture locked in a slow struggle.

The French naturalist Henri Mouhot is often said to have “discovered” Angkor Wat in 1860. But this is a myth. The temple was never truly lost. Local Cambodian communities had kept it as a Buddhist place of pilgrimage for centuries. Earlier European visitors had also seen it, such as a Portuguese friar in 1586.

What Mouhot did was bring Angkor to the attention of the West. His vivid books inspired French colonial explorers. Later, in 1908, the EFEO set up a program to look after Angkor.

Today Angkor Wat is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1992. It is one of the most visited ancient sites in the world, with about 2.6 million foreign visitors in 2018.

Keeping it safe is a constant struggle. Experts worry that so many visitors wear down the stone. The tropical climate also speeds up decay.

Teams from France, Japan, China, Germany, and other countries work there all the time. They use old stone-working skills and also laser scanning and 3D models. Their aim is to save the temple for the future.

Angkor Wat is a paradox, which means two opposite things are true at once. The Khmer Empire built it and was a leading power in Southeast Asia for hundreds of years. Now that empire is gone from the world. The water system that fed the greatest city of its age has been dry for centuries. Its canals are clogged with earth, and its lakes have silted up into meadows.

The faith of the temple changed too. First came Hindu belief. Then came Buddhism.

And yet the temple lives on. It appears on the Cambodian flag, and it has been there since the first version in 1863. Millions of visitors stand in its galleries. They look at carvings made by hands that have been dust for eight centuries.

The engineers who designed the water system, the stone masons who carved the walls, and the kings who ordered the temples are all gone. But their work remains. It shows how deeply people want to build something that will outlast the builder.

So when we look at Angkor today, one question remains. Why did such a great city fall silent?
