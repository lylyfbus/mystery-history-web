Angkor Wat: The Largest Religious Monument Ever Built and the City That Vanished

Angkor Wat at dawn, the largest religious monument ever constructed, reflects in its ancient moat

Imagine a dense jungle in northwestern Cambodia. In the middle of it, water-filled moats full of lotus flowers surround a huge stone temple. The walls around it are more than a kilometer long on each side. This is Angkor Wat, the largest religious monument ever built by human hands. Its name means “City of Temples” in the Khmer language.

The temple covers about 162.6 hectares (401 acres). That is so big that it makes every cathedral, mosque, and temple on Earth look small. Five famous towers rise 65 meters above the plain. They stand in a pattern like the five dots on a die. This pattern stands for the sacred peaks of Mount Meru.

The walls carry more than 12,000 square meters of carvings that stand out from the stone. These carvings are called bas-reliefs. They show scenes from Hindu myths, real battles, and the daily life of the Khmer Empire at its height.

King Suryavarman II built the temple in the first half of the twelfth century. He was one of the most ambitious rulers in the history of Southeast Asia. He made the temple for the Hindu god Vishnu. Many scholars also believe it was meant to be the king’s own tomb.

Yet within three centuries, the great city around the temple was swallowed by the jungle. It may have been the largest city complex in the world before the age of factories. Its people left it, and the outside world mostly forgot it.

This is a story of human ambition pushed to the limit, and then pulled back by nature and history. It holds great engineering, changes of religion, and a mighty empire that reached too far. It also holds the strange beauty of ruins taken back by the forest.

Modern scientists have scanned the land with lasers. This method is called LIDAR. It showed that the Angkor city complex was far larger than anyone had imagined. It was a spread-out “megacity” of more than 1,000 square kilometers, bigger than modern Los Angeles. A web of canals, reservoirs, and moats carried water for more than 750,000 people.

So the mystery of Angkor has two parts. How was it built? And why was it left empty? The answers tell us how fragile even the greatest human work can be.

Suryavarman II came to power around 1113 CE. There had been a violent fight over who should rule, and it may even have included the murder of the king before him. He was a king of huge ambition, in war and in building.

His reign lasted until about 1145 or 1150 CE. He led armies across Southeast Asia. His power reached from the coast of Vietnam to the Malay Peninsula. He also began the biggest building project the region had ever seen.

Angkor Wat was much more than a temple. It was a map of the Hindu universe, drawn in stone. It was so huge that it needed tens of thousands of workers, craftsmen, and engineers. They worked for about 37 years.

The design is full of meaning. The five central towers stand for the five peaks of Mount Meru. This sacred mountain stands in the center of the Hindu world and is the home of the gods. The moat measures 1,500 by 1,300 meters, nearly a square mile of water. It stands for the cosmic ocean.

The three rings of galleries stand for three levels of the universe. These are the underworld, the earth, and the heavens.

Most Khmer temples face east, toward the rising sun. Angkor Wat faces west. In Hindu belief, west is the direction of death. So many scholars think the temple was built as Suryavarman’s tomb.

The builders were very exact. The walls point to the four main directions with an error of less than one degree. The sizes of the temple seem to hold number patterns. These suggest that the builders knew a great deal about the stars and the calendar.

Angkor Wat was built from an estimated 5 to 10 million sandstone blocks. Some weigh up to 1.5 tons each. The stone came from the Kulen Hills, about 40 kilometers to the northeast. It was probably moved along canals. Rollers and elephants may have helped too.

The amount of stone is about the same as in the Great Pyramid of Giza. But the pyramids are plain, and Angkor Wat is not. Almost every surface is carved. Walls, doorways, columns, roofs, and galleries show gods, dancers, warriors, and scenes from Hindu epics.

The most famous carving is the “Churning of the Ocean of Milk.” It stretches for nearly 50 meters along a gallery wall. It shows 88 devas (gods) and 92 asuras (demons) pulling on the serpent Vasuki. They are churning the cosmic ocean to make the drink that gives life forever.

Every figure has its own face, jewelry, and clothes. The whole scene is full of movement and energy. It feels almost like a film.

Angkor Wat did not stand alone. It was the center of a huge city called Angkor. This city was the capital of the Khmer Empire from the ninth to the fifteenth centuries CE.

The true size of the city stayed hidden until the twenty-first century. Then scientists used LIDAR, which stands for Light Detection and Ranging. Lasers on aircraft can see through the leaves of the jungle. They showed the outline of a city of amazing size.

The Greater Angkor Project made the maps. It is a joint effort of the University of Sydney, the École française d’Extrême-Orient (EFEO), and Cambodian authorities. They found that the city covered about 1,000 square kilometers. It is the largest known city from before the age of factories.

The secret of this huge city was water. Angkor has a tropical climate with a strong monsoon cycle. For months the rain pours down. Then for months it is almost dry.

The Khmer engineers solved this problem with a clever system of water works. It had barays, which are huge man-made lakes. It also had canals, moats, and banks of earth. They caught the monsoon rain and let it out in the dry season. So the rice fields had water all year.

The biggest lake, the West Baray, was about 8 kilometers long and 2.2 kilometers wide. It held about 50 million cubic meters of water. It was one of the biggest man-made lakes of the ancient world.

This water system gave the Khmer extra rice. The extra rice fed a huge city. It fed the armies that built the empire. It also fed the workers who built the temples.

The LIDAR surveys began in 2012. They changed what we know about the site. Aircraft fired millions of laser pulses at the ground. The machines measured how long the light took to bounce back. From this, researchers made detailed 3D maps of the ground under the thick trees.

The results were astonishing. Under the jungle lay whole neighborhoods, roads, canals, ponds, and temples. Researchers on the ground had never seen them.

Angkor turned out to be a low-density megacity. It was a wide land of villages, rice fields, and temple groups, all joined by roads and waterways. It was very different from the tight, walled towns of medieval Europe. Its population was similar, but it covered a far larger area.

The scans also showed temples that nobody knew about. One of them is Mahendraparvata. It lies hidden under the jungle on the Kulen Hills, and it is older than Angkor itself.

One fascinating thing about Angkor Wat is how its religion changed over time. You can still see the changes in the stone.

Suryavarman II built the temple for Vishnu, a god of the Hindu faith. The earliest carvings show Hindu scenes. Vishnu rests on the serpent Ananta. The battle of Kurukshetra comes from the Mahabharata. The Churning of the Ocean of Milk is credited to the Ramayana.

By the late thirteenth century, Theravada Buddhism was spreading from Sri Lanka and the Mon kingdoms. Under its influence, the temple slowly became a Buddhist place of worship. New carvings showed the Buddha and stories from Buddhist writings.

Some older Hindu carvings were changed. Figures of Vishnu were quietly re-carved to look like the Buddha. The change of faith can still be read in the stone itself. It is like a page that has been written over, and it makes Angkor Wat a rare record of a spiritual change.

The temple is still an active Buddhist place of worship today. Monks and pilgrims come to pray, as people have done for more than 800 years.

By the fifteenth century, the great city of Angkor was in deep decline. People still argue about the exact causes. It seems that problems with nature, politics, and money all came together.

In 1431, the Siamese (Thai) kingdom of Ayutthaya attacked Angkor and robbed it. This was a heavy blow to the already weak Khmer state. The Khmer court moved the capital south to Phnom Penh. That city was closer to the sea and to trade routes.

Those routes had become more and more important. Trade by sea was taking over from the land routes that had kept Angkor rich.

But the Siamese attack was probably the last blow, not the root cause. Nature played a big part too. Scientists studied mud cores taken from the Tonle Sap lake and from the moats of Angkor. These showed long droughts in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. In between came times of very heavy monsoon floods.

The Khmer water system was built for a steady, predictable monsoon. It may not have coped with these extremes. In droughts, the lakes ran dry. In floods, the canals overflowed, and the banks of the barays may have broken.

Then the damage spread through the whole system. Without steady water, less rice grew. Without extra rice, the city could not be fed. Without a fed people, nobody could keep the water system working. Each failure made the next one worse.

Of all the temples of Angkor, Ta Prohm shows best how nature takes back what people build. King Jayavarman VII built it. Modern experts have left it half in ruins on purpose.

Huge silk-cotton trees and strangler figs grow right out of its walls and roofs. Their roots pour over the doorways and slowly crush the stone blocks. The sight is hauntingly beautiful. It seems to tell the whole story of Angkor.

French experts from the EFEO began conservation work at Angkor in the early twentieth century. They found that the jungle had done terrible damage. Tree roots had pushed stones apart. Monsoon rains had worn away carvings. Years of plants had made the foundations unsafe.

They decided to leave Ta Prohm mostly as it was. They wanted to keep the powerful picture of nature and architecture locked in a slow struggle.

The French naturalist Henri Mouhot is often said to have “discovered” Angkor Wat in 1860. But this is a myth. The temple was never truly lost. Local Cambodian communities had kept it as a Buddhist place of pilgrimage for centuries.

What Mouhot did was bring Angkor to the attention of the West. His vivid books inspired French colonial explorers. Eventually, in 1898, they set up the EFEO conservation program.

Today Angkor Wat is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 1992. It is one of the most visited ancient sites in the world, with over 2.5 million tourists each year.

Keeping it safe is a constant struggle. So many visitors wear down the stone. Pumping out groundwater threatens the foundations. Thieves still steal carvings. The tropical climate speeds up decay.

Teams from France, Japan, China, Germany, and other countries work there all the time. They use old stone-working skills and also laser scanning and 3D models. Their aim is to save the temple for the future.

Angkor Wat is a paradox, which means two opposite things are true at once. The Khmer Empire built it and was the leading power in Southeast Asia for nearly five centuries. Now that empire is gone from the world. The water system that fed the greatest city of its age has been dry for centuries. Its canals are clogged with earth, and its lakes have silted up into meadows.

The religions that inspired the temple have changed too. First came Buddhism. Then came the modern world of the twenty-first century.

And yet the temple lives on. It appears on the Cambodian flag, the only national flag in the world to show a building. Millions of visitors stand in its galleries. They look at carvings made by hands that have been dust for eight centuries.

The engineers who designed the water system, the stone masons who carved the walls, and the kings who ordered the temples are all gone. But their work remains. It shows how deeply people want to build something that will outlast the builder.

Everything else is only a comment on how long stone lasts and how short an empire’s life can be. So when we look at Angkor today, one question remains. Why did such a great city fall silent?
