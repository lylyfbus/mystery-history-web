The Yonaguni Monument: Japan's Underwater Mystery

A diver looks for sharks

In 1986, a diver named Kihachiro Aratake went into the sea off Yonaguni Island. Yonaguni is the westernmost island of Japan. Each winter, hammerhead sharks gather there. Aratake was looking for a new place to watch them.

He found something else.

About 25 meters below the surface, in clear blue water, lay a huge block of stone. It stretched across the seafloor like the bones of a giant building. It had flat terraces, straight walls, and sharp right angles. A wide, level path ran along its base.

Aratake had found what the world now calls the Yonaguni Monument. Is it a ruin left by people long ago? Or did nature carve it? Scientists have argued about this for nearly four decades.

A giant in the blue

First, let us find the place. Yonaguni belongs to Japan’s Ryukyu Islands. It is about 107 kilometers from Taiwan, which is closer than the main islands of Japan. About 1,700 people live there.

The monument is also called the Yonaguni Submarine Ruins. Sources give different sizes. The main shape is about 100 to 150 meters long. Its highest point is about 5 or 6 meters under the surface, and its base goes down to about 25 meters.

The rock is fine-grained sandstone and mudstone of the Yaeyama Group. It formed about 20 million years ago, in the Early Miocene epoch.

Now let us see what the divers saw.

Steps, walls, and a road

Many people who see the main formation think that it looks like a giant stepped platform or pyramid. There is a large, flat upper terrace. There are steps that go down like a huge staircase. There are almost straight walls that meet at angles of about 90 degrees.

There is also a broad, flat path along the base, often called “The Road.” It looks very even for a long way. Smaller platforms and ledges sit around it.

People who think that the site is made by people say that there are too many of these shapes, and they are too regular, to be natural. Divers’ photos and videos show huge angular blocks that rise from the sandy seafloor, with flat sides and sharp edges.

But a building under the sea needs one more thing to be possible. It needs dry land.

When the sea was lower

About 20,000 years ago, during the coldest time of the last Ice Age, the seas were much lower than today. The formation now lies under water. So back then, it would have stood on dry land.

Supporters say that if people built it, they built it before the seas rose at the end of the Ice Age. It would then be among the oldest structures on Earth, as old as Göbekli Tepe in Turkey or older.

But not everyone agrees. The archaeologist Richard Pearson thinks that the story of how the site sank is unlikely.

Two scientists stand at opposite ends of this debate, so let us meet them.

Kimura: people made it

Dr. Masaaki Kimura is a Japanese marine geologist at the University of the Ryukyus. He has studied the monument for many years. He is the best-known supporter of the idea that people made it.

His view has changed over time. At first he said that the site was at least 10,000 years old. In a 2007 report, he changed that to 2,000 to 3,000 years.

He points to features that he says natural forces could not explain. One is the even width of “The Road.” Another is the right angles where walls meet. He also sees what he thinks are carvings on some surfaces. And he sees smaller shapes around the main one that he believes form a whole building complex.

Kimura has seen in it a castle, roads, monuments, and even a stadium. He and others link the site to the Jōmon, who lived in Japan from about 14,000 BCE to 300 BCE. Kimura also links it to the legend of Mu, a lost continent. That idea has no support in mainstream geology or archaeology.

Schoch: nature made it

Dr. Robert Schoch is an American geologist at Boston University. He first dived at Yonaguni in 1997. He agrees that the formation is impressive. But he says that the natural qualities of the rock can explain it.

Sandstone and mudstone are sedimentary rocks, which form from layers of sand and mud. They break naturally along joint planes. These are old lines of weakness in the rock. When the rock breaks along them, it leaves flat faces and right angles. The area also has many earthquakes, and quakes crack rock along those planes.

Schoch says that the steps match the natural layers in the rock. He says that the straight walls come from jointing. He thinks that the carvings Kimura sees are natural scratches.

He also points out that similar rock shapes appear on land nearby, on Yonaguni itself. Another famous example is the Giant’s Causeway in Northern Ireland, where nature made very geometric features.

So who is right?

What most experts say

Most geologists think that the monument is a natural formation. Rock layers cracked along joint planes, and erosion and earth movement shaped it.

In 2024, a team of geologists led by Hironobu Suga of Kyushu University reported that no archaeological remains or signs of human activity have been found there. They also saw erosion still at work on the rock.

Some writers call the claims of a human origin pseudoarchaeology, which means a false kind of archaeology. And neither Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs nor the government of Okinawa Prefecture treats the monument as an important cultural object. Neither has done research or protection work there.

Is the story over, then? Not quite, because the monument has taught us something else.

What the stones teach us

The monument will probably not rewrite history. But it does remind us of an idea that is worth taking seriously. Rising seas at the end of the last Ice Age flooded large areas of dry land. Some early human communities may lie under the ocean now, out of reach and mostly unmapped.

The debate also shows how hard underwater archaeology is. On land, a careful dig can show layers of living, building methods, and objects. Underwater sites can only be reached by diving. That is hard on the body and limited in time. It allows looking and some sampling, but not a full dig.

So what do we know? Most geologists say that the rock is natural, and the 2024 team found no signs of human activity. What is open is whether any human hand ever touched it. Kimura and his supporters think so, and Schoch does not.

Think again of Aratake in the clear blue water in 1986. He went looking for sharks and found stairs on the seafloor. Nature can make shapes that look just like buildings, given enough time and the right rock. And the sea still hides places that nobody has explored.
