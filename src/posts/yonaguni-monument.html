The Yonaguni Monument: Japan's Underwater Mystery

In 1986, a diving guide named Kihachiro Aratake slipped into the warm sea off the south coast of Yonaguni Island. Yonaguni is the westernmost inhabited island of Japan. Aratake was looking for a new spot to watch the hammerhead sharks that gather there each winter.

He found something else. It would start one of the hottest debates in modern archaeology. Archaeology is the study of the past through the things people left behind.

About 25 meters below the surface, in clear blue water, lay a huge block of stone. It stretched across the seafloor like the bones of a giant ancient building. It had flat terraces, almost straight walls, and sharp right angles. A wide, level path ran along its base.

Aratake had found what the world now calls the Yonaguni Monument. It is a rock structure under the sea. It is so angular, so geometric, and so unlike anything else on the ocean floor that it has split geologists, archaeologists, and alternative researchers for nearly four decades.

The Yonaguni Monument is also called the Yonaguni Submarine Ruins. It lies in the Philippine Sea, about 100 meters off the south coast of Yonaguni. Yonaguni is the southernmost of Japan’s Ryukyu Islands. It is closer to Taiwan, about 108 kilometers away, than to the main islands of Japan. About 1,700 people live there, and people have lived on the island for at least two thousand years.

The main shape is about 150 meters long and 40 meters wide. Its highest point is just 5 meters under the surface. Its base goes down to about 40 meters. The rock is fine-grained sandstone and mudstone of the Yaeyama Group. This formed in the Early Miocene epoch, about 20 million years ago.

The debate since Aratake’s find comes down to one question. Are the odd, regular shapes the work of human hands? Or did nature make them?

Many people who see the main formation think it looks less like natural rock and more like a giant stepped platform or pyramid. There is a large, flat upper terrace about 100 meters long. There are steps going down in regular drops of one to two meters, like a huge staircase. There are almost straight walls up to 10 meters high that meet at angles of about 90 degrees.

There is also a broad, flat path, often called “The Road.” It runs along the base of the structure. It is about 5 meters wide and looks very even for a long way. Other features include what some people call a “stone circle” and a “twin megalith.” A megalith is a very large stone. There are also smaller platforms and ledges. People who think the site is man-made say there are too many, and they are too regular, to be natural.

The sight is truly striking. Divers’ photos and videos show huge angular blocks rising from the sandy seafloor. Their flat sides and sharp edges cast bold shadows in the filtered sunlight.

The strongest argument for a human origin is about sea level. About 20,000 years ago, during the Last Glacial Maximum, the seas were 120 to 130 meters lower than today. That was the coldest time of the last Ice Age. The formation now lies 5 to 40 meters under water. So back then it would have stood on dry land. It would have been part of a land bridge joining the Ryukyu Islands to the Asian mainland.

If people built it, they built it before the seas rose at the end of the Ice Age. That was about 10,000 to 8,000 years ago. It would then be one of the oldest large structures on Earth. It would be older than Göbekli Tepe and older than the oldest known cities. It would be older than the start of what most archaeologists call civilization.

Two main voices stand at opposite ends of the debate. One is Dr. Masaaki Kimura, a Japanese marine geologist at the University of the Ryukyus. He has studied the monument for over two decades. He is the strongest supporter of the idea that it is man-made. The other is Dr. Robert Schoch, an American geologist at Boston University. He is best known for his controversial new dating of the Great Sphinx of Giza. He has dived at Yonaguni and argues firmly that the formation is natural.

Kimura’s view has changed over the years. At first he was careful. Later he became more and more sure that people had shaped the rock. He points to several features that he says natural forces could not explain. One is the even width of “The Road.” Another is the right angles where walls meet. He also sees what he thinks are tool marks on some surfaces. And he sees smaller shapes around the main one that he believes form a whole building complex.

Kimura has suggested that it could have been a castle, a temple, or a ceremonial center of an ancient people. They might have been the Jōmon. The Jōmon lived in Japan from about 14,000 BCE to 300 BCE. They are known for their fancy pottery and for some of the earliest signs of village life in East Asia.

Schoch first dived at Yonaguni in 1997. He sees it very differently. He agrees that the formation is impressive and its features are striking. But he says the natural qualities of the rock can explain every part of it.

Sandstone and mudstone are sedimentary rocks, which form from layers of sand and mud. They break naturally along joint planes. These are old lines of weakness in the rock. When the rock breaks along them, it leaves flat faces and right angles. The area also has many earthquakes, and quakes crack rock along those planes.

Schoch says the steps match the natural layers in the rock. He says the straight walls come from jointing. He says “The Road” is a natural shelf. It was made by uneven erosion along an especially hard layer. He points out that similar shapes, just as regular, appear on land around the world. One famous example is the Giant’s Causeway in Northern Ireland, where nature made very geometric features.

Neither Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs nor the government of Okinawa Prefecture treats the monument as an important cultural object. Neither has done research or protection work there. The site is not on any list of cultural property. No official dig has been approved. No peer-reviewed archaeology journal has published evidence for a man-made origin. Peer-reviewed means that other experts have checked the work before it is printed.

Most archaeologists overwhelmingly think it is a natural formation. Several scholars have called the claims of a human origin pseudoarchaeology, which means a false kind of archaeology. Some supporters link Yonaguni to the legend of Mu, or Lemuria. This is a lost continent in the Pacific that people first proposed in the nineteenth century. But the Mu idea has no support in mainstream geology or archaeology. The study of plate tectonics, the slow movement of Earth’s crust, has shown that such a lost continent is false.

Yonaguni matters not because it will probably rewrite history. Science says clearly that it is natural. It matters because it shows how hard it is to study the past. Suppose an unknown civilization once lived on coasts that are now under the sea. Even a small chance of this is one of the most tempting questions in archaeology.

The coastal settlement theory says that early human towns were mostly along coasts that are now underwater. Many archaeologists accept it. Rising seas at the end of the last Ice Age drowned millions of square kilometers of dry, livable land. Some of the earliest human communities may lie under the ocean now, out of reach and mostly unmapped.

The Yonaguni debate also shows how hard underwater archaeology is. On land, a careful dig can reveal layers of living, building methods, and objects. Underwater sites can only be reached by diving. That is hard on the body and limited in time. It allows looking and some sampling, but not a full dig.

The Yonaguni Monument sits on the border between what we know and what we wish were true. It has an undeniable visual power. It is a huge, angular, stepped shape on the ocean floor in one of the most earthquake-prone parts of the world. When you look at it, you want it to be man-made. When you study it as a geologist, you see that nature can make things that look just like buildings, given enough time and the right rock.

The mainstream view is clear. The Yonaguni Monument is a natural formation. Rock layers cracked along joint planes, and millions of years of erosion and earth movement shaped it. No tools, pottery, bones, or inscriptions have ever been found at or near it. A real archaeological site would have such things.

But even if it is natural, it has done something useful. It has drawn attention to the vast lands that were dry during the last Ice Age and are now under water. They may hide real treasures that nobody has found yet. Sometimes the best discoveries are not the ones that prove our ideas. They are the ones that remind us how much we still have to learn.
