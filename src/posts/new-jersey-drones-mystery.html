New Jersey Drones: The Mystery in the Sky

In mid-November 2024, people in New Jersey began looking up at the night sky. What they saw frightened them. Strange flying machines seemed to be moving over their homes. Some witnesses said they were drones far bigger than anything people can buy in a shop. A drone is a small aircraft with no pilot on board.

The machines appeared over neighborhoods, military bases, and important public works. Witnesses said they moved in the dark with bright lights, white, red, and green. Some of the lights seemed to hover and shift.

The reports started in Morris County. On November 13, 2024, an unauthorized drone was confirmed over Picatinny Arsenal, a U.S. Army research base. Within days, sightings had spread to Somerset County and other parts of New Jersey. In early December, the FBI asked people to report drones near the Raritan River. Similar reports were coming in from New York, Connecticut, Pennsylvania, and Maryland.

Many witnesses described very large machines. At a briefing on December 11, officials spoke of drones up to six feet across, flying for six to seven hours a night. Some witnesses said they were as big as cars. Most drones sold in stores cannot fly for that long.

Some witnesses said drones flew in formation. Two lawmakers said drones followed a Coast Guard boat. Later records tied that report to passenger jets landing at a New York airport. The FAA also closed the sky over Trump’s golf club in Bedminster to drones.

The FBI, the Department of Homeland Security, and the FAA began investigations. The FAA is the Federal Aviation Administration, which controls the skies over the United States. Elected officials, from town mayors to the President-elect, demanded answers. A news report a year later said that dozens of sightings were still unresolved.

The story begins on November 13, 2024. That day a first confirmed unauthorized drone was detected in restricted airspace above Picatinny Arsenal. This is a U.S. Army center for developing weapons and explosives. It is in Morris County.

The base is surrounded by restricted airspace, where unauthorized flying machines are forbidden. Over the next weeks, ten more incursions were reported over the base, through December 6, 2024. A Picatinny spokesperson later said that the suspected drones were not the result of any military activity at the base.

At the same time, civilians in Morris County began to report strange lights in the sky. Witnesses said these were not the familiar blinking lights of airliners. They described bright lights that seemed to hover and drift slowly.

Many descriptions were alike. People described large objects, much bigger than the drones in electronics stores. Some said the objects were almost silent. Mayor Mike Ghassali of Montvale later said they were 15 to 20 feet long. That is far bigger than the official estimate. Size is very hard to judge in the night sky.

Some witnesses said the craft could hover in one place for a long time. That is far more than a typical store-bought drone can do on one battery charge.

The number of reports was huge. The FBI tip line had more than 3,000 reports by December 10. By December 16, federal officials had reviewed more than 5,000 reported sightings. Reports came from New Jersey and many other states.

The FAA put temporary flight limits over Trump’s Bedminster golf club on November 22, and later over Picatinny Arsenal. Federal officials also said there were a few sightings over other military sites.

Federal investigators looked at the thousands of reports. They said that many were lawful drones, aircraft with pilots, or stars. Some reports could not be explained so easily.

As reports multiplied, the federal government’s response showed both caution and public frustration. On December 12, 2024, John Kirby, a spokesperson for the White House National Security Council, spoke at a press briefing. He said that many of the reported objects were actually aircraft with pilots, flying legally. He also said there was no sign of a danger to the country or of a foreign plot.

The FBI, the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Defense, and the FAA issued a joint statement. On December 16, they said they had “not identified anything anomalous.” They said the sightings did not show a risk to the public or to national security. The statement was meant to calm the public.

But many people felt the official reassurances did not match what they saw. Governor Phil Murphy of New Jersey wrote to President Biden on December 13. He asked for more help from the federal government.

Senator Andy Kim of New Jersey took an unusual step. He joined local police on a night patrol on December 12. A sighting that night was later identified as a small plane. President-elect Donald Trump also spoke about the sightings. He suggested that the military should shoot down unknown drones.

All this politics added fuel to the public guessing. Then came the most explosive claim of all. Representative Jeff Van Drew of New Jersey said the drones were coming from an Iranian “mothership” off the East Coast. The claim was dramatic and alarming. The Pentagon denied it. It said there were no Iranian ships off the coast.

On December 18, the FAA banned drone flights for a month over 22 New Jersey communities. Later bans covered parts of New York as well.

On December 20, the Senate passed a bill to extend the powers of officials to deal with unwanted drones. Even so, sightings were still reported all through December. The gap between what officials said and what witnesses described never fully closed.

As federal investigators studied the reports, a mixed picture came out. The joint statement said the sightings included lawful drones flown by hobbyists, businesses, and police. It also named aircraft with pilots, and stars that people took for drones. Later records tied some reports to a Black Hawk helicopter, a small plane near a nuclear power plant, and passenger jets. Some reports stayed unexplained.

Experts pointed out something else. A video of “large drones” over a house in Maryland was shown to be the constellation Orion. Orion is a bright winter group of stars. People who do not know the night sky could mistake bright stars and planets, such as Venus, for the lights of a hovering aircraft.

Some sightings happened near airports. There, planes with their landing lights on could seem to hover or move slowly. And since April 2021, an FAA rule has let small drones fly at night if they have bright lights. So there may have been more night drones than in earlier years.

But these explanations may not account for every sighting. Some witnesses were police officers and others with training. They said what they saw did not match any ordinary explanation.

Several details from the 2024 drone wave are hard to explain. The unauthorized drones over Picatinny Arsenal were confirmed by law enforcement at the base. So at least some drones were really flying over a sensitive military research base. The reports there came again and again over three weeks. And the reports of large drones that stayed up for hours do not fit most drones on sale at the time. But witnesses can easily misjudge size and time at night.

One odd claim came in November 2025, in a report marking one year since the sightings. The mayor of Montvale, New Jersey, Mike Ghassali, passed on a story from a local drone hobbyist he trusted. The hobbyist said that one of the large mystery drones took control of his own drone and landed it. If true, this would point to technology far beyond anything the public can buy.

The hobbyist did not come forward in public. He feared harm to his career. So the story cannot be checked. The report also said that dozens of sightings were still unresolved a year after the first wave.

A key feature of the 2024 New Jersey drone mystery was the role of social media. It made the whole thing bigger and also twisted it. People posted blurry night photos, shaky videos of far-off lights, and more and more far-fetched theories about where the drones came from and what they were for.

The posts spread fast, and every new sighting, real or not, could reach a huge audience. This created a loop. The attention caused by the reports led to more reports, which led to more attention.

Some experts spoke of mass panic and social contagion. This is when worry spreads from person to person, and people read ordinary sights as proof of something mysterious. But it would be too simple to call all the reports mass hysteria. Drones were confirmed over Picatinny Arsenal, so some real drones were in the sky. This is true even if most reports had ordinary explanations.

This was not the first time that large numbers of people reported strange objects in the sky. It will not be the last. In 1946, about 2,000 sightings of “ghost rockets” were logged, mostly in Sweden and nearby lands. These were rocket-like objects that streaked across the sky after World War II. They led to official investigations by the Swedish and U.S. governments, among others.

In 2019 and 2020, people in Colorado and Nebraska reported unknown drones at night over farm areas.

In 1997, the Phoenix Lights incident took place. Many Arizona residents saw a huge V-shaped group of lights over Phoenix. The Air National Guard said some of the lights were flares dropped by training jets. Many witnesses reject that explanation.

The New Jersey drone sightings fit a familiar pattern. First comes a believable early report. Then the media spreads it, and social media spreads it like wildfire. A flood of new reports follows, and many are mistakes. And a small group of truly unexplained sightings keeps the mystery alive.

The 2024 New Jersey drone mystery was a big mass sighting event. It mixed real national security worries, the spread of fear from person to person, media attention, and a few truly unexplained sightings. Together they made a perfect storm of public anxiety.

Officials said that many sightings were lawful drones, aircraft with pilots, and stars. They found no sign of a national security threat. But some things keep the mystery alive. Unauthorized drones were confirmed over Picatinny Arsenal. Some sightings were still unresolved a year later. And some witnesses insist that what they saw was not an ordinary aircraft.

The New Jersey drone wave showed that today, with cameras everywhere, social media, and advanced drone technology, the line between the explained and the unexplained is blurrier than ever. Watching the skies can still turn up surprises that nobody in authority is ready to explain. What exactly were those lights? We still do not know.
