Machu Picchu: The Lost Citadel of the Incas That Was Never Really Lost

It is the morning of July 24, 1911, in the Peruvian Andes. A 35-year-old Yale lecturer named Hiram Bingham III climbs a steep, muddy slope. A local farmer has promised to show him some ruins on the ridge above.

Bingham was not looking for a lost city. He was searching for the last capital of the Neo-Inca resistance, which he thought was Vitcos. Then a local farmer named Melchor Arteaga told him about some old stone walls on a nearby peak.

What Bingham saw from the top of the ridge would change his life. It would also change how the world understood the Inca Empire. He saw a whole city of granite blocks, wonderfully well preserved. It lay across a narrow saddle between two peaks, 2,430 meters (7,970 feet) above sea level. The Urubamba River roared through the gorge about 450 meters (1,480 feet) below.

Bingham later claimed that he had “discovered” Machu Picchu. In truth, the site was never really lost. A few local farming families lived near the ruins. They grew crops on the old terraces.

Others had visited before Bingham. A Cusco explorer named Agustín Lizárraga wrote his name on a wall there in 1902. Maps from the 1870s also mention the place. What Bingham did was show Machu Picchu to the world. In 1912 he led a large expedition backed by Yale and National Geographic. It dug up many objects and photographed the site for an amazed public.

More than a century later, Machu Picchu draws more than a million visitors a year. It is one of the New Seven Wonders of the World, chosen in 2007. Yet big questions about the citadel have no answer.

Why was it built on a nearly unreachable mountain ridge? What was it really for? And why was it left empty after barely a century, to be swallowed by cloud forest? The Spanish conquered the Inca Empire, and many Inca sites were damaged or taken over. But there is no sign that they wrecked this one.

Experts long said Machu Picchu was built around 1450 CE. At that time Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui ruled (1438–1471). He was a Sapa Inca, or emperor. A 2021 study of bones from the site suggested people lived there from about 1420, a few decades earlier. He did more than anyone to turn the Inca from a regional kingdom into the largest empire in pre-Columbian America. Pre-Columbian means before Columbus arrived.

The name Pachacuti is often translated as “Earth-Shaker” in Quechua. He is said to have conquered huge lands and reorganized how the empire was run. He is also linked to some of the boldest building projects in Andean history, such as the rebuilding of Cusco.

Most archaeologists think Machu Picchu was a royal estate. It would have been a country palace. The emperor and his court could go there to leave the capital for religious ceremonies and to rest. The remote spot, the dramatic setting, and the grand religious buildings all fit this idea.

But the estate idea does not explain everything. Some features look more like a sacred sanctuary or an observatory than a pleasure palace. Some researchers think the place had several uses at once.

The citadel has roughly 150 to 200 buildings. They stand on broad terraces that run side by side around a huge central square. The buildings are made of granite blocks cut so exactly that they fit without mortar. The joints are very tight. This style is called ashlar masonry, and it marks the best Inca building.

The site has two main parts. One is the farming sector, with its spectacular terraces that step down the mountainside. The other is the urban sector, with temples, homes, workshops, and storehouses.

The urban sector is split again into the Hanan (upper) and Hurin (lower) districts. This two-part plan echoes the way Cusco was divided.

Three buildings stand out. The Intihuatana stone is a carved granite pillar. Many people believe it worked as a solar clock or a tool for watching the sky. Experts still argue about how it was used.

The Temple of the Sun is a half-round tower built around a huge natural rock. Some people think its windows were used to watch the sun. But modern studies say it was not a precise instrument. The Temple of the Three Windows has openings in the shape of a trapezoid. They frame views of the mountains around. This may have had a meaning in the Inca view of the cosmos.

The engineering needed to build here is almost as amazing as the buildings. The site sits on a ridge between two peaks. It is in one of the most earthquake-prone and unstable regions on Earth. Heavy rain, about 2,000 mm a year, threatens to wash everything down the mountain.

Inca engineers solved these problems with great skill. They built the terraces and ground in ways that drain water well.

The terraces were not only for farming. They were also huge retaining walls that held the slope in place and stopped landslides. The stone buildings had slightly leaning walls and trapezoid doors and windows. This made them very strong against earthquakes. Machu Picchu has stood through centuries of earthquakes.

The site also had a clever water system. Fresh spring water ran from the mountain above through stone-lined channels and fountains. It flowed down through the urban sector and gave hundreds of people a steady supply.

At its busiest, an estimated 500 to 750 people lived at Machu Picchu. That was small by Inca standards. Most were support staff, craftspeople, and religious specialists who served the royal court. Bones found in Bingham’s digs suggest a mixed population from many parts of the empire. That fits the idea of an imperial center that drew people from all over the Tawantinsuyu. This was the Inca name for their empire in Quechua.

The terraces grew maize, potatoes, and other crops. These were enough to feed the people who lived there. More supplies could come up from the valley below along the well-kept Inca road network.

One of the biggest mysteries is why it was abandoned. People lived there for about a century, until around 1530 or a little later. Then they left, and the cloud forest crept in. The timing matches the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire. That began in 1532, when Francisco Pizarro captured the Inca emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca.

Yet experts have found no clear evidence that the Spanish ever reached Machu Picchu. Cusco was taken by the Spanish and rebuilt. Many other Inca sites were damaged. Machu Picchu shows no sign of battle, burning, or deliberate wreckage.

Many experts think the site was quietly left as the empire fell apart around it. The people may have fled to join the Neo-Inca resistance at Vilcabamba. They may have gone back to their home provinces. Or they may have died of the European diseases that swept through the Andes after the first contact.

Smallpox, in particular, may have hit the people of Machu Picchu even before the Spanish came in force. Some experts think it killed most of the people there. That would have broken the systems that kept such remote places alive. This is an idea, not a proven fact.

When Hiram Bingham arrived in 1911, plants had mostly taken over the site, but the buildings were still sound. A few local families lived near the ruins. They farmed the old terraces.

Bingham’s story of his “discovery” was shaped by the ideas of his time. Back then, European and American explorers often said they had “found” places that local people had always known. Bingham took objects from the site, including human remains, pottery, and metalwork. They went to Yale University. Peru said they had only been lent. After a long dispute, Yale agreed in 2010 to return them. The first group went back to Peru in 2011, and the last in 2012.

People saw this return as a landmark in giving back cultural heritage that had been taken during colonial times. The objects are now kept at the Casa Concha museum in Cusco. Bingham became a professor, a United States Senator, and one of the most famous explorers of the twentieth century. But his legacy is mixed. It includes real scientific achievement and also the colonial attitudes that shaped his work.

Today Machu Picchu faces new problems. Huge numbers of visitors strain the fragile stone buildings and the land around. In 2025 there were more than 1.5 million.

The Peruvian government set up a ticket system with timed entry and a daily limit on visitors. The limit has been raised more than once. Even so, erosion, worn stone paths, and damage to the terraces are serious worries. Climate change is also seen as a threat to the ruins and the cloud forest around them.

Still, Machu Picchu remains one of the most inspiring archaeological sites on Earth. It shows the vision, engineering, and spiritual dreams of a people. They built a remarkable city on a remote mountain ridge. There is no clear sign that the conquistadors who conquered the Inca ever found it. So why did the Inca build it, and why did they leave?
