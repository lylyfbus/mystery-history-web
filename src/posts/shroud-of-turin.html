Shroud of Turin: The 14-Foot Linen That Science Still Can't Explain

In a cathedral in Turin, Italy, a long piece of linen cloth lies in a climate-controlled case. The cathedral is called the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist. Few objects on Earth have caused so much study, so much devotion, and so much argument.

The cloth is about 4.4 meters long and 1.1 meters wide (14.3 by 3.7 feet). On it, in faint amber-brown color, is the front and back of a man. His hands are crossed over his body and his eyes are closed. The marks on the cloth match a cruel execution on a cross. There are lines from a whip on the back. There are marks of nails in the wrists and feet, and a mark on the side. Blood has run across the scalp in a pattern like a crown of thorns.

For centuries, millions of Christians have honored this cloth as the burial cloth of Jesus of Nazareth. The Gospels describe a linen wrap that covered his body in the tomb. Scientists have spent decades asking a simple question. Is the image a miracle, a natural effect, or a medieval fake? After more than a century of testing, the answer is still unclear. Each time one side seems to win, new evidence reopens the case.

The Shroud has been tested in many ways. Scientists have used radiocarbon dating, which finds the age of old things by measuring a form of carbon in them. They have studied its blood, its pollen, its threads, and its image with computers. Even so, it has not given a final answer.

The Shroud first appears in the record in the 1350s. A French knight named Geoffrey de Charny showed it in a church he built in the small town of Lirey, France. He never explained how he got it. A local bishop later said it was a fake. For centuries the cloth passed among European noble families. It reached Turin in 1578 and has stayed there ever since.

In December 1532, a fire broke out in the Sainte-Chapelle in Chambéry, France, where the Shroud was kept in a silver box. The heat melted the silver, and the hot metal burned through the folded linen in several places. The burn holes and scorch marks can still be seen. People put out the fire with water. Later, nuns sewed patches over the damaged parts. Some researchers say the water, smoke, and heat of that fire may have added carbon to the cloth and changed later dating results.

The modern story began in May 1898. An Italian amateur photographer named Secondo Pia was allowed to photograph the Shroud at a public showing. In his darkroom he developed his glass-plate negatives, and he was stunned. The image on the cloth is itself a kind of negative, with light and dark reversed. On his photograph, the faint marks turned into a clear, detailed picture of a man. It was much clearer than anything seen on the cloth with the eye.

This seemed hard to explain. Photography had not been invented in the 1350s. Could a medieval forger have made a negative image on purpose? The Pia photographs started the modern scientific study of the Shroud.

In 1988, the Vatican finally allowed radiocarbon dating. Small samples were cut from a corner of the cloth. They went to three separate laboratories: the University of Oxford, the University of Arizona, and the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH) in Zurich. The results appeared in the journal Nature in 1989. All three labs dated the cloth to between 1260 and 1390 CE, with 95% confidence. That fits a medieval cloth and its first record in the 1350s. Many newspapers said the case was closed.

But questions came quickly. The sample came from one corner of the cloth, chosen because it was already damaged. In 2000, researchers Sue Benford and Joseph Marino said that this corner held medieval repair threads woven into the old cloth. They called it an “invisible reweave.” If the sample mixed first-century linen with newer thread, the date would land somewhere in between, as the tests showed.

Critics also say the corner was the part most dirtied by centuries of handling and by the 1532 fire. No expert in radiocarbon dating has formally backed the repair idea. The 1988 result is still the accepted view of science. Yet the argument has never fully ended, and no new samples have been allowed.

Another relic is linked to the Shroud. It is the Sudarium of Oviedo, a face cloth kept in the Cathedral of San Salvador in Oviedo, Spain. Its history can be traced to the 7th century, much earlier than the 1350s. Some researchers say its bloodstains match the blood marks on the face of the Shroud. Both cloths are reported to carry blood of type AB. If they covered the same body, the Shroud would be older than the Middle Ages. This link is still debated.

Long before 1988, the Shroud had drawn serious science. The biggest study was the Shroud of Turin Research Project, known as STURP. About 30 American scientists worked in Turin in October 1978. They brought several tons of equipment. They tested for 120 hours in a row, in shifts, for five days.

STURP’s main finding was plain. The image is not painted. The team found no paint, no brush strokes, and no artist’s materials on the image. The image came from changes on the top of the linen fibers, which were darkened by a chemical change. This change reached only about 200 nanometers deep. That is about one-fifth the thickness of a human hair.

The bloodstains, however, were real blood. The team found hemoglobin, a part of blood, and not paint. The blood seems to have been on the cloth before the body image formed, because the image does not appear under the bloodstains. A forger would have had to add real blood first and then make the image around it. That would make a forgery much harder.

A Swiss criminologist named Max Frei studied pollen using sticky tape taken from the cloth. He found pollen grains from plants of the Judean desert, Anatolia, and Europe. That fits a cloth that traveled from the Middle East through the Byzantine Empire to western Europe.

The image also has an odd 3D quality. A machine called the VP-8 image analyzer turned its brightness into a three-dimensional shape. The brightness matches how far the cloth was from the body. No known painting or photograph does this.

How was the image made? That is the biggest mystery. One idea is called the Maillard reaction. It says that gases from a decaying body, such as ammonia, reacted with tiny traces on the linen and left the image. That is possible, but no one has shown that it makes an image this good.

A second idea says that a short, strong burst of radiation scorched the top fibers. In 2022, Italian researchers used a method called wide-angle X-ray scattering. They said the linen showed signs of ancient aging, not medieval aging. That gave this idea more support.

The simplest explanation is still a medieval fake. Yet no one has copied the Shroud’s special features with medieval materials and methods. The image is thin, it is a negative, and it holds 3D information. All of this is still very hard to repeat.

The Shroud sits where science, faith, and history meet. The 1988 radiocarbon date says medieval. The STURP study says the image is not painted. The blood tests say real blood. The negative image seems beyond medieval skill. Each clue points in a slightly different direction, and no single theory fits them all.

Perhaps the Shroud is the cleverest fake in history, made by a genius whose methods were never matched. Perhaps it is something else, a natural effect we do not yet understand. It does not prove or disprove anyone’s beliefs. What makes it special is that after a century of the most careful study any object has ever had, it still keeps its secret. We can date rocks on Mars and read the DNA of Neanderthals. Yet one plain piece of linen still resists our best efforts to explain it.
