Shroud of Turin: The 14-Foot Linen That Science Still Can't Explain

A photograph that stunned its maker

It was May 1898. An Italian photographer named Secondo Pia took the first photograph of a famous old cloth in Turin, Italy. Later he looked at his negative plate. A negative is a picture in which light and dark are reversed. Pia was stunned.

The cloth itself is a kind of negative, with light and dark reversed. On the photograph, its faint marks turned into a clear picture of a man. It was much clearer than anything that anyone had seen on the cloth with the eye.

This seemed hard to explain. Photography had not been invented in the 1350s, when the cloth first appears in the record. Could a medieval forger have made a negative image on purpose? The photographs of Pia started the modern scientific study of the cloth. It is called the Shroud of Turin. A shroud is a cloth that covers a body for burial.

A cloth in a cathedral

Today the Shroud lies in a protective case in the Cathedral of Saint John the Baptist, in Turin. It is a piece of linen about 4.4 meters long and 1.1 meters wide.

On the cloth, in a faint amber-brown color, is the front and back of a man. His hands are crossed over his body. Many people say that the marks look like a crucifixion. There are lines that look like whip marks on the back. There are marks in the wrists and feet, and a mark on the side. Stains on the head look like a crown of thorns.

For centuries, millions of Christians have honored this cloth as the burial cloth of Jesus of Nazareth. The Gospels describe a linen wrap that covered his body in the tomb. Scientists have spent decades asking a simple question. Is the image a miracle, a natural effect, or a medieval fake? Before we try to answer, let us follow the cloth through time.

From a small town to Turin

The Shroud first appears in the record in the 1350s. A French knight named Geoffrey de Charny started a church in the small town of Lirey, France. The cloth was shown there around 1355. Later a bishop, Pierre d’Arcis, wrote that it was a fake.

For centuries, the cloth passed among noble families. In 1532, a fire broke out in the chapel in Chambéry, France, where it was kept. Melted silver burned through the folded linen in several places. The burn marks can still be seen. Later, nuns sewed patches over the damaged parts.

The cloth reached Turin in 1578 and has stayed there ever since. Only much later did scientists come with their tools.

Scientists come to Turin

The biggest early study was the Shroud of Turin Research Project, known as STURP. A team of American scientists worked in Turin in October 1978. They tested the cloth for 120 hours in a row.

The team said that the image is not painted. They found no paints, dyes, or stains on the image. They linked the image to a chemical change in the top of the linen fibers.

They also said that the stains contain hemoglobin, a part of blood. This means, they said, that the stains are blood and not paint. Not every scientist accepts all of these conclusions.

The image also has an odd 3D quality. STURP said that a machine called the VP-8 image analyzer found three-dimensional information in it. So the team had found a cloth with no paint on the image. But the next test would point the other way.

The test that said “medieval”

In 1988, church leaders allowed radiocarbon dating. This method finds the age of old things by measuring a form of carbon in them. In April, a small sample was cut from one spot on the cloth. It was shared between three separate laboratories. They were the University of Oxford, the University of Arizona, and the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH) in Zurich.

The results appeared in the journal Nature in 1989. All three labs dated the cloth to between 1260 and 1390 CE, with 95% confidence. That fits a medieval cloth and its first record in the 1350s. For many people, the case seemed closed.

But questions came. We look at them next.

Doubts about the test

Researchers Sue Benford and Joseph Marino said that the sampled spot held medieval repair threads woven into the old cloth. They called it an “invisible reweave.” Other experts, including the textile expert Mechthild Flury-Lemberg, rejected this idea. The official report says that the sample was taken away from patches and burned areas.

One scientist also said that the fire of 1532 may have added carbon to the cloth and changed the dating results. Other tests did not support this idea.

In 2019, other researchers studied the raw numbers of the labs and found differences from the published ones. They asked for new tests. Still, no expert in radiocarbon dating has said that the 1988 result is unreliable. And church leaders have not released new samples since 1989. So the question of age stays open.

Another relic is linked to the Shroud. It is the Sudarium of Oviedo, a face cloth kept in the Cathedral of San Salvador in Oviedo, Spain. Its story says that it was taken from Jerusalem in the 600s, but that story is mostly legend. The first document about it is from 1075. Tests dated the cloth to around 700 CE. Some researchers say that both cloths carry blood of type AB. Other scientists say that such tests can give false results on old cloth. Any link between the two cloths is unproven. So a bigger question waits.

How was the image made?

That is the biggest mystery. One idea is called the Maillard reaction. It says that gases from a decaying body reacted with the linen and left the image. But the chemist Raymond Rogers found no signs of a decaying body on the image.

A second idea says that a short, strong burst of radiation scorched the top fibers. Most scientists do not accept it.

In 2022, Italian researchers tested one thread with a method called wide-angle X-ray scattering. They said that the linen was much older than the 1988 date. But this works only if the cloth was kept very warm for centuries. They asked other labs to repeat the test.

The simplest explanation is still a medieval fake. Some people have made similar images with medieval methods, such as rubbing linen over a carved relief. Supporters say that none of these copies matches every feature of the Shroud.

Still looking at the plate

Let us go back to Pia and his glass plate in 1898. Here is what we know. The 1988 radiocarbon date says medieval. The STURP team says that the image is not painted. Some tests say that the stains are blood. Supporters say that the negative image is hard to copy.

Here is what is still open. Perhaps the Shroud is a clever fake from the Middle Ages. Perhaps it is something else, a natural effect that we do not yet understand. Each clue points in a slightly different direction, and no single idea fits them all.

The Shroud does not prove or disprove anyone’s beliefs. What makes it special is that after more than a century of study, people still argue about it. We can read the DNA of Neanderthals. Yet one plain piece of linen still divides experts.
