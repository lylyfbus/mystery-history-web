The New England Vampire Panic of 1892: When a TB Outbreak Spawned a Vampire Hunt

It was a cold March morning in 1892. A group of men gathered at a cemetery in Exeter, Rhode Island. They had shovels in their hands and dread in their hearts. They had come to dig up the dead.

The graves belonged to the family of George Brown, a modest farmer. He had watched helplessly as consumption took his family. Consumption was a dreaded wasting disease. We now call it tuberculosis. It took his wife Mary Eliza in 1883, his daughter Mary Olive in 1884, and his daughter Mercy Lena in early 1892.

Now his son Edwin was gravely ill with the same disease.

The townspeople of Exeter had a theory. It came from generations of folk belief. They thought one of the dead Brown women was a vampire. She was rising from her grave at night to drain the life from her living relatives. The only way to save Edwin was to dig up the bodies, check them for signs of vampirism, and perform the right ritual.

George Brown was exhausted. He reportedly did not believe in vampires himself. But he went along with his neighbors.

On March 17, 1892, the men opened the graves. A doctor named Harold Metcalf and a newspaper reporter were there too. The bodies of Mary Eliza and Mary Olive had decayed as expected. But Mercy Lena had been placed in a crypt, because the ground was frozen in the cold New England winter. Her body looked oddly well preserved. And when they looked inside her chest, they found liquid blood in her heart and liver. The doctor said this was normal for a body buried so recently.

To the frightened villagers, this seemed to be proof. They believed Mercy Brown was a vampire. They removed her heart and liver and burned them on a nearby rock. They mixed the ashes into a medicine for Edwin to drink. It did not work. Edwin died two months later.

The Mercy Brown case is the most famous recorded case of the New England vampire panic. This was a wave of fear that swept through rural New England from the 1780s to the 1890s. The folklorist Michael Bell has found about 80 cases in the northeastern United States, most of them in New England.

It was not the work of foolish people. These were ordinary farm families who were afraid for their loved ones.

But their families were dying one by one from a disease they did not understand and could not cure. So they turned to an old folk tradition. It gave the only explanation that made sense to them: the dead were feeding on the living.

The panic was strongest in Rhode Island, eastern Connecticut, southern Massachusetts, Vermont, and other rural parts of New England. About a century before the first of these cases, this same region had been shaken by the Salem witch trials. In both cases, fear and pressure from neighbors played a part. Small communities faced threats they could not understand.

To understand the vampire panic, you need to know the disease that caused it. Tuberculosis was called consumption in the 18th and 19th centuries. It was one of the most feared diseases in history. In 19th-century America it was a leading cause of death.

It was called consumption because it seemed to eat its victims from the inside out. A healthy person would get a cough that would not go away. Then they would lose weight, grow pale and weak, and sweat through the night. Over months or years they wasted away.

The disease attacked the lungs. Victims coughed up blood. Their voices grew hoarse, their eyes sank, and their skin turned ghostly white. It was a long and terrible way to die, and it was everywhere. By some estimates, it caused as many as one in every four deaths.

A germ causes the disease. It is a bacterium called Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Robert Koch announced the discovery of it in 1882. He later won the Nobel Prize for his work on tuberculosis, in 1905. In theory, it should have ended the vampire panic overnight. Old beliefs die hard, and Mercy Brown was dug up ten years after Koch’s news.

Tuberculosis was so frightening to country people because of how it spread. Modern medicine knows that bacteria cause it. It passes between people who live close together, such as members of one household.

But a 19th-century farmer watching his family die one by one saw something that looked supernatural. When one family member died of consumption, another fell ill. Then another. Then another. It was as if the dead were reaching out of the grave to claim the living.

The farmer was right that the sickness spread within a household. But his explanation was wrong. That mistake was the seed of the vampire panic.

The vampire belief in New England was not the same as the European tradition. It was not like the vampire in Bram Stoker’s Dracula or in Hollywood films. There were no capes, no fangs, no castles in Transylvania, and no fear of garlic or crucifixes. The New England vampire was quieter and more homely. In some ways that made it more frightening.

The folk belief said that when a family member died of consumption, that person’s spirit could drain the life force from living relatives. They would then sicken and die in the same way. One cure was to dig up the body of the suspected vampire and look for certain signs.

One sign was a body that was unusually well kept. People took this as a sign that the dead person was not truly dead. Another sign was liquid blood in the heart or other organs. This was seen as strong proof.

The usual ritual was to burn the heart and liver. The ashes were scattered or drunk. Sometimes the bones of the skeleton were rearranged so that the vampire could not rise.

This was not mindless mob violence. It was a folk ritual carried out with care and real belief. Often the grieving family agreed to it and took part.

Michael Bell argues that the families did not use the word “vampire” for what they were doing. He says the label came mostly from newspapers. Reporters wanted a sensational story. They noticed the likeness between New England rituals and the European vampire legends made popular by Stoker’s novel. It was published in 1897, just five years after the Mercy Brown case.

Bell has studied this subject for decades. He argues that the New England tradition is better seen as a folk medical practice than as a vampire myth. It was a desperate attempt to fight a terrifying disease with the only way of explaining things that people had.

Even the great American naturalist and writer Henry David Thoreau wrote about such a burning in his journal on September 26, 1859. He had read about a Vermont family. Several of them had died of consumption. The family burned the lungs, heart, and liver of the one who died last, to keep the others from getting sick. Thoreau began the entry with the words “The savage in man is never quite eradicated.”

The Mercy Brown case was not the first of the New England vampire exhumations. It was just the most famous. Bell found his earliest case from 1784. Many more probably went unrecorded.

In 1788 and 1789, the family of Justus Forward in Belchertown, Massachusetts, was involved in one of the early cases. In 1817, the father of Frederick Ransom had his son’s body dug up in Woodstock, Vermont. Frederick had died of consumption at age 20. His heart was burned at a blacksmith’s forge. Around 1830, the Corwin family of the same town burned a dead son’s heart on the village green.

Many cases followed a similar pattern. There were several deaths in one family from consumption. Then a dead family member was dug up. The body was inspected, and the heart and other organs were burned.

Perhaps the most important case for archaeology was found around 1990. Some sources say 1990 and others say 1991. Boys were playing near a gravel pit in Griswold, Connecticut. They found an old cemetery. Connecticut state archaeologist Nick Bellantoni studied the site.

He found one burial that had been ritually rearranged. It was called “J.B. 55,” after the brass tacks on the coffin lid. They seem to give his initials and his age. The skull and thighbones had been placed on top of the ribs in a skull-and-crossbones pattern. This seems to have been done years after the burial, which fits the New England vampire ritual. The ribs showed signs of a lung disease, probably tuberculosis. In 2019, DNA work pointed to a farmer named John Barber.

The J.B. 55 skeleton is among the strongest physical evidence of the vampire panic. It suggests that the digging up of graves was not only a tale told by journalists.

The New England vampire panic did not end in one dramatic moment. There was no single discovery or announcement. It ended slowly and unevenly. The germ theory of disease gradually replaced folk explanations in people’s minds.

One big step was Robert Koch’s discovery of the tuberculosis germ in 1882. It gave a scientific explanation for the disease that had terrified rural communities for generations. Once people understood that a bacterium caused consumption, and not the undead, the reason for the vampire rituals fell away.

But the change was not instant. In country areas where folk traditions ran deep and doctors were hard to reach, old beliefs lasted for years. The Mercy Brown case happened ten years after Koch announced the germ. It shows how stubborn folk belief can be.

The panic faded slowly. Bell thinks the practice ended largely because embalming became common after the Civil War. The latest case he found was in Pennsylvania in 1949.

The New England vampire panic is a story about what happens when people face an unseen, unstoppable killer and do not know its cause. It is easy, from the comfort of the 21st century, to call the villagers of Exeter ignorant and superstitious. But they were not stupid.

They saw something real. Tuberculosis deaths were clustering within families. From the evidence they had, they drew a logical but wrong conclusion. The dead person was the only link between the victims. So the dead person must be causing the deaths.

The tragedy is not that people believed in vampires. It is that they had no better choice. George Brown watched his family die one by one. The only remedy his community could offer was to burn his daughter’s heart and mix the ashes into medicine. That he went along with the ritual does not show ignorance. It shows desperation.

The real danger in this story was not the undead. It was a germ that killed silently, in a time when no cure was known.
