The New England Vampire Panic of 1892: When a TB Outbreak Spawned a Vampire Hunt

It was a cold March morning in 1892. A group of men gathered at the Chestnut Hill Cemetery in Exeter, Rhode Island. They had shovels in their hands and dread in their hearts. They had come to dig up the dead.

The graves belonged to the family of George Brown, a modest farmer. He had watched helplessly as consumption took his family. Consumption was a dreaded wasting disease. We now call it tuberculosis. It took his wife Mary Eliza in 1883, his eldest daughter Mary Olive in 1884, and his youngest daughter Mercy Lena just weeks earlier, in January 1892.

Now his only living child, his son Edwin, was gravely ill. He was coughing blood and growing thinner by the day.

The townspeople of Exeter had a theory. It came from generations of folk belief. They thought one of the dead Brown women was a vampire. She was rising from her grave at night to drain the life from her living relatives. The only way to save Edwin was to dig up the bodies, check them for signs of vampirism, and perform the right ritual.

George Brown was exhausted and desperate. He gave his permission.

On March 17, 1892, the men opened the graves. What they found horrified them. The bodies of Mary Eliza and Mary Olive had decayed as expected. But Mercy Lena had been placed in a crypt for only two months, in the cold New England winter. Her body was remarkably well preserved. Her skin still looked lifelike. Her cheeks had color. And when they looked inside her chest, they found liquid blood in her heart and liver.

To the frightened villagers, this was proof. Mercy Brown was a vampire. They removed her heart and liver and burned them on a nearby rock. They mixed the ashes into a medicine for Edwin to drink. It did not work. Edwin died two months later.

The Mercy Brown case is the most famous recorded case of the New England vampire panic. This was a wave of superstitious terror that swept through rural New England from the 1780s to the 1890s. It affected dozens of families, and possibly hundreds.

It was not the work of uneducated, primitive people. These were Yankee farmers, descendants of the Puritans. They lived near universities and churches in one of the most literate regions of the United States.

But their families were dying one by one from a disease they did not understand and could not cure. So they turned to an old folk tradition. It gave the only explanation that made sense to them: the dead were feeding on the living.

The panic was strongest in Rhode Island, eastern Connecticut, southern Massachusetts, Vermont, and other rural parts of New England. Two centuries earlier, this same region had been shaken by the Salem witch trials. That is no accident. In both cases, ignorance, fear, and pressure from neighbors collided. Isolated communities faced threats they could not understand.

To understand the vampire panic, you need to know the disease that caused it. Tuberculosis was called consumption in the 18th and 19th centuries. It was one of the most feared diseases in human history. It was certainly the deadliest in 19th-century America.

It was called consumption because it seemed to eat its victims from the inside out. A healthy person would get a cough that would not go away. Then they would lose weight, grow pale and weak, and sweat through the night. Over months or years they wasted away.

The disease attacked the lungs. Victims coughed up blood. Their voices grew hoarse, their eyes sank, and their skin turned ghostly white. It was a long and terrible way to die, and it was everywhere. In some regions, consumption caused about one in every four deaths.

A germ causes the disease. It is a bacterium called Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Robert Koch identified it in 1882. This discovery earned him the Nobel Prize. In theory, it should have ended the vampire panic overnight. But the news traveled slowly to rural New England, and old beliefs die hard. Even after Koch’s discovery, the digging up of graves went on for another decade.

Tuberculosis was so frightening to country people because of how it spread. Modern medicine knows that bacteria cause it. It passes between people who live close together, such as members of one household.

But a 19th-century farmer watching his family die one by one saw something that looked supernatural. When one family member died of consumption, another fell ill. Then another. Then another. It was as if the dead were reaching out of the grave to claim the living.

The farmer’s observation was medically correct about how it spread in a household. But his explanation was tragically wrong. That mistake was the seed of the vampire panic.

The vampire belief in New England was not the same as the European tradition. It was not like the vampire in Bram Stoker’s Dracula or in Hollywood films. There were no capes, no fangs, no castles in Transylvania, and no fear of garlic or crucifixes. The New England vampire was quieter and more homely. In some ways that made it more frightening.

The folk belief said that when a family member died of consumption, that person’s spirit could drain the life force from living relatives. They would then sicken and die in the same way. The cure was to dig up the body of the suspected vampire and look for certain signs.

One sign was a body that was unusually well kept. The skin might still look lifelike, the cheeks might be rosy, and the limbs might still bend. People took this as a sign that the dead person was not truly dead. They thought the person was keeping a link to the living. The most important sign was liquid blood in the heart or other organs. This was seen as certain proof of vampirism.

The usual ritual was to burn the heart and liver. The ashes were scattered or drunk. Sometimes the bones of the skeleton were rearranged so that the vampire could not rise.

This was not mindless mob violence. It was a folk ritual carried out with care and real belief. Often the grieving family agreed to it and took part.

The people of rural New England did not use the word “vampire” for what they were doing. Outsiders used it later. They were journalists, folklorists, and newspaper reporters who wanted a sensational story. They noticed the likeness between New England rituals and the European vampire legends made popular by Stoker’s novel. It was published in 1897, just five years after the Mercy Brown case.

The New Englanders used phrases such as “the dead feeding on the living.” Or they simply described the ritual without a name.

The folklorist Michael Bell has studied this subject for decades. He argues that the New England tradition is better seen as a folk medical practice than as a vampire myth. It was a desperate attempt to fight a terrifying disease with the only way of explaining things that people had.

Even the great American naturalist and writer Henry David Thoreau wrote about such a digging-up in his journal in March 1859. This shows how widespread and accepted the practice was. Thoreau wrote about a family in a nearby town. They had lost several members to consumption. They dug up the body of the first to die and found it “remarkably fresh.” The heart was burned, and the ashes were given to the surviving family members.

Thoreau usually had little sympathy for superstition. Yet he reported this without mockery. That suggests the practice was common enough that even an educated Harvard man did not find it remarkable.

The Mercy Brown case was not the first or the last of the New England vampire exhumations. It was just the most famous. Folklorists and historians have recorded dozens of similar cases over more than a century. Many more probably went unrecorded.

Around 1819, Nancy Young of Foster, Rhode Island, was dug up and burned after her family suffered several deaths from consumption. In 1854, the Ray family of Jewett City, Connecticut, dug up and burned the bodies of several family members who had died of the disease. In 1874, the Wilbur family of Exeter, Rhode Island, performed a similar ritual. In 1817, Frederick Ransom’s father had his son’s body dug up in South Woodstock, Vermont, and the heart burned.

Each case followed the same pattern. There were several deaths in one family from consumption. Then the most recently dead family member was dug up. The body was inspected, and the heart and other organs were burned in a ritual.

Perhaps the most important case for archaeology was found in 1990. Children were playing near a gravel mine in Griswold, Connecticut. They found an old cemetery from colonial times. Connecticut state archaeologist Nick Bellantoni dug up the site.

He found one burial that had been ritually rearranged. It was called “J.B.,” after the brass tacks on the coffin lid. The head had been removed. The skull and thighbones were placed on top of the ribs and backbone in a skull-and-crossbones pattern. This had been done about five years after death, which fits the New England vampire ritual. The bones belonged to a man of about fifty who died in the 1830s.

The J.B. skeleton is among the strongest physical evidence of the vampire panic. It is archaeological proof that the digging up of graves was not a myth or an exaggeration by journalists. It was a real, practiced ritual with a special signature. The remains are now kept at the Connecticut State Museum of Natural History at the University of Connecticut in Storrs.

In 1896, just four years after the Mercy Brown case, a physician named Dr. William Browning published an article in the Providence Journal. It was titled “Vampirism in New England.” It was one of the first medical comments on the matter. It marks the moment when the medical world began to publicly deal with the folk practice.

Browning understood that bacteria cause tuberculosis. He described the rituals in a calm, scientific way, though he was also fascinated by the culture. He recorded several cases. He noted that the practice was “by no means uncommon” in rural Rhode Island. His article brought the vampire panic to wider public attention. It also began the shift from folk belief to historical curiosity.

The New England vampire panic did not end in one dramatic moment. There was no single discovery or announcement. It ended slowly and unevenly. The germ theory of disease gradually replaced folk explanations in people’s minds.

The turning point was Robert Koch’s discovery of the tuberculosis germ in 1882. It gave a scientific explanation for the disease that had terrified rural communities for generations. Once people understood that a bacterium caused consumption, and not the undead, the reason for the vampire rituals fell away.

But the change was not instant. In country areas where folk traditions ran deep and doctors were hard to reach, old beliefs lasted for years. The Mercy Brown case itself happened ten years after Koch identified the germ. It shows how stubborn folk belief can be, even as science moves forward.

The panic finally faded not through one discovery but through the slow modernizing of rural New England. Roads improved. Doctors became easier to reach. Hospitals and public health campaigns taught communities about the bacteria that cause tuberculosis.

The New England vampire panic is a story about what happens when people face an unseen, unstoppable killer and do not know its cause. It is easy, from the comfort of the 21st century, to call the villagers of Exeter ignorant and superstitious. But they were not stupid.

They saw something real. Tuberculosis deaths were clustering within families. From the evidence they had, they drew a logical but wrong conclusion. The dead person was the only link between the victims. So the dead person must be causing the deaths.

The tragedy is not that people believed in vampires. It is that they had no better choice. George Brown watched his family die one by one. The only remedy his community could offer was to burn his daughter’s heart and mix the ashes into medicine. That he agreed to the ritual does not show ignorance. It shows desperation.

The real monsters in this story were not the undead. They were the bacteria that killed silently. And they were the poverty that left country communities without the medical knowledge that might have saved them.
