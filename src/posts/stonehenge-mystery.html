Stonehenge: The Ancient Monument That Still Defies Explanation

A ring of stones on a windy plain

Imagine that you stand on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England. The sky is huge, and the grass rolls away to every horizon. The wind blows hard. In the middle of all this empty space stands a circle of very old stones.

This is Stonehenge. It is a ring of huge stones called sarsens, with smaller stones called bluestones. People have been fascinated by it for thousands of years. It may be the most famous prehistoric monument on Earth.

The stones are only part of the story. Let us look at how the ring grew, and then at the biggest question of all.

Fifteen hundred years of building

Stonehenge was not built in one go. The work began around 3100 BCE and went on in stages until about 1600 BCE. That is about 1,500 years. Many generations came and went while the ring changed shape.

The first stage was a round space with a ditch and a bank around it. Builders dug the ditch into hard chalk with tools made from deer antlers and animal bones. Nobody had metal tools. It was a huge amount of hand work, so a large community must have worked together.

Inside the bank, the builders dug a ring of 56 pits. They are called the Aubrey Holes. Burned human remains have been found in at least 25 of them. So Stonehenge was a place for the dead, perhaps from the very start.

Next came the bluestones, and then the great sarsens. Where did all these stones come from?

Stones that had to travel

The bluestones weigh about 2 to 5 tons each. They come from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, about 150 miles away. The holes at Stonehenge could hold up to 80 of them, and 43 can still be traced today. Sources give different dates for when they were set up. Some say about 2400 BCE. They may have stood somewhere else first.

The sarsens are even bigger. Each is a block of hard sandstone that weighs about 25 tons. They came from near the Marlborough Downs, about 15 to 20 miles to the north. The builders shaped them with hammerstones. A hammerstone is a round stone that you use to pound and smooth rock. Then they raised the sarsens around 2600 to 2400 BCE.

Some sarsens carry flat stones across the top, called lintels. The builders joined them with a woodworking trick. A peg on one stone fits into a hole in the other. The tallest set of two uprights and one lintel stood about 24 feet high.

How did people with no metal tools move such weights? Nobody knows exactly. Experiments show that a large team can pull a stone this size on a wooden sledge. But that only starts the answer.

Did ice carry the bluestones?

Here is an old idea. Some researchers once said that glaciers carried the bluestones from Wales during the last Ice Age. Then nobody would have had to move them at all.

But this idea has a problem. In 2019, diggers found two quarries in the Preseli Hills. People cut stones there around 3000 BCE. So it seems that people took the stones, and ice did not. Some think the stones went overland on sledges. Others think boats helped.

The ice idea is old, and it is not supported today. But the stones still had surprises left.

A stone from far to the north

The Altar Stone lies flat inside the monument. People long thought that it came from Wales, like the bluestones. Then in 2024, a team of geologists tested tiny crystals in it called zircons. They also tested the chemistry of the rock. They said it matched rock in the Orcadian Basin in northeast Scotland.

If that is right, the stone traveled more than 430 miles, or 700 kilometers. The team thinks that it went by ship along the coast, because a trip over land would have been very hard.

But a later study did not find a match with rocks there. So the true home of the Altar Stone is argued about again. This puzzle shows how much is still open. And it shows something else. The people of Stonehenge may have been in touch with distant places.

Visitors, a village, and “Superhenge”

In 2002, archaeologists found a rich grave near Stonehenge. It belonged to a man now called the Amesbury Archer. He was buried about 2300 BCE. His grave held gold hair ornaments, the oldest gold objects known from England. It also held three small copper knives.

Scientists studied his teeth. Teeth keep chemical marks from the water and food of childhood. They showed that he grew up in central Europe, probably near the Alps. So a man from far away was buried near the stones.

Two miles northeast lies Durrington Walls, a huge round earthwork about 1,600 feet across. Some archaeologists think it was the village of the builders.

From 2010 to 2014, the Hidden Landscapes Project looked under the grass with radar and other tools, without digging. In 2015, a survey seemed to show a row of up to 90 buried stones at Durrington Walls. People called it “Superhenge.” But in 2016, diggers found no stones there. They found holes where timber posts once stood.

Land of the living, land of the dead

What was Stonehenge for? People have argued for centuries, and no one idea has won everyone over.

One well-known idea comes from the archaeologist Mike Parker Pearson. He says that Durrington Walls, with its timber circles, was the domain of the living. Stonehenge, with its stones and burials, was the domain of the dead. The River Avon linked the two. People may have walked from the wooden place of the living to the stone place of the ancestors.

A second idea looks at the sky. The solstice is the day with the longest or the shortest daylight. English Heritage says that Stonehenge lines up with the sun at the solstices. At the summer solstice, the sun rises in the northeast. At the winter solstice, it sets in the southwest. In the 1960s, the astronomer Gerald Hawkins said that the stones could follow the sun and moon over 56 years. Many archaeologists doubt his bigger claims.

A third idea is healing. The archaeologists Geoffrey Wainwright and Timothy Darvill proposed that sick people came to the stones to get well.

What we know, and what we still ask

In 1986, the stones and the land around them became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Today, many people still gather there for the summer solstice sunrise.

Let us say plainly what we know. We know roughly when Stonehenge was built. We know where most of the stones came from. The sarsens came from near the Marlborough Downs, and the bluestones came from Wales. We know that hammerstones shaped them. And we know that the builders were people of the New Stone Age, who had no writing and no metal tools.

We do not know why. Was it a graveyard, a calendar, a place of healing, or all of these? Nobody can say. Perhaps the answer changed as each new generation came back to the stones.

So go back to the wind on Salisbury Plain. The ring still stands there, and it keeps its secret.
