Stonehenge: The Ancient Monument That Still Defies Explanation

On Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England, the sky is huge and the grassland rolls to every horizon. The wind blows hard. In the middle of it stands a circle of ancient stones. People have been fascinated by it for thousands of years.

This is Stonehenge. It is a ring of huge stones called sarsens, with smaller stones called bluestones. The main line of the monument matches the movements of the sun at the solstices. People had used this land long before the stones went up. It may be the most famous prehistoric monument on Earth.

Stonehenge was built in stages over about 1,500 years. The work began around 3100 BCE. It went through many phases of building, change, and reuse until around 1600 BCE. The builders had no writing and no metal tools. Yet they raised stones weighing about 25 tons or more. They moved the bluestones about 150 miles from Wales. They shaped the stones with stone hammers and raised them into forms that have lasted five thousand years in British weather. Why they did it, and how, is a great puzzle of the ancient world.

The land around Stonehenge is itself a giant monument. Archaeologists call it the Stonehenge Landscape. It is a huge ritual area on Salisbury Plain. It holds many ancient features. There are burial mounds, ceremonial roads, earth banks and ditches, and the remains of old villages.

The Stonehenge Avenue is a ceremonial path with a ditch along each side. It joins the stone circle to the River Avon about two miles away. The Stonehenge Cursus is a huge rectangular earthwork about 1.7 miles long. It is older than the stone circle, and no one knows what it was for.

Durrington Walls lies about two miles northeast of Stonehenge. It is a huge round earthwork about 1,600 feet across. Some archaeologists think it was the village where the builders of Stonehenge lived while they worked. Many burial mounds dot the nearby ridges. In 1986, the whole landscape was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Stonehenge was built in several phases. Stonehenge 1 (about 3100 BCE) was the first. It was a round enclosure with a ditch and a bank. Inside it, builders dug a ring of 56 pits called the Aubrey Holes. They may have held wooden posts or bluestones. Burned human remains have been found in at least 25 of the holes. So Stonehenge was a burial place for a very long time.

The ditch was dug into the chalk rock with tools made from deer antlers and animal bones. That was a huge amount of hand work. It needed a large community working together.

Next came the bluestones. The holes in the monument could hold up to 80 of them, and 43 can still be traced today. Each weighs about 2 to 5 tons. They came from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, about 150 miles away. Sources give different dates for when they were set up. Some say about 2400 BCE, and the stones may have stood somewhere else before. How did people of the New Stone Age move them so far? It is still a big question in archaeology.

The great sarsen stones came about 2600 to 2400 BCE. They were pulled from the area of the Marlborough Downs, about 15 to 20 miles to the north. Each is a block of hard sandstone weighing about 25 tons. The builders shaped them into upright stones and flat stones that lie across the top, called lintels.

The famous trilithon is made of two uprights capped by one lintel. The pieces are joined with mortise-and-tenon joints, a woodworking trick in which a peg on one piece fits a hole in another. The tallest trilithon stood about 24 feet high. Later phases moved the bluestones around and made other changes. The site kept changing for more than a thousand years.

How the work was done is almost as mysterious as why. Experiments show that a stone of this size can be pulled on a wooden sledge by a large team of people. Nobody knows exactly how the builders did it.

The bluestones were an even bigger challenge. Some researchers once said that glaciers carried them from Wales during the last Ice Age. But in 2019, diggers found two quarries in the Preseli Hills. People cut stones there around 3000 BCE. This supports the idea that people moved the stones. Some think they went overland on sledges. Others think boats helped. The builders shaped the stones with hammerstones, which are round stones used to pound and smooth rock.

A discovery in 2024 added a surprising twist. The Altar Stone is a flat sandstone that lies inside the monument. People had long thought that it came from Wales, like the bluestones. But a team of geologists tested tiny crystals in it called zircons, and the chemistry of the rock. They said it matched rock in the Orcadian Basin in northeast Scotland.

If that is right, the Altar Stone traveled more than 430 miles (700 kilometers) from northern Scotland to Salisbury Plain. The team thinks it went by ship along the coast, because a trip over land would have been very hard. They say it points to travel and trade links along Great Britain. But a later study did not find a match with rocks there. So the true home of the Altar Stone is argued about again.

In 2002, archaeologists found one of the most important graves near Stonehenge. It belonged to a man now called the Amesbury Archer. He was buried about 2300 BCE. His grave was rich. It held gold hair ornaments, the oldest gold objects known from England. It also held three small copper knives and other tools.

Scientists studied his teeth with isotope analysis, which reads the chemical marks left by the water and food of a person's childhood. It showed that he grew up not in Britain, but in central Europe, probably near the Alps. His presence suggests that Stonehenge was part of a web of links across Europe.

From 2010 to 2014, the Stonehenge Hidden Landscapes Project looked at the land around Stonehenge. It was a team effort of several European research groups. They used ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry, and other remote sensing tools. These see below the ground without digging even one trench.

The results were astonishing. The team found a large network of unknown ancient features. All had lain hidden under the grass for thousands of years. One find caused big headlines. In 2015, a survey seemed to show a row of up to 90 buried standing stones at Durrington Walls. People nicknamed it "Superhenge."

But in 2016, diggers found no stones there. Instead, they found a ring of huge holes for timber posts. The posts were later pulled out and the holes were filled with chalk.

The project showed that Stonehenge was not alone. It was part of a huge, busy ritual landscape far more complex than people had thought.

What was Stonehenge for? People have argued for centuries, and no one theory has won everyone over. One well-known modern idea came from the Stonehenge Riverside Project. It was led by Mike Parker Pearson.

His team said Stonehenge belonged to a pair of ceremonial sites. Durrington Walls had timber circles. They saw it as the "domain of the living." Stonehenge had a stone circle and burials of burned remains. They saw it as the "domain of the dead." The River Avon linked the two sites. People may have walked from the wooden place of the living to the stone place of the ancestors.

Other theories look at Stonehenge's astronomy. English Heritage says the monument lines up with the sun at the solstices. At the summer solstice, the sun rises behind the Heel Stone in the northeast. At the winter solstice, the sun sets to the southwest. The solstice is the day of the year with the longest or the shortest daylight.

In the 1960s, astronomer Gerald Hawkins argued that Stonehenge could be used to follow the sun and moon over a cycle of 56 years. Many archaeologists doubt his bigger claims. But the solstice lines are widely accepted, and they are still one of the site's best features.

Each year, thousands of people gather at Stonehenge for the summer solstice sunrise. Big midwinter gatherings at nearby Durrington Walls suggest that people met at the solstices long ago too.

Some researchers say Stonehenge was a healing center. Archaeologists Geoffrey Wainwright and Timothy Darvill proposed that people came to the stones to be healed. The truth may be that Stonehenge had many uses in its 1,500 years. It may have been a graveyard, a calendar, a ceremonial center, a healing place, and a monument to ancestors. Its meanings may have changed as each new generation came back to the stones.

People have studied, measured, dug, photographed, scanned, and analyzed Stonehenge for hundreds of years. It still has not given a final answer. We know roughly when it was built. We know where most of the stones came from: the sarsens from near the Marlborough Downs and the bluestones from Wales. We know how they were shaped, with hammerstones. We even have a good idea of who built it. They were communities of the New Stone Age, linked across hundreds of miles.

But we still do not know why. Was it a graveyard? A calendar? A temple? A show of power? A healing center? Perhaps it was all of these, and more. Perhaps Stonehenge still fascinates us, five thousand years after the first ditch was cut into the chalk, because it does not have just one answer. It has many. It means something different to each generation that stands before it. The builders made more than a circle of stones. They made a mirror in which every age sees itself.
