The Lost City of Heracleion: An Ancient Egyptian Metropolis Hidden Beneath the Sea for 1,200 Years

For more than a thousand years, it was only a whisper in old books. It was a rich port city at the mouth of the Nile in Egypt. The Greek writer Herodotus mentioned it in the fifth century BCE. The geographer Strabo described it four centuries later. But no one had ever found it. There were no ruins and no traces. Nothing showed that it had ever existed.

The Egyptians called the city Thonis. The Greeks called it Heracleion. Scholars began to wonder if Thonis-Heracleion was only a legend, a story made up by ancient writers.

Then, in 2000, a French underwater archaeologist named Franck Goddio made a find. He worked with his team at the European Institute for Underwater Archaeology (IEASM), together with the Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities. The ruins lay about 6.5 kilometers off the coast, in Abu Qir Bay, near Alexandria. They lay under about 10 meters of Mediterranean water. It was a whole ancient city, with temples, harbors, statues, ships, and thousands of objects. The mud of the Nile delta had kept them for more than twelve centuries. The lost city of Heracleion was real. And it was spectacular.

The find matters greatly. <a href="/posts/alexander-great-tomb">Alexander the Great</a> founded Alexandria in 331 BCE. Before that, Thonis-Heracleion was Egypt’s main port on the Mediterranean. Every ship from the Greek world had to stop there first. It was a busy city where Egyptian and Greek peoples met. They traded goods, ideas, and religious customs.

The city was probably founded around the 8th century BCE. For centuries it thrived as a center of trade and worship. Its Temple of Amun-Gereb was one of the most important holy places in Egypt in its late period. It played a central part in the rites by which pharaohs passed on their rule.

Stories about the city’s beginnings credited Heracles (Hercules) himself. He was said to have first set foot in Egypt at this very spot. Herodotus also wrote that Helen of Troy and her lover Paris visited Heracleion before the Trojan War. They hid in the temple during a storm. So the city was more than a trading post. It was a sacred place, a gateway between the peoples of the Mediterranean and the kingdom of the pharaohs.

The search began in 1996. Goddio and his team started a careful survey of the western part of Abu Qir Bay, northeast of Alexandria. Goddio had once been a business executive. He left that world to study underwater archaeology. He was already known for finding sunken ships and drowned lands in the Philippines and elsewhere.

This was his biggest project. The team wanted to map and explore about 11 by 15 kilometers of seabed. They used the best tools of the time. These included side-scan sonar, which uses sound to make pictures of the sea floor. They also used magnetometers, which sense metal and buried objects, and sub-bottom profilers, which look beneath the seabed. They also used nuclear magnetic resonance magnetometers. The team worked slowly and made a detailed map of the underwater land.

For four years the work was hard and often disappointing. Centuries of mud covered the seabed, and the ruins lay under layers of clay and sand.

The breakthrough came in 2000. The team improved its surveys and began to dig in chosen places. They started to find clear signs of an ancient city. First came pieces of pottery and building material. Then came larger things. There were the bases of buildings, parts of walls, and at last the huge stone blocks of a great temple. The size of the find was amazing.

One of the first dramatic finds was a huge red granite statue of Hapy. He was the Egyptian god of the Nile’s yearly flood. The statue stood about 5.4 meters (over 17 feet) tall. It had lain unseen on the sea floor for more than a thousand years.

A black granite stele, an upright carved stone slab, settled the question. It had writing in Egyptian hieroglyphs, which are picture signs, and in Greek. It proved the city was Thonis-Heracleion. Another important find was a large stele of the pharaoh Nectanebo I (about 378 to 362 BCE), perfectly kept. It named the town by both its Egyptian name, Thonis, and its Greek name, Heracleion. That was the final proof that the city of the old books and the ruins under the sea were one and the same.

Thonis-Heracleion was not a small fishing village. It was a major port and a great market. For centuries it was Egypt’s main gateway to the Mediterranean. It stood on a group of islands side by side in the Nile Delta. Canals and waterways cut through the city. It had many harbors and places to anchor.

Its docks, temples, and tower-houses were linked by ferries, bridges, and floating bridges. The watery town looks somewhat like medieval Venice. At its heart stood the great temple of Amun-Gereb. He was the chief god of the Egyptians in the Late Period.

King Nectanebo I added many new parts to this huge temple complex in the fourth century BCE. Smaller shrines surrounded it. They honored Osiris, Khonsu, and other gods. The Greeks called Khonsu Herakles, which gave the city its Greek name. The remains show that the shrines at Heracleion were famous across the ancient world. People said they brought miraculous healing, and pilgrims came from all over the Mediterranean.

One of the most remarkable finds showed a yearly celebration, the Mysteries of Osiris. It was one of Egypt’s most important religious festivals. Each year in the month of Khoiak, about October to November, a statue of the god Osiris rode in a ceremonial boat called a barque. A great procession carried him from the temple of Amun at Heracleion to his shrine at Canopus. The rite acted out the myth of Osiris’s death and return to life. People believed it made sure that the Nile would flood each year and the land would be fertile.

Archaeologists found model barques. These were small ceremonial boats made of lead, copper alloy, and wood, with fine decoration. They were left as gifts to the gods during these processions.

Greek soldiers for hire were used to defend the city as early as the Twenty-sixth Dynasty. Recent finds suggest that Greeks began to settle in the city well before the Ptolemaic period. The city was a cultural bridge between Egypt and Greece. Its temples and rites mixed parts of both traditions.

The loss of Thonis-Heracleion is one of the most dramatic cases of a city destroyed by nature in the ancient world. It did not fall in one night. It was destroyed slowly over several centuries by earthquakes, huge waves called tsunamis, rising seas, and soil liquefaction. That is when wet ground suddenly acts like a liquid.

The key event happened around 101 BCE. A severe flood, probably set off by an earthquake, made the ground under the city’s central island liquefy. The hard clay under Heracleion suddenly turned liquid. The buildings, temples, and monuments fell into the water.

The city was already in decline. Alexandria, founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BCE, stood about 15 miles to the southwest. It had slowly outshone Heracleion as Egypt’s main port. By the second century BCE, most foreign trade had moved to Alexandria, and Heracleion was fading. A few people clung to the remains during Roman times and the start of Arab rule. But by the end of the eighth century CE, what was left of Thonis-Heracleion had sunk for good beneath the Mediterranean.

The underwater digs have brought up many treasures. There are huge stone statues of pharaohs, queens, and gods. Some stand over 5 meters tall, and they are wonderfully preserved beneath the sea floor. They show the grand scale of the city’s temples. There is gold jewelry, including rings, earrings, and pendants made with great skill. It hints at the wealth of the people and the fine goods that came through the ports.

Hundreds of stone anchors of different sizes and designs match Egyptian, Greek, and Phoenician ships. They prove the many trade routes that met at Heracleion. Most surprising of all, seventy-two ancient shipwrecks have been found in the harbor area. That is one of the largest groups of ancient ships ever found. The ships carried amphorae, which are tall clay jars, from all over the Mediterranean. There were Greek wine jars, Phoenician storage jars, and Egyptian containers. Together they paint a lively picture of a busy international port.

Greek historians wove the city into some of the most famous legends of the ancient world. Herodotus told how Paris and Helen of Troy sought refuge at the Canopic mouth of the Nile. Paris’s servants fled to a nearby temple. The watchman Thonis captured their ships. Diodorus Siculus linked the city to the legend of Heracles. He said that the hero had tamed a terrible flood of the Nile and put the river back in its proper course.

People once dismissed these legends as pure myth. After the discovery, they gained new meaning. A major port at the Canopic mouth of the Nile, with temples to Heracles and other Greek gods, gave a believable base for stories handed down for thousands of years.

Today the dig at Thonis-Heracleion goes on. Only a small part of the site has been fully explored, and each diving season brings new surprises. Many of the best objects have been shown in big museum exhibitions around the world. These include the British Museum in London and the Institut du Monde Arabe in Paris. The sand and debris that covered the ruins for more than a thousand years kept them in remarkably good shape. They protected the site from looters and from the damage that comes with open air.

The story of Heracleion reminds us that the Mediterranean sea floor still holds great secrets. Sometimes the best finds lie beneath the waves, and not on land. A city that was lost for 1,200 years is now telling its story. We are only beginning to listen.
