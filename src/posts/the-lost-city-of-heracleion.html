The Lost City of Heracleion: An Ancient Egyptian Metropolis Hidden Beneath the Sea for 1,200 Years

For more than a thousand years, it was only a whisper in old books. It was a rich port city at the mouth of the Nile in Egypt. The Greek writer Herodotus mentioned it in the fifth century BCE. The geographer Strabo described it four centuries later. But no one had ever found it. There were no ruins and no traces. Nothing showed that it had ever existed.

The Egyptians called the city Thonis. The Greeks called it Heracleion. Some scholars began to wonder if Thonis-Heracleion was only a legend, a story made up by ancient writers.

Then, in 2000, a French underwater archaeologist named Franck Goddio made a find. He worked with his team at the European Institute for Underwater Archaeology (IEASM), together with the Egyptian antiquities authorities. The ruins lay about 6.5 kilometers off the coast, in Abu Qir Bay, near Alexandria. They lay under about 10 meters of Mediterranean water. It was a whole ancient city, with temples, harbors, statues, ships, and thousands of objects. The mud of the Nile delta had kept them for more than a thousand years. The lost city of Heracleion was real. And it was spectacular.

The find matters greatly. Alexander the Great founded Alexandria in 331 BCE. Before that, Thonis-Heracleion was Egypt’s main port on the Mediterranean. Foreign ships often stopped there first. It was a busy city where Egyptian and Greek peoples met. They traded goods, ideas, and religious customs.

The city was probably founded around the 8th century BCE. For centuries it thrived as a center of trade and worship. Its Temple of Amun-Gereb was the city’s main holy place.

Stories about the city’s beginnings credited Heracles (Hercules) himself. He was said to have first set foot in Egypt at this very spot. Herodotus also wrote that Paris and Helen of Troy came to the Canopic mouth of the Nile on their way from Greece. So the city was more than a trading post. It was a sacred place, a gateway between the peoples of the Mediterranean and the kingdom of the pharaohs.

The search began in 1996. Goddio and his team started a careful survey of the western part of Abu Qir Bay, northeast of Alexandria. Goddio was already known for underwater digs in the Philippines and elsewhere.

This was his biggest project. The team wanted to map and explore about 11 by 15 kilometers of seabed. They used modern tools. These included side-scan sonar, which uses sound to make pictures of the sea floor. They also used magnetometers, which sense metal and buried objects, and sub-bottom profilers, which look beneath the seabed. The team worked slowly and made a detailed map of the underwater land.

For four years the work was hard and often disappointing. Centuries of mud covered the seabed, and the ruins lay under layers of clay and sand.

The breakthrough came in 2000. The team improved its surveys and began to dig in chosen places. They started to find clear signs of an ancient city. First came pieces of pottery and building material. Then came larger things. There were the bases of buildings, parts of walls, and at last the huge stone blocks of a great temple. The size of the find was amazing.

One of the great finds was a huge red granite statue of Hapy. He was the Egyptian god of the Nile’s yearly flood. The statue stood about 5.4 meters (over 17 feet) tall. It had lain unseen on the sea floor for more than a thousand years.

A stele, an upright carved stone slab, helped settle the question. Writing on stelae found at the site showed that Thonis and Heracleion were two names for one city. One of them was a large, well-kept stele of the pharaoh Nectanebo I, who ruled from about 380 to 362 BCE. So the city of the old books and the ruins under the sea were one and the same.

Thonis-Heracleion was not a small fishing village. It was a major port and a great market. For centuries it was Egypt’s main gateway to the Mediterranean. It stood on a group of islands side by side in the Nile Delta. Canals and waterways cut through the city. It had many harbors and places to anchor.

Its docks and temples sat beside the canals. Some experts compare the watery town to Venice. At its heart stood the great temple of Amun-Gereb. He was the chief god of the Egyptians in the Late Period.

King Nectanebo I added many new parts to this huge temple complex in the fourth century BCE. Smaller shrines surrounded it. They honored Osiris, Khonsu, and other gods. The Greeks called Khonsu Herakles, which gave the city its Greek name.

One of the most remarkable finds showed a yearly celebration, the Mysteries of Osiris. It was one of Egypt’s most important religious festivals. Each year in the month of Khoiak, about October to November, a statue of the god Osiris rode in a ceremonial boat called a barque. A great procession carried him from the temple of Amun at Heracleion to his shrine at Canopus. The rite acted out the myth of Osiris’s death and return to life.

Archaeologists found many small lead boats, called model barques, in the canals near the temple. They were probably left as gifts to the gods during these rites.

Finds such as Greek writing and Athenian weights show that Greeks traded there. The city was a cultural bridge between Egypt and Greece. Its temples and rites mixed parts of both traditions.

The loss of Thonis-Heracleion was a dramatic case of a city taken by nature. It did not fall in one night. It sank slowly over several centuries. Earthquakes, huge waves called tsunamis, rising seas, and soil liquefaction all played a part. That is when wet ground suddenly acts like a liquid.

Some studies point to around 101 BCE as a key moment. The ground under part of the city may have turned liquid. Buildings, temples, and monuments then fell into the water. Experts still study exactly how and when this happened. The great temple itself was wrecked around 140 BCE.

The city was already in decline. Alexandria, founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BCE, stood about 30 kilometers to the southwest. In the second century BCE it took over from Heracleion as Egypt’s main port. By the end of the eighth century CE, what was left of Thonis-Heracleion had sunk for good beneath the Mediterranean.

The underwater digs have brought up many treasures. There are huge stone statues of pharaohs, queens, and gods. Some stand over 5 meters tall, and they are wonderfully preserved beneath the sea floor. They show the grand scale of the city’s temples. They also show the wealth of the city and the goods that came through its ports.

More than 700 stone anchors of many shapes have been found. They point to the many ships that came to Heracleion. Most surprising of all are the ancient shipwrecks in the harbor area. Reports give different numbers, from about 70 to 125, because the count grew as the digging went on. It is one of the largest groups of ancient ships ever found. Many ships carried amphorae, which are tall clay jars. Together the finds paint a lively picture of a busy international port.

Greek historians wove the city into some of the most famous legends of the ancient world. Herodotus told how winds drove Paris and Helen of Troy to the Canopic mouth of the Nile. Paris’s servants fled to a nearby temple of Heracles. A man named Thonis, who guarded the river mouth, sent word to the king of Egypt. Diodorus Siculus also linked Heracles to the Nile in his old stories.

These are legends, not history. But the discovery gave them a real setting. A major port at the Canopic mouth of the Nile, with a temple linked to Heracles, fits the old stories.

Today the dig at Thonis-Heracleion goes on. Only a small part of the site has been fully explored, and each diving season brings new surprises. Many of the best objects have been shown in museum exhibitions around the world. The sand and mud that covered the ruins for more than a thousand years helped keep them in good shape.

The story of Heracleion reminds us that the Mediterranean sea floor still holds great secrets. Sometimes the best finds lie beneath the waves, and not on land. A city that was lost for more than a thousand years is now telling its story. We are only beginning to listen.
