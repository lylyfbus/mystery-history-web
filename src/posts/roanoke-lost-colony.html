The Lost Colony of Roanoke: America's Oldest Unsolved Mystery and the Word Carved in Wood

It was August 1590. An English governor named John White stepped off a ship onto the sandy shore of Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina. He was looking for his daughter, his granddaughter, and the 115 other English colonists he had left behind three years before.

He found nothing. The settlement was empty. The houses had been taken apart. There were no bodies and no signs of violence or of a fight.

There were only two carvings. The word “CROATOAN” was cut into a wooden post at the entrance of the palisade, which is a fence made of tall stakes. On a nearby tree, someone had carved the letters “CRO.”

White knew what CROATOAN meant. It was the name of a nearby island. A Croatan chief named Manteo lived there, and he had made friends with the English. It was also a secret signal agreed upon beforehand. If the colonists had to leave, they would carve their new destination on a tree.

But before White could search Croatoan Island, a fierce storm pushed his ships out to sea. He never returned.

The Lost Colony of Roanoke was made up of 117 men, women, and children. They vanished from the edge of the known world. Their fate has been unsolved for more than four centuries. It is America’s oldest cold case. Even with modern archaeology, DNA tests, and satellite pictures, we still do not know for sure what happened.

The story of the Lost Colony is more than a mystery. It is the founding legend of English America. It is a tale of big dreams, pride, bad luck, and the hard facts of life in a new colony in the sixteenth century.

It includes Sir Walter Raleigh’s dream of a New World empire. It includes the first English child born on American soil. It includes a war with Spain that left the colonists without supplies for three years. And it includes a single carved word that has led to a thousand theories.

New research has brought us closer to an answer than ever. It includes a hidden mark found on a sixteenth-century map, and DNA tests on possible descendants. But “closer” is not the same as “certain.” The Lost Colony still keeps its secrets, in the same stubborn silence that met John White on that August day in 1590.

The Roanoke plan was the idea of Sir Walter Raleigh. He was a charming courtier, an explorer, and a favorite of Queen Elizabeth I. In 1584, he received a royal patent. This was a paper that allowed him to set up a colony in North America. He did not waste time.

He sent a scouting trip led by Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe. They explored the Outer Banks, part of what is now North Carolina. They came back with glowing reports. They described a rich, green land with friendly Native Americans.

The Queen was so pleased that she named the whole region “Virginia.” She did this in honor of her unmarried status.

The first real attempt to settle came in 1585. Raleigh sent about 108 men to Roanoke Island, under the command of Ralph Lane. It was a disaster from the start. The settlers were mostly soldiers, not farmers. They arrived too late in the year to plant crops.

They relied on the local Algonquian peoples for food. That soon turned into hostility, when the English demanded food at sword-point.

In June 1586, the fleet of Sir Francis Drake arrived. It had just raided Spanish towns in the Caribbean. The hungry colonists begged to be taken home. Lane reluctantly agreed. The first Roanoke colony had lasted less than a year.

Raleigh did not give up. In 1587, he planned a second trip, with a new idea. This time he picked families instead of soldiers. They were men, women, and children who would build a lasting farming settlement.

The leader was John White. He was an artist and mapmaker. He had gone on the first voyage and knew the area well.

White’s group had 117 settlers. They included his daughter, Eleanor White Dare, who was expecting a baby, and her husband, Ananias Dare. The plan was to settle on Chesapeake Bay, a better place to the north. But the ship’s pilot would not sail past Roanoke. He seems to have been eager to start a privateering mission against Spanish ships. Privateers were armed ships that raided enemy vessels. White and the colonists were stuck on Roanoke Island against their will.

On August 18, 1587, just weeks after they landed, Eleanor Dare gave birth to a daughter, Virginia Dare. She was the first English child born in the Americas. Her name became part of American folklore as a symbol of the Lost Colony.

She was about three years old when John White came back and found the colony empty. Her fate is the emotional heart of the mystery. Did she die on Roanoke? Did she die at sea? Did she grow up among Native Americans? Or did she live and die without anyone noticing?

John White sailed back to England in late August 1587, only weeks after Virginia’s birth. He wanted more supplies and more recruits for the struggling colony. He planned to return within months. He did not come back for three years.

The reason was war. England and Spain went to war in 1588. Queen Elizabeth ordered every ship held at home to defend against the Spanish Armada. No ships were free to carry supplies to the colony.

White managed to get two small ships for a rescue trip in 1588. But French pirates attacked them off the coast of France, and they had to turn back. It was March 1590 before White got a place on a privateering trip heading to the Caribbean. The captains agreed to stop at Roanoke on the way home.

White finally reached Roanoke Island on August 18, 1590. It was his granddaughter’s third birthday. The settlement had been left carefully and on purpose. The colonists had taken apart their houses and carried off the building materials. A palisade stood around the site.

The word “CROATOAN” was carved in big, clear letters on one of the palisade posts. On a tree near the entrance, the letters “CRO” could still be seen.

White later wrote that he was “greatly joyed” to see the word CROATOAN. Before he left in 1587, he had told the colonists what to do. If they had to leave, they should carve the name of their destination. If they were leaving in danger, they should add a cross above the name.

There was no cross above CROATOAN. So it seemed the colonists had left of their own free will. They had gone to Croatoan Island, today’s Hatteras Island, to live with Manteo’s people.

Yet White never reached Croatoan Island. In his own account, the ship captains wanted to sail south first. They had seen what looked like a wreck, and it might have goods worth saving. Then a fierce storm blew up. It damaged one of the ships. The expedition gave up the search and went back to the Caribbean.

White was heartbroken. He wrote that he would have searched Croatoan “with all the means I could” if the weather and the captains had allowed. But the chance was gone. White was now an old man in poor health, and he never returned to America. He died in England sometime after 1593. He never learned what had happened to his daughter, his son-in-law, or his granddaughter.

For centuries, the search for the Lost Colony was mostly guesswork. That changed in 2012. Researchers from the First Colony Foundation looked at a sixteenth-century map made by John White himself. It is called the “La Virginea Pars” map, and it is kept in the British Museum.

Two small patches of paper had been glued over the map. Underneath, the researchers found a hidden mark. It was a four-pointed star, or diamond shape. It marked a spot at the head of the Albemarle Sound, near today’s Edenton, North Carolina. In sixteenth-century maps, such marks usually meant a fort.

The researchers called the place “Site X.” This suggested that Raleigh had a backup plan. There may have been a second, secret place where the colonists could go if Roanoke did not work out.

In 2015, archaeologists from the First Colony Foundation began digging at Site X. They found what they had hoped for. There were English objects from the late sixteenth century. They included pieces of Surrey-Hampshire border ware, a kind of English pottery, and a copper aglet, the metal tip of a lace used in Elizabethan clothes.

There were also other items that fit with Roanoke-era colonists. The objects lay next to Native American pottery. This suggests that the English and the Algonquian peoples lived together or traded.

The most widely supported theory fits the CROATOAN carving. It also fits later reports from the Jamestown settlers. They heard stories of Englishmen living among Native American communities to the south and west. The theory says the colonists moved inland in two or more groups. Little by little, they joined local Native American communities.

Some may have died of disease or violence. Some may have lived long enough to have children. Those children may have grown up speaking Algonquian, not English. Their European roots may have shown only in their faces and surnames.

Since 2005, researchers with the Lost Colony DNA Projects have tried to find descendants through genetic genealogy. This means using DNA and family records to trace family lines. The most promising lead is the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina. This Native American community is centered in Robeson County. Many Lumbee people have European looks and surnames that match those of the Lost Colonists.

DNA tests show that some Lumbee men carry a European Y-chromosome type that fits descent from English male settlers. The Y chromosome is passed from father to son. But proving a link to the Lost Colony in particular is still very hard.

The Lost Colony of Roanoke is a mystery that will not die. The reason is not that the answer cannot be known. It is that the question lies so deep in the American mind. Solving it would feel like losing something important.

Think of John White standing in an empty clearing and staring at a single carved word. It is the founding image of English America. He came looking for his family and found only silence.

The most likely answer fits the CROATOAN carving, the Site X digs, and the Jamestown reports. The colonists moved inland. Little by little, they joined local Native American communities. They did so out of need, not conquest.

The final answer may come from DNA. A genetic link between a modern Lumbee family and a particular colonist could close a 430-year-old question.

Until then, the Lost Colony remains what it has always been. It is a question carved into a post, a word on a tree, and a silence that has lasted four centuries. They came. They settled. They vanished. And America has been trying to find them ever since.
