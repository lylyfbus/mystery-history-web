The Lost Colony of Roanoke: America's Oldest Unsolved Mystery and the Word Carved in Wood

A word cut into a post

It was August 1590. An English governor named John White stepped off a ship onto the sandy shore of Roanoke Island, in what is now North Carolina. He was looking for his daughter, his granddaughter, and the other English colonists he had left behind three years before.

He found nothing. The settlement was empty. The houses had been taken apart. White found no bodies and no signs of a fight.

There were only two carvings. The word “CROATOAN” was cut into a post at the entrance of the palisade, which is a fence made of tall stakes. On a nearby tree, someone had carved the letters “CRO.”

About 115 men, women, and children had lived here. Sources give numbers from 115 to 117. Where had they gone? Let us follow the story from the start, and see what White knew.

A dream of a New World

The plan was the idea of Sir Walter Raleigh. He was a charming courtier, an explorer, and a favorite of Queen Elizabeth I. In 1584, he received a royal patent. This was a paper that allowed him to set up a colony in North America.

He sent a scouting trip led by Philip Amadas and Arthur Barlowe. They explored the Outer Banks, part of what is now North Carolina. They came back with glowing reports. They described a rich, green land with friendly Native Americans.

The Queen was so pleased that the whole region was named “Virginia.” A common story says this honored her, the “Virgin Queen.”

The first real attempt to settle came in 1585. Raleigh sent about 108 men to Roanoke Island, under the command of Ralph Lane. It went badly from the start. The settlers included many soldiers, not only farmers. They arrived too late in the year to plant crops.

They relied on the local Algonquian peoples for food. That soon turned into hostility, when the English demanded food at sword-point. In June 1586, the fleet of Sir Francis Drake arrived. The hungry colonists begged to be taken home, and Lane agreed. The first colony had lasted less than a year.

So Raleigh tried again, with a new idea.

Families come to Roanoke

In 1587, Raleigh picked families instead of soldiers. They were men, women, and children who would build a lasting farming settlement. The leader was John White. He was an artist and mapmaker, and he had gone on the first voyage.

White’s group had about 115 settlers. They included his daughter, Eleanor White Dare, who was expecting a baby, and her husband, Ananias Dare. The plan was to settle on Chesapeake Bay, a better place to the north. But the ship’s pilot, Simon Fernandes, would not sail on past Roanoke.

Some historians think the pilot wanted to go privateering against Spanish ships. Privateers were armed ships that raided enemy vessels. Whatever the reason, the colonists stayed on Roanoke Island.

On August 18, 1587, just weeks after they landed, Eleanor Dare gave birth to a daughter, Virginia Dare. She was the first English child born in an English colony in the Americas. Her name became part of American folklore as a symbol of the Lost Colony.

Soon after, John White sailed back to England. He wanted more supplies and more recruits for the struggling colony. He planned to return within months.

Three years without a ship

White did not come back for three years. The reason was war. England and Spain went to war in 1588. Queen Elizabeth ordered every ship held at home to defend against the Spanish Armada. No ships were free to carry supplies to the colony.

White managed to get two small ships for a rescue trip in 1588. But French ships attacked them, and they had to turn back. It was March 1590 before White got a place on a privateering trip heading to the Caribbean. The captains agreed to stop at Roanoke on the way home.

White finally reached Roanoke Island on August 18, 1590. It was his granddaughter’s third birthday. Think about that for a moment. A grandfather came home to a little girl, and the little girl was not there.

So what could he read from the signs left behind?

The agreed signal

White had one big clue, and we know about it from his own words. Before he left in 1587, he had told the colonists what to do. If they had to leave, they should carve the name of their destination. If they were leaving in danger, they should add a cross above the name.

CROATOAN was the name of a nearby island. A Croatan man named Manteo lived there, and he had made friends with the English. White wrote that he was “greatly joyed” to see the word.

He saw no cross above it. So White thought the colonists had left without being forced. He believed they had gone to Croatoan Island, which later became part of Hatteras Island, to live with Manteo’s people.

Yet White never reached Croatoan Island. A fierce storm pushed his ships out to sea. He wrote that he would have searched Croatoan “with all the means I could” if the weather and the captains had allowed. He never returned to America.

For centuries after that, people could only guess. Then the guessing began to change.

A star under a patch

In 2012, researchers from the First Colony Foundation looked again at a sixteenth-century map made by John White himself. It is called the “La Virginea Pars” map, and it is kept in the British Museum.

Two small patches of paper had been glued over the map. Underneath, the researchers found a hidden mark. It was a small four-pointed star, colored red and blue. It marked a spot about 50 miles inland, near Albemarle Sound and today’s Edenton, North Carolina. Some think it marked a fort.

The researchers called the place “Site X.” It may mean the colonists had a second place to go if Roanoke did not work out.

In 2015, archaeologists from the First Colony Foundation began digging at Site X. They found English objects from the late sixteenth century. They included pieces of border ware, a kind of English pottery, and a copper aglet, a small metal tube used on clothes.

The finds are not proof that Roanoke colonists lived there. The team leader thinks some colonists may have moved inland to live with Native American allies.

Did they join their neighbors?

A widely held theory fits the CROATOAN carving. It also fits later reports from the Jamestown settlers. They heard stories of Englishmen living among Native American communities. The theory says the colonists split into groups. Little by little, they joined local Native American communities.

Some may have died of disease or violence. Some may have lived long enough to have children. Those children may have grown up speaking Algonquian, not English. Their European roots may have shown only in their faces and surnames.

Some researchers have tried to find descendants through genetic genealogy. This means using DNA and family records to trace family lines. One group they looked at is the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina. This Native American community is centered in Robeson County. Some people think Lumbee surnames match those of the colonists.

Some of these tests found European Y-chromosome types in Lumbee men. The Y chromosome is passed from father to son. But European DNA could have come from many later settlers. No living descendant of the colonists has been identified, and most historians do not accept the link.

What we know, and what we do not

So what do we know? A colony of families was left on Roanoke in 1587. War kept help away for three years. In 1590, White found an empty settlement and a word cut into a post. He saw no cross, so he believed they had left on their own.

What do we not know? We do not know where the colonists went, or what happened to Virginia Dare. The final answer may come from new digs or new records. Nobody can yet say. The question is more than 430 years old.

Think once more of John White in that empty clearing. He had come looking for his family. He found one word, and no more. People have been asking about that word ever since.
