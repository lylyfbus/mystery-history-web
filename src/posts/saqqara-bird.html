The Saqqara Bird: Did Ancient Egyptians Understand Flight?

A small bird in a glass case

Imagine that you walk into the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. Around you are the golden treasures of pharaohs and the painted coffins of nobles. Then you stop at a glass case with a very small object inside.

It is a wooden bird, only 18 centimeters wide from wing tip to wing tip. It weighs just 39 grams. Someone carved it from sycamore wood about 2,200 years ago. It is small enough to sit in the palm of your hand.

At first, it looks like a simple model of a bird. But look more closely, and the Saqqara Bird becomes a puzzle. Some people think that it is a model of an aircraft. Let us find out why, and whether they are right.

Look at the wings first. They are straight and set at a sharp angle. They are not curved like the wings of a bird in flight. The body is smooth and narrows to a point.

Now look at the tail. It stands straight up, like the tail fin of a modern aircraft. A real bird’s tail feathers spread out flat. And the carving has no legs.

Some researchers say that it looks strangely like a modern glider. A glider is an aircraft that flies without an engine. So where did this odd little bird come from?

A tomb at Saqqara

The bird was found in 1898, when people dug out the Pa-di-Imen tomb at Saqqara. The name Pa-di-Imen means “gift of Amun.” Saqqara is a necropolis, which is a very large ancient burial ground. It lies about 30 kilometers south of Cairo, and it is about seven kilometers long.

For more than 3,000 years, Saqqara was the cemetery of Memphis, the ancient capital. It holds the Step Pyramid of Djoser, built more than 4,500 years ago. That pyramid is part of the oldest large stone building complex known in history.

The tomb of Pa-di-Imen comes from around 200 BCE. At that time, Greek kings ruled Egypt. They were the family of Ptolemy, a general of Alexander the Great.

The bird was written into the museum list as a simple bird figure. Then it was put away in storage, and few people noticed it for a long time. That changed when a doctor looked at it again.

The doctor who saw a glider

Dr. Khalil Messiha was an Egyptian doctor. He looked closely at the bird in the museum in Cairo. He became convinced that it was no ordinary bird. He believed that it was a small model of a glider.

Messiha pointed to several things that make it different from other Egyptian birds. The wings are straight and angled downward. The tail fin stands straight up from the body. Modern aircraft have a tail like that, but no known kind of bird does. He also said that the shape matched the rules of modern aircraft design.

A small group of researchers agreed. They say that the carving shows that the ancient Egyptians understood how things fly, many centuries before anyone was supposed to know. His claims got a lot of attention, above all when books and films about ancient mysteries showed the bird.

Some of those books went even further. We will look at them in a moment, but first, let us ask a simple question. Can the bird really fly?

Copies that tumbled

A glider designer named Martin Gregorie studied the Saqqara Bird. He built copies and tested them as gliders. His results were clear. The bird, as carved, does not fly steadily.

A glider needs a small flat wing at the back, called a tailplane, to keep it steady. The Saqqara Bird has none. Without a tailplane, Gregorie’s copies tumbled in the air.

Then he added a modern tailplane, which the ancient carving does not have. Only then did the copies glide in a steady way. Even so, they flew badly. They moved forward only about two and a half meters for each meter they dropped.

So the copy only worked after a modern part was added. That is a poor sign for the idea of an ancient aircraft. But a newer test went even deeper.

What the computer found

In 2023, researchers at the Institute of Aerospace Technology Bremen published a scientific study of the bird. First they made an exact 3D scan of the object. Then they used a method called computational fluid dynamics, or CFD. In CFD, a computer works out how air flows around a shape.

The team tested how the Saqqara Bird would behave in the air. Their results did not support the idea of an ancient aircraft. They found three problems.

First, its best glide ratio was low. Second, its balance point lies too far back. If you launched the bird from a height, it would tip and tumble. Third, the lift was not the same on both wings, and uneven lift would make the bird roll over.

The researchers wrote that their results are “not consistent with the suggestion that the Saqqara Bird demonstrates ancient knowledge of aerodynamics.” In plain words, the bird is not a model of a glider. So what was it?

A holy falcon, or a boat’s mast?

Many experts think that it was a holy object for ceremonies, perhaps in the shape of a falcon. Falcons stood for gods in Egyptian religion, such as Horus. Horus was linked with kings and the sky. Experts have also suggested that it was a child’s toy or a weather vane.

There is another idea. The bird may have been a decoration on the mast of a sacred boat. Such boats were used in religious festivals, such as the Opet Festival. Wall carvings in the Temple of Khonsu at Karnak show such boats. They come from the late New Kingdom.

There is a clue that fits this idea. The hole in the underside shows that the bird was probably made to sit on a stick or pole. But nobody knows for sure.

Visitors from space?

The idea of an ancient flying machine fits an old idea called the “ancient astronaut” idea. It says that visitors from space came to Earth long ago. The Swiss writer Erich von Däniken made it popular in his 1968 bestseller Chariots of the Gods?. He wrote that beings from other worlds shared advanced knowledge with early peoples.

Almost all scientists reject this idea. Kevin Desmond, a writer on the history of flight, found no evidence that the Egyptians built aircraft. The Egyptians were skilled builders, and they built the pyramids. But nothing shows that they tried to build flying machines.

Back to the glass case

Let us return to the museum in Cairo. The Saqqara Bird still catches the imagination, because it is so hard to put in one box. Here is what we know. It is a careful carving of sycamore wood, about 2,200 years old. Tests with copies and with a computer show that, as carved, it cannot fly steadily.

Here is what we do not know. Nobody can say for sure what the carver meant it to be. It may have been a holy falcon, a toy, or a decoration for a boat. Its maker may simply have liked smooth shapes.

The bird also teaches a useful lesson. Looking like something is not the same as being it. An ancient object may look like a modern machine. That does not mean that it worked like one.

So the little bird still sits in its glass case. It has won a fame that its maker could never have imagined. And one question stays open. What did the carver want it to be?
