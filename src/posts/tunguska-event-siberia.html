The Tunguska Event: The Morning the Sky Exploded Over Siberia

A farmer named S. Semenov sat on his porch in Siberia. It was the morning of June 30, 1908, at about 7:17 local time. He lived at Vanavara, a small trading post in the forest. Suddenly he saw “a blindingly bright white-blue light.” It seemed to split the sky in two.

The heat was so strong that he felt his shirt was about to catch fire. Then came a crash like thunder, and it threw him from his chair. “I lost my senses for a moment,” he said later, “but then my wife ran out and led me into the house.”

He went on: “After that, such noise came, as if rocks were falling or cannons were firing, the Earth shook.” Semenov was about 40 miles from the center of the blast.

Something had exploded in the sky near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. First there was a column of light, brighter than the sun. Some people called it a “second sun.” It raced across the sky and then blew up.

Scientists think the blast was as strong as 10 to 50 megatons of TNT. TNT is an explosive, and a megaton is one million tons of it. That is about 1,000 times the power of the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

The blast knocked down about 80 million trees. They fell over an area of 830 square miles, or 2,150 square kilometers. That is larger than many big cities. Herds of reindeer were burned up.

The Evenki are the native people of that forest. Evenki families and Russian settlers dozens of miles away were thrown from their porches and chairs. People heard the sound hundreds of miles away.

Machines that measure earthquakes felt the shaking all across Europe and Asia. A wave of air pressure went around the whole Earth twice.

Then came the strangest part. The first team of scientists reached the place nineteen years later. They found no crater. A crater is the big hole a rock from space makes when it hits the ground.

Something had caused the biggest explosion in recorded human history. Yet it had left almost no mark of itself. More than a century later, the Tunguska Event is still a great puzzle of science.

The time after the blast was strange too. For days, the night skies over Europe and Asia glowed with a silver light. London is 3,000 miles from the blast. Even there, people could read newspapers at midnight.

Today we understand these “glowing clouds.” They were noctilucent clouds, which means clouds that shine at night. They formed from tiny bits of ice that the blast had thrown high into the air. They were beautiful, and they were also a little frightening. They showed that something huge had happened far away in the Siberian wild.

Many ideas grew up to explain it. Some were careful science, and some were very odd. People blamed a meteor, a comet, or a black hole passing through the Earth. They blamed antimatter, or an experiment by Nikola Tesla that went wrong. Some even blamed an alien spaceship with a nuclear engine.

The blast happened in a very lonely part of the world. It was in the taiga, the thick forest of central Siberia. The nearest city was hundreds of miles away.

That is why the blast killed so few people. Experts think there may have been up to three deaths. It is also why scientists took almost twenty years to come and look.

Other people saw the fireball from other places. Some saw a pillar of fire that reached from the ground to the sky. Others spoke of a “second sun” that for a moment was brighter than the real one.

Evenki people who were nearer to the blast said the shock wave threw them through the air. Their reindeer herds were scattered or killed.

Hundreds of miles away, in the city of Irkutsk, the Magnetic and Meteorological Observatory recorded the shaking of the ground. It also recorded strong waves of pressure in the air.

Even in London and Washington D.C., barographs caught the pulse. A barograph is a machine that records air pressure. The Earth truly rang like a bell.

Why did nobody come for so long? The place was very hard to reach. Russia was also in trouble in those years. First came World War I, then the Russian Revolution, then the Russian Civil War. So no science team reached the blast site until 1927.

The man who finally got there was Leonid Kulik. He was a Russian mineralogist and geologist, a scientist who studies rocks and minerals. He had read reports of the explosion, and he could not stop thinking about it.

Kulik asked the Soviet government to pay for a trip. He argued that a giant meteorite might have caused the blast. A meteorite is a rock from space that lands on Earth. He said the iron from such a rock could be useful to Soviet factories.

What Kulik saw at the site was hard to take in. Millions of flattened trees spread out from one central point. Seen from above, the fallen forest had the shape of a butterfly. The trees had gone down like matchsticks, and their tops pointed away from the center.

Near the center, the trees were still standing. But they had lost their branches and bark. They looked like a forest of telephone poles. Scientists call this a “telegraph forest.” It is a sign of a powerful explosion in the air above. The damage went on for dozens of miles in every direction.

Kulik expected to find a huge crater at the center. He searched for years. He drained a lake. He dug trenches.

He found nothing. There was no crater. There were no pieces of meteorite, no iron, and no nickel. Nothing showed what had done the damage. How could something flatten 80 million trees over 830 square miles and leave no hole in the ground?

The answer came slowly, over many decades. The Tunguska object never hit the ground. It exploded in the air. Scientists call this an airburst.

Scientists now believe the object was a stony meteoroid or the core of a comet. A meteoroid is a rock moving through space. The object was about 50 to 200 meters wide, or 160 to 660 feet. It entered Earth’s air at about 54,000 kilometers per hour, or 33,500 miles per hour.

At that speed, the air pushed and rubbed against it with great force. The object grew terribly hot and broke apart. This happened about 5 to 10 kilometers above the ground, or 3 to 6 miles.

The explosion gave off as much energy as 10 to 30 megatons of TNT. That is like a large hydrogen bomb.

Because the blast was several miles up, it sent a crushing shock wave down onto the forest. The trees fell, but no crater formed. The great heat turned almost all of the object into gas. That is why Kulik found no pieces.

Most scientists accept the airburst idea. But what was the object? Was it a stony asteroid or an icy comet? Planet scientists still argue about that today.

One side says it was an asteroid, a rocky body about 50 to 80 meters wide. It was probably a type of stony space rock called a carbonaceous chondrite or an ordinary chondrite.

Computer models support this idea. They show that a stony asteroid of this size, moving at a usual speed for asteroids near Earth, would make an airburst this big. Asteroids of this size are fairly common. Experts think a blast like Tunguska happens about once every 300 to 1,000 years.

The other side says it was the core of a small comet, made mostly of ice and dust. They give three reasons. First, there was very little meteorite rubble, and a comet would turn to gas more completely than a stony asteroid. Second, the “glowing skies” fit with ice high in the air.

Third, some people said they saw a shining trail in the sky for days before the explosion. Italian teams also went to Tunguska in the 1970s and the 2000s. They studied the mud at the bottom of lakes near the site. They believed their finds fitted a comet. Other scientists have challenged that view.

In 2013, an asteroid about 20 meters wide exploded over Chelyabinsk in Russia. The force was about 500 kilotons of TNT, which is about 1/30th of Tunguska. About 1,500 people were hurt, mostly by broken glass. Dozens of car cameras filmed the fireball. Nobody had seen the rock coming, and that was a wake-up call for people who protect our planet.

In September 2022, NASA crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid on purpose. The mission was the Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART. The asteroid was called Dimorphos. The crash changed its orbit, the path it follows through space.

It was the first time people showed they could change the path of an asteroid. It is a key first step in defending the Earth. If a rock the size of Tunguska were found heading for Earth, a tool like DART might push it aside.

What if a Tunguska airburst happened over a modern city? The result would be terrible. It would be like a large hydrogen bomb going off several miles up, though without the poison dust such a bomb leaves behind.

The blast wave would break windows across hundreds of square miles. It would knock down buildings, and the winds would be as strong as a hurricane. The heat would cause bad burns and start fires over a wide area.

Chelyabinsk had only about 1/30th of the power of Tunguska. Still, it damaged more than 7,200 buildings in six cities. A blast as big as Tunguska over a crowded place could kill millions.

NASA and other space agencies are searching for objects that come near Earth. One of NASA’s programs is the Planetary Defense Coordination Office. The hard part is finding the rocks. Objects 50 to 200 meters wide are difficult to see. So far we have listed only a small share of those that cross Earth’s path.

One idea blamed Nikola Tesla’s Wardenclyffe Tower. Tesla was testing ways to send energy without wires, and the story says his tower caused the blast by accident. There is no proof the tower could make anything close to that power. Tesla never claimed it did.

Some physicists suggested a tiny black hole from the start of the universe. It would have passed right through the Earth, going in at Siberia and out through the North Atlantic. But then there should have been a second blast on the way out. Nobody ever recorded one.

Another idea was a lump of antimatter hitting the air. Antimatter is like a mirror form of normal matter, and the two destroy each other when they meet. That could explain the damage and the missing crater. But it would also give off special gamma rays, and none were found.

In 1946, the Soviet science fiction writer Alexander Kazantsev had another idea. He suggested that an alien spaceship with a nuclear engine blew up over Tunguska.

Scientists have studied all these ideas carefully and turned them down. Almost all of them support the airburst. The other stories live on anyway. That tells us more about how people love the unknown than about the evidence.

Tunguska is the closest thing to a near miss from space in modern history. On a warm June morning in 1908, a rock, or a ball of ice and dust, came in at 33,500 miles per hour. It blew up with the force of a hydrogen bomb over the empty forest.

Think about the timing. The Earth is always turning. If the object had come four hours and 47 minutes later, the city of St. Petersburg would have been right under the blast. The harm would have been too great to imagine.

The event was a warning from space. Our planet moves through a kind of shooting gallery. The space above our heads looks empty, but it is not. It is full of rock, ice, and rubble left over from when the solar system formed. Now and then, one piece finds its way to Earth.

The next Tunguska is out there somewhere. Was the last one an asteroid or a comet? And when will the next one come? We still do not know.
