The Tunguska Event: The Morning the Sky Exploded Over Siberia

A flash over the porch

It is the morning of June 30, 1908, at about 7:15. A farmer named S. Semenov sits on his porch at Vanavara, a small trading post in the forest of Siberia.

Suddenly a bright flash of light fills the sky. Years later, Semenov wrote a letter about it. He said the sky seemed to split in two.

Then he felt great heat. A crash like thunder threw him to the ground, and for a moment he lost his senses. The Earth shook. The noise sounded like falling rocks or cannon fire.

Semenov was about 60 kilometers, or 37 miles, from the center of the blast. His letter is one of the few stories that people wrote down from that morning. So what could make such a blast? Let us look at what it left behind.

A forest, and a shaking world

Something had exploded in the sky near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. Witnesses spoke of a column of light, brighter than the sun. Some called it a “second sun.” It raced across the sky and then blew up.

Scientists give different numbers for the power of the blast. Estimates run from about 3 to 30 megatons of TNT, and many sources say 10 to 20. TNT is an explosive, and a megaton is one million tons of it. Even the low number is hundreds of times the power of the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

The blast knocked down several million trees. They fell over about 830 square miles, or 2,150 square kilometers. That is larger than many big cities. You may read that 80 million trees fell, but experts now say that number is too high. Many reindeer and dogs were killed.

The Evenki are the native people of that forest. Some Evenki families and Russian settlers, dozens of miles away, were thrown to the ground.

Few people lived there, and that is why so few died. Reports differ. Some say two people died, and others say at least three.

Far away, in the city of Irkutsk, instruments recorded the shaking of the ground. Machines that record earthquakes picked it up in Russia and in Germany.

Other machines measure the pressure of the air. They are called barographs. Even in London and Washington D.C., they caught the pulse of the blast. Machines noticed it all over the world. The Earth truly rang like a bell. For days, the night skies over Europe and Asia glowed with light. In many places, people could read newspapers near midnight.

Scientists think these glowing skies came from dust or ice that the blast threw high into the air.

With so much shaking and glowing, you would expect scientists to rush to the spot. They did not. Let us see why.

Nobody came for nineteen years

The place was very hard to reach. It lay in the taiga, the thick forest of central Siberia, and no big town was close. Russia was also in trouble in those years, with war and revolution. A first trip in 1921 could not reach the site.

So the first team of scientists arrived in 1927, about nineteen years later. Their leader was Leonid Kulik, a Russian mineralogist and geologist. That is a scientist who studies rocks and minerals. He had read reports of the explosion, and he could not stop thinking about it.

Kulik thought a giant meteorite might have caused the blast. A meteorite is a rock from space that lands on Earth. He asked the Soviet government to pay for the trip.

What he saw was hard to take in. Millions of flattened trees spread out from one central point. Seen from above, the fallen forest had the shape of a butterfly.

Near the center, the trees were still standing, but they had lost their branches. They looked like a forest of telephone poles. Scientists call this a “telegraph forest.” It is a sign of a powerful explosion in the air above.

Now Kulik wanted the crater. A crater is the big hole that a rock from space makes when it hits the ground.

The hole that was never there

Kulik searched for years. He looked at many small pits in the ground. He drained one of them. He dug trenches.

He found nothing like a crater from a meteorite. At the bottom of the pit that he drained, he found an old tree stump. How could something flatten millions of trees and leave no hole in the ground?

The answer came slowly, over many decades. The Tunguska object most likely never hit the ground. It blew up in the air. Scientists call this an airburst.

Imagine a rock from space that rushes into our air at about 55,000 kilometers per hour. The air pushes and rubs against it with great force. It grows terribly hot and breaks apart. This happened about 5 to 10 kilometers above the ground.

Because the blast was several miles up, it sent a crushing shock wave down onto the forest. The trees fell, but no crater formed. The great heat probably turned most of the object into gas. That may be why Kulik found no big pieces.

Most scientists accept the airburst idea. But they still argue about one question.

Ideas that did not hold up

Before we reach that question, let us visit some odd ideas. Scientists have studied them carefully and turned them down.

One idea blamed Nikola Tesla’s Wardenclyffe Tower. Tesla was testing ways to send energy without wires, and the story says his tower caused the blast by accident. Historians say the facts do not fit. Tesla never finished the tower, and the first “death ray” story about it came decades later.

Some physicists suggested a tiny black hole from the start of the universe. It would have passed right through the Earth, going in at Siberia and out through the North Atlantic. But then there should have been a second blast on the way out. Nobody ever recorded one.

Another idea was a lump of antimatter. Antimatter is like a mirror form of normal matter, and the two destroy each other when they meet. That could explain the damage and the missing crater. But it would also give off special gamma rays, and none were found.

In 1946, the Soviet writer Alexander Kazantsev wrote a science fiction story called “Explosion.” In it, an alien spaceship with a nuclear engine blows up over Siberia. The story helped make the spaceship idea popular. It was fiction, not evidence.

A rock or a ball of ice?

Almost all scientists support the airburst. But what was the object? Many Western scientists favor an asteroid, and many Russian scientists favor a comet. Many give its size as about 50 to 80 meters wide, and some say 100 meters or more.

The first side says it was a stony asteroid. Computer models support this idea. They show that a rock of this size, moving at a usual speed for asteroids near Earth, could make an airburst this big. Experts think a blast like Tunguska happens about once every 300 to 1,000 years.

The other side says it was the core of a small comet, made mostly of ice and dust. They give two reasons. First, there was very little meteorite rubble, and a comet would turn to gas more completely than a stony asteroid. Second, the glowing skies fit with ice high in the air.

Italian researchers also joined in. Starting in 2007, they looked at the mud at the bottom of Lake Cheko, a small lake near the site. They thought the lake might be a crater from a piece of the object. Other scientists have challenged that view.

So the answer is still open. And the next one may not wait another hundred years.

A warning from the sky

In 2013, an asteroid about 18 meters wide exploded over Chelyabinsk in Russia. Its force was about 500 kilotons of TNT. That is about 30 times the Hiroshima bomb, but much less than Tunguska. About 1,500 people sought help for injuries, mostly from broken glass, and more than 7,200 buildings were damaged in six cities. Nobody had seen the rock coming.

NASA and other space agencies now search for objects that come near Earth. One of NASA’s programs is the Planetary Defense Coordination Office. The hard part is finding the rocks. Objects 50 to 200 meters wide are difficult to see, and many are still not found.

In September 2022, NASA crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid on purpose. The mission was the Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART. The asteroid was called Dimorphos, a small moon that circles a larger asteroid, Didymos. The crash changed the path of Dimorphos. It was the first test of this idea, and it is a key first step in defending the Earth.

Back on the porch

Think again of Semenov on his porch. He saw a flash, and a rock or a ball of ice came apart in the air above the forest.

Here is what we know. In 1908, something blew up over Siberia, several miles up, with the force of a large hydrogen bomb. It knocked down several million trees and left no crater. Most scientists agree that it was an airburst.

Here is what is still open. Was it an asteroid or a comet? Nobody can say for sure.

It was a warning from space. The sky above our heads looks empty, but it is full of rock, ice, and rubble left over from when the solar system formed. Now and then, one piece finds its way to Earth. If a rock of the size of Tunguska ever heads our way, a tool like DART might push it aside.
