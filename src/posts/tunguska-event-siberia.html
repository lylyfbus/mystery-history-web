The Tunguska Event: The Morning the Sky Exploded Over Siberia

A farmer named S. Semenov sat on his porch in Siberia. It was the morning of June 30, 1908, at about 7:15 local time. He lived at Vanavara, a small trading post in the forest. Suddenly he saw a bright flash of light. In a letter written years later, he said it seemed to split the sky in two.

He felt great heat. Then came a crash like thunder, and it threw him to the ground. He wrote that he lost his senses for a moment. The Earth shook, and the noise sounded like falling rocks or cannon fire.

Semenov was about 60 kilometers, or 37 miles, from the center of the blast. His story is one of the few that people wrote down from that morning.

Something had exploded in the sky near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River. Witnesses spoke of a column of light, brighter than the sun. Some people called it a “second sun.” It raced across the sky and then blew up.

Scientists give different numbers for the blast. Estimates run from about 3 to 30 megatons of TNT, and many sources say 10 to 20. TNT is an explosive, and a megaton is one million tons of it. Even the low estimate is hundreds of times the power of the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

The blast knocked down several million trees. They fell over an area of about 830 square miles, or 2,150 square kilometers. That is larger than many big cities. You may read that 80 million trees fell. Experts now say that number is too high. Many reindeer and dogs were killed.

The Evenki are the native people of that forest. Some Evenki families and Russian settlers dozens of miles away were thrown to the ground. Many people heard the sound from far away.

Instruments that record earthquakes picked up the shaking in Russia and in Germany. Machines that measure air pressure noticed the blast all over the world.

Then came the strangest part. The first team of scientists reached the place in 1927, about nineteen years later. They found no crater. A crater is the big hole a rock from space makes when it hits the ground.

Something had caused one of the biggest explosions in recorded human history. Yet it had left almost no mark of itself. More than a century later, the Tunguska Event is still a great puzzle of science.

The time after the blast was strange too. For days, the night skies over Europe and Asia glowed with light. In many places, people could read newspapers near midnight.

Scientists think these glowing skies came from dust or ice that the blast threw high into the air. They were beautiful, and they were also a little frightening. They showed that something huge had happened far away in the Siberian wild.

Many ideas grew up to explain it. Some were careful science, and some were very odd. People blamed a meteor, a comet, or a black hole passing through the Earth. They blamed antimatter, or an experiment by Nikola Tesla that went wrong. Some even blamed an alien spaceship with a nuclear engine.

The blast happened in a very lonely part of the world. It was in the taiga, the thick forest of central Siberia. No big town was close.

That is why the blast killed so few people. Reports differ. Some say two people died, and others say at least three. It is also why scientists took almost twenty years to come and look.

Other people saw the fireball from other places. Some saw a pillar of fire that reached from the ground to the sky. Others spoke of a “second sun” that for a moment was brighter than the real one.

Evenki people who were nearer to the blast said the shock wave threw them through the air. Their reindeer herds were scattered or killed.

Far away, in the city of Irkutsk, instruments recorded the shaking of the ground. Other stations recorded strong waves of pressure in the air.

Even in London and Washington D.C., barographs caught the pulse. A barograph is a machine that records air pressure. The Earth truly rang like a bell.

Why did nobody come for so long? The place was very hard to reach. Russia was also in trouble in those years, with war and revolution. A first trip in 1921 could not reach the site. So no science team reached the blast site until 1927.

The man who finally got there was Leonid Kulik. He was a Russian mineralogist and geologist, a scientist who studies rocks and minerals. He had read reports of the explosion, and he could not stop thinking about it.

Kulik asked the Soviet government to pay for a trip. He thought a giant meteorite might have caused the blast. A meteorite is a rock from space that lands on Earth.

What Kulik saw at the site was hard to take in. Millions of flattened trees spread out from one central point. Seen from above, the fallen forest had the shape of a butterfly. The trees had gone down like matchsticks, and their tops pointed away from the center.

Near the center, the trees were still standing. But they had lost their branches. They looked like a forest of telephone poles. Scientists call this a “telegraph forest.” It is a sign of a powerful explosion in the air above. The damage went on for dozens of miles in every direction.

Kulik expected to find a huge crater at the center. He searched for years. He looked at many small pits in the ground. He drained one of them. He dug trenches.

He found nothing like a crater from a meteorite. At the bottom of the pit he drained, he found an old tree stump. Nothing showed what had done the damage. How could something flatten millions of trees over 830 square miles and leave no hole in the ground?

The answer came slowly, over many decades. The Tunguska object most likely never hit the ground. It exploded in the air. Scientists call this an airburst.

Many scientists now believe the object was a stony meteoroid or the core of a comet. A meteoroid is a rock moving through space. Estimates of its size differ. Many give about 50 to 80 meters wide, or 160 to 260 feet. Some give 100 meters or more. It may have entered Earth’s air at about 55,000 kilometers per hour, or 34,000 miles per hour.

At that speed, the air pushed and rubbed against it with great force. The object grew terribly hot and broke apart. This happened about 5 to 10 kilometers above the ground, or 3 to 6 miles.

The explosion gave off a huge amount of energy, as we saw above. That is like a large hydrogen bomb.

Because the blast was several miles up, it sent a crushing shock wave down onto the forest. The trees fell, but no crater formed. The great heat probably turned most of the object into gas. That may be why Kulik found no big pieces.

Most scientists accept the airburst idea. But what was the object? Was it a stony asteroid or an icy comet? Scientists still argue about that today. Many Western scientists favor an asteroid. Many Russian scientists favor a comet.

One side says it was an asteroid, a rocky body about 50 to 80 meters wide.

Computer models support this idea. They show that a stony asteroid of this size, moving at a usual speed for asteroids near Earth, could make an airburst this big. Asteroids of this size are fairly common. Experts think a blast like Tunguska happens about once every 300 to 1,000 years.

The other side says it was the core of a small comet, made mostly of ice and dust. They give two reasons. First, there was very little meteorite rubble, and a comet would turn to gas more completely than a stony asteroid. Second, the glowing skies fit with ice high in the air.

Italian researchers also studied Tunguska. Starting in 2007, they looked at the mud at the bottom of Lake Cheko, a small lake near the site. They thought the lake might be a crater from a piece of the object. Other scientists have challenged that view.

In 2013, an asteroid about 18 meters wide exploded over Chelyabinsk in Russia. The force was about 500 kilotons of TNT, which is about 30 times the Hiroshima bomb but much less than Tunguska. About 1,500 people sought help for injuries, mostly from broken glass. Dozens of car cameras filmed the fireball. Nobody had seen the rock coming, and that was a wake-up call for people who protect our planet.

In September 2022, NASA crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid on purpose. The mission was the Double Asteroid Redirection Test, or DART. The asteroid was called Dimorphos. It is a small moon that circles a larger asteroid, Didymos. The crash changed the path of Dimorphos around Didymos.

It was the first mission to test this idea by crashing a spacecraft into an asteroid. It is a key first step in defending the Earth. If a rock the size of Tunguska were found heading for Earth, a tool like DART might push it aside.

What if a Tunguska airburst happened over a modern city? The result would be terrible. It would be like a large hydrogen bomb going off several miles up, though without the poison dust such a bomb leaves behind.

The blast wave would break windows across a wide area. It would knock down buildings. The heat could cause burns and start fires.

Chelyabinsk was far weaker than Tunguska. Still, it damaged more than 7,200 buildings in six cities. A blast as big as Tunguska over a crowded place could hurt a great many people.

NASA and other space agencies are searching for objects that come near Earth. One of NASA’s programs is the Planetary Defense Coordination Office. The hard part is finding the rocks. Objects 50 to 200 meters wide are difficult to see. Many are still not found.

One idea blamed Nikola Tesla’s Wardenclyffe Tower. Tesla was testing ways to send energy without wires, and the story says his tower caused the blast by accident. Historians say the facts do not fit. Tesla never finished the tower, and the first “death ray” story about it came decades later.

Some physicists suggested a tiny black hole from the start of the universe. It would have passed right through the Earth, going in at Siberia and out through the North Atlantic. But then there should have been a second blast on the way out. Nobody ever recorded one.

Another idea was a lump of antimatter hitting the air. Antimatter is like a mirror form of normal matter, and the two destroy each other when they meet. That could explain the damage and the missing crater. But it would also give off special gamma rays, and none were found.

In 1946, the Soviet science fiction writer Alexander Kazantsev wrote a story called “Explosion.” In it, an alien spaceship with a nuclear engine blows up over Siberia. The story helped make the spaceship idea popular.

Scientists have studied all these ideas carefully and turned them down. Almost all of them support the airburst. The other stories live on anyway. That tells us more about how people love the unknown than about the evidence.

Tunguska was one of the biggest space blasts in modern history. On a warm June morning in 1908, a rock, or a ball of ice and dust, came in very fast. It blew up with the force of a hydrogen bomb over the empty forest.

Think about the timing. The Earth is always turning. If the object had come about five hours later, the spinning Earth might have carried the city of St. Petersburg under the blast. The harm would have been too great to imagine.

The event was a warning from space. Our planet moves through a kind of shooting gallery. The space above our heads looks empty, but it is not. It is full of rock, ice, and rubble left over from when the solar system formed. Now and then, one piece finds its way to Earth.

The next Tunguska is out there somewhere. Was the last one an asteroid or a comet? And when will the next one come? We still do not know.
