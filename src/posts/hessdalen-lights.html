The Hessdalen Lights: Mysterious Glowing Orbs That Haunt a Norwegian Valley

Balls of light over a frozen valley

In December 1981, the people of a small valley in Norway began to see bright lights in the sky. They saw them again and again, and they could not explain them.

The valley is called Hessdalen. It lies in central Norway, south-east of the city of Trondheim, in Holtålen municipality in Trøndelag county. Mountains stand all around it. Only a few hundred people live there, on small farms among the pine forests.

On dark winter nights, silent balls of light are said to float above the frozen fields. Nobody calls them. Nobody can say when they will come. Let us find out what the witnesses say about them.

What the witnesses saw

The people described glowing balls. They were usually white or yellow, and sometimes red. The lights could also be bright white or fiery red. Some flashes were blue. One study says that the balls ranged from a few decimeters up to about 30 meters across.

The witnesses said that the lights made no sound. Sometimes they hung in one place. Sometimes they drifted slowly across the valley. Some people said that the lights shot away at great speed and then turned sharply. The lights joined together and split apart. Then they vanished without a trace. Some lasted only a few seconds, and others stayed for well over an hour.

A few witnesses said that the lights seemed to notice them. They said that the lights changed their path when someone came close. Nobody has proved this, and it may be only how the lights looked to them.

Reports of strange lights in the valley go back to at least the 1930s. But the busiest time came between December 1981 and the middle of 1984. In those years, people reported the lights 15 to 20 times a week. Tourists, reporters, and curious visitors came to the valley. The quiet place turned into an outdoor observatory.

This was not a vague shape that a few people saw on one night. The lights came back many times. Some were caught in photographs and on film. So scientists began to ask for proof that they could measure.

A valley that makes people curious

Look at the place first. The valley is narrow and runs from north to south. The lights are seen along a stretch of about 12 kilometers.

The ground matters, too. Some of the rock holds grains of a mineral called quartz. Many scientists think that the shape of the land, the weather, and the rock may play a part. We will come back to that idea soon.

The Hessdalen Lights are different from most UFO sightings. They come back again and again. They appear more often in winter and in the evening hours. Some have been seen on radar and photographed by automatic cameras. That is why the valley became a well-known place for people who study strange lights.

Scientists come to the valley

In 1983, a UFO researcher named Erling Strand started Project Hessdalen. Groups from Norway and Sweden joined in. The Norwegian military supported the project from 1983 to 1985.

The project brought instruments into the valley. There were cameras, radar, and infrared viewers. There were also magnetometers, which measure magnetism. A report from the field study of 1984 listed 53 observations. It concluded that the lights could be measured.

The plan was simple. The team would watch the lights with care, collect facts that they could measure, and try to learn what the lights were. Between 1983 and 1985, they ran several field trips.

The results were not always clear. Some events were seen on radar and by eye at the same time. Others showed up in only one of the two. That is one reason why the lights are still argued about.

In 1998, a permanent station was set up in the valley. It is called the Hessdalen Automatic Measurement Station, and people call it the “Blue Box.” It is a shipping container with cameras and other sensors. Erling Strand and Bjørn Gitle Hauge guided the work.

The station can watch the sky all the time. Its records show that the lights appear more often in winter. They appear mostly between about 9 or 10 at night and 1 in the morning. But what is the light made of?

Reading the colors

Scientists use a spectroscope to find out. It splits light into its separate colors, called wavelengths. Each chemical element leaves its own pattern of bright lines.

In 2004, the Italian physicist Massimo Teodorani published a long study of the lights in the Journal of Scientific Exploration. The study was peer-reviewed. That means other experts checked the work first. In 2007, Hauge wrote a report on the light itself.

The results are not simple. One account says that the analysis found hydrogen, oxygen, and titanium. Other writers say that scandium may play a part. Another account says that the tools were too weak to name the elements for sure.

If the light really comes from glowing gas, that would fit a plasma. A plasma is a gas in which the atoms have lost some of their electrons, and that makes it glow. Some researchers think that dust from the valley may be part of it.

After more than four decades of work, the lights are still unexplained. But there are several ideas. The best known is the plasma idea.

Idea one: a glowing ball of gas

Teodorani, who has studied the lights in depth, supports the plasma idea. By this theory, each light is a natural plasma in the air, a glowing ball of charged gas. It would be similar to ball lightning, but not the same. One model says that dust and the gas radon make the plasma.

The idea has trouble with the way the lights behave. They hang in the air for a long time. Natural plasma, such as ball lightning, usually lasts only seconds. Until recently, none of the ideas explained everything that people saw.

Rocks, a battery, a fire

A second idea is about piezoelectricity. This is a hard word for a simple thing. Some minerals, above all quartz, make an electric charge when they are squeezed. By this idea, pressure in the rocks of the valley makes charges that light up the air above. Critics say that the valley has little quartz. It is also not clear that the effect could make lights so large, so bright, and so lasting.

A third idea is sometimes called the “natural battery” theory. By this idea, the sides of the valley work like the two ends of a giant battery. The electric currents then charge the air above.

A fourth idea is combustion, which means burning. Dust from the valley floor that holds scandium would burn in the air. Some think that this fits the colors of the light.

Skeptics give a simpler answer. Brian Dunning wrote that many of the station’s pictures look like the landing lights of aircraft. He points out that an air route runs straight up the valley. He also says that no lights have been reported from inside the valley.

Researchers have also tested other ideas, such as solar activity, cosmic rays, and the upper air. None has been proven. Some say that they can rule out simple answers such as thunderstorms. The lights do not seem to be linked to them.

Still glowing

Today the lights are still reported, but far less often than in the great years of 1981 to 1984. About 20 sightings a year are reported, and the project says that the real number may be higher.

Project Hessdalen is now more than 40 years old. It is a non-profit group run by volunteers, a form of citizen science, and it was registered as a non-profit in 2023. Members pay for equipment with fees and gifts. The group streams camera pictures on the internet. The Blue Box now has more tools, such as a passive radar and an infrasound sensor, which picks up sound too low for people to hear. The group also plans to use fast drones that fly close to the lights.

Most UFO sightings are short and poorly observed. At Hessdalen, instruments have recorded for decades. Do the lights come from something real and physical? Many researchers think so. The most popular idea is some kind of natural plasma. Skeptics think that many lights are ordinary lights, such as aircraft. Nobody knows exactly how it works.

So come back to the frozen valley in the long northern night. Perhaps better instruments will give the answer one day. Until then, the lights keep glowing in the Norwegian sky. What are they?
