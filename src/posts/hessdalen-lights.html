The Hessdalen Lights: Mysterious Glowing Orbs That Haunt a Norwegian Valley

Hessdalen is a lonely valley in central Norway, about 120 kilometers south of the city of Trondheim. On dark winter nights, something strange happens there. Silent balls of light appear in the sky above the frozen farms and the pine forests. Nobody calls them. Nobody can say when they will come.

The lights can be bright white, warm yellow, or fiery red. Now and then they are blue. They float and zigzag. They join together and split apart. Then they vanish without a trace. Some last only a few seconds, and others stay for well over an hour.

This has been going on for nearly a century. The busiest time was between December 1981 and the middle of 1984. In those years, people reported the lights up to twenty times a week. Fewer than two hundred people live in this small farming valley. It became one of the most closely studied places of its kind on Earth.

The Hessdalen Lights are different from most UFO sightings. They come back again and again, and scientists can partly predict them. They have been caught on radar and photographed by automatic cameras. Physicists from Norway, Italy, and other countries have studied their light with special instruments.

Scientists have worked on the puzzle for more than four decades. Still, no single theory has explained what the lights are. Nobody knows why they appear here or why they behave as they do.

The valley is narrow and runs from north to south. It is about 15 kilometers long and lies roughly 617 meters above sea level. It belongs to the municipality of Holtålen in Trøndelag county. Few people live there, and mountains stand all around it. The mountains are made of old granite and gneiss, rock that formed some 400 million years ago.

The ground under the valley matters to this story. The rock is rich in a mineral called quartz. Fault lines, which are deep cracks in the Earth’s crust, cut across the valley. There are also large amounts of copper and zinc. These details became very important to the scientists.

People first reported odd lights in the area as early as the 1930s. But serious attention came much later, with the great wave of sightings that began in late 1981.

In December 1981, the people of the valley began to see bright lights in the sky, again and again. They could not explain them. Their descriptions matched closely. They saw glowing balls, usually white or yellow, but sometimes red or orange. Some seemed only a few centimeters across. Others seemed as big as a car.

The lights came by day and by night, but they looked most striking against the dark northern sky. They made no sound. Sometimes they hung in one place. Sometimes they drifted slowly across the valley. And sometimes they shot away at amazing speed and then turned sharply.

Some witnesses said the lights seemed to notice them. The lights grew brighter when people flashed lamps at them. They changed their path when someone came close.

The number of sightings was remarkable. From late 1981 to the middle of 1984, observers recorded the lights 15 to 20 times a week. Tourists, reporters, and curious visitors came from all over Scandinavia and beyond. The quiet valley turned into an outdoor observatory.

The wave was important for a second reason. It was steady. This was not a few doubtful witnesses who saw a vague shape on one night. The lights came back many times. Dozens of trustworthy people saw them at the same moment. The lights were also caught in photographs and on film.

The Norwegian news covered the story widely, and the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment took an interest. Many of the local people were farmers and workers. They had no special interest in UFOs or ghost stories. They were simply puzzled.

The lights did not act like airplanes, helicopters, weather balloons, or satellites. They did not act like anything else known in the sky. They made no sound. No heat from them could be measured, and they left no mark on the ground. They appeared, danced in silence, and vanished. Then they came back, night after night, for more than two years.

So many sightings led to the first serious scientific study. In 1983, Dr. Erling Strand of Østfold University College started Project Hessdalen. Research groups from Norway and Sweden joined in, along with university scientists and volunteer engineers.

The project brought many instruments into the valley. There were radar systems, laser rangefinders to measure distance, and cameras. There were magnetometers, which measure magnetism. There were also spectrographic cameras, which record the colors hidden inside light.

The goal was simple. The team would watch the lights with care, collect facts they could measure, and learn what the lights were made of. Between 1983 and 1985, Project Hessdalen ran several field trips.

The results were striking. Radar picked up the lights. That showed they were real, physical things and not tricks of the eye. The color study showed the marks of certain chemical elements, including scandium and iron. This suggested the lights were real glowing objects that made their own light.

That success led to more research. In 1998, a permanent station was set up in the valley. It is called the Hessdalen Automatic Measurement Station, or AMS. It is a small building, and local people call it the “Blue Box.”

The station has automatic cameras, spectrometers, and sensors. They can spot and record the lights 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. This was a big step forward. For the first time, the lights could be watched without a break. The records could then be studied with statistics.

Between 1998 and 2004, Italian and Norwegian scientists worked together on a new project. Dr. Massimo Teodorani and Dr. Bjørn Gitle Hauge led it. They brought more instruments, did more field work, and published their results in peer-reviewed science journals. Peer-reviewed means that other experts check the work first.

Hauge’s radar work measured the size, height, and movement of the lights. These were some of the most exact measurements ever collected.

One of the most important results is about the light itself. The researchers passed the light from the balls through a spectroscope. This instrument splits light into its separate colors, called wavelengths. Each chemical element leaves its own pattern of bright lines.

The lines matched scandium, iron, and oxygen. There were lines for nitrogen too. This pattern fits a plasma. A plasma is a gas in which the atoms have lost some of their electrons, and that makes it glow. The metal in the plasma may come from dust or from minerals in the valley.

These results matter because they rule out many simple answers. Tricks of the eye, reflections, and bending of light in the air do not leave the marks of chemical elements. The Hessdalen Lights make their own light. They do it through a physical process that gives matter an electric charge.

After more than four decades of work, the lights are still unexplained. But scientists have offered several ideas. The best known is the plasma idea. Its strongest supporter is Dr. Teodorani, an Italian astrophysicist who has studied the lights in depth.

By this theory, the lights are a kind of natural plasma in the air. Each one is a glowing ball of charged gas. It would be similar to ball lightning, but not the same. The color study supports this, because it shows a plasma with metal in it.

But the plasma idea has trouble with the way the lights behave. They hang in the air for a long time. They turn suddenly. They seem to react to people. Natural plasma, such as ball lightning, usually lasts only seconds. It does not move in the complex way seen at Hessdalen, as if it had a purpose.

A second main idea is about piezoelectricity. This is a hard word for a simple thing. Some minerals, above all quartz, make an electric charge when they are squeezed. The valley sits on rock that is rich in quartz, and fault lines cut across it.

By this theory, pressure inside the Earth squeezes the rocks of the valley. The rocks make electric charges. The charges turn the air above into glowing balls of plasma.

The idea is attractive because it explains why the lights appear in Hessdalen. The valley has quartz-rich rock, fault lines, and the right shape of land. Together they could make the charges. But critics have a doubt. The effect usually makes only tiny amounts of electricity. It is not clear that it could make lights so large, so bright, and so lasting.

A related idea is sometimes called the “natural battery” theory. The valley holds large amounts of copper and zinc. By this idea, the ground works like one giant battery. Groundwater does the job of the liquid inside a battery, which is called an electrolyte. The electric currents then charge the air above.

Over the decades, researchers have tested many simple answers and thrown them out, one by one. Airplanes and helicopters are ruled out. Radar shows objects that stay still or hover, and there is no engine noise at all. Weather balloons, satellites, and meteors are ruled out by the way the lights move and how long they last.

People often blame the planet Venus for UFO sightings. But Venus cannot hover, zigzag, or change color. Tricks of the eye and bending of light are ruled out by the color study, which shows real light. Car headlights are ruled out too, because radar found the objects high in the air.

A study in 2015 and 2016 asked whether earthquakes could be linked to the lights. The results were mixed. Data from 2025 and 2026 found something new. The lights were more active during magnetic storms caused by the Sun, in the period called Solar Cycle 25.

So the Sun may play a part. But that does not explain why the lights appear in Hessdalen. Other places are hit by the same magnetic storms, and they have no such lights.

In the mid-2020s, the Hessdalen Lights still appear. But they come far less often than in the great years of 1981 to 1984. Current estimates are about 10 to 20 sightings a year. Project Hessdalen is now in its fifth decade. It is one of the longest scientific studies of an unexplained event in history.

The equipment in the valley has been improved again and again. New automatic stations have joined the first AMS from 1998. They carry cameras that see many kinds of light, VLF/ELF radio receivers, magnetometers, and weather sensors. VLF and ELF are very low radio waves.

A drone program began in the mid-2020s. Researchers can now fly instruments straight toward the lights and around them when they appear. The drones record light and movement in finer detail than ever before.

The drone films showed something important. Some balls of light seem to rise from the ground or from the tops of ridges before they climb into the sky. This suggests the lights may start in the rocks and not in the air.

The researchers have also asked ordinary people to help. This is called citizen science. They made a phone app for visitors. With it, anyone can report a sighting at once, with GPS position and phone photographs.

The valley now gets a modest number of tourists. It has a small visitor center. In the winter months, guides lead “light-watching” trips, because the long northern nights are the best time to look.

Sightings are fewer than at the peak in the 1980s. But the quality of the data is far better, thanks to better spectroscopes, digital cameras, and computers. The record now covers more than four decades. It is one of the fullest records of strange lights ever put together.

The Hessdalen Lights are among the best-recorded unexplained events in the world. Most UFO sightings are short and poorly observed, and nobody can study them afterward. The Hessdalen Lights are different. Instruments have recorded them. Spectroscopes have studied them. Radar has tracked them. Automatic stations have watched them for decades.

The evidence is clear. Something real and physical makes light in the Hessdalen valley. It has done so for at least ninety years. The most likely answer is some kind of natural plasma, made by the special rocks and air of the valley. But nobody knows exactly how it works. The complex behavior of the lights still defeats every theory.

As instruments get better and the data grows, the answer may come one day. Or the lights may go on reminding us that nature still has secrets, even in the 21st century. Until then, they keep glowing in the Norwegian sky, silent and beautiful. What are they?
