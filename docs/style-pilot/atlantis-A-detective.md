# The Lost City of Atlantis

*Draft A: the detective voice*

## The sentence that stops

The book stops in the middle of a sentence.

Zeus, the king of the Greek gods, is angry. A rich and proud island has gone too far, and he wants to punish it. He calls the other gods to a meeting. He begins to speak. And there the page ends.

The writer was Plato, a famous thinker in the Greek city of Athens. He wrote these lines around 360 BCE, and he never finished them. But he had already given the island a name.

Atlantis.

For 2,400 years, people have searched for it. They have named the Azores, the Bahamas, the North Sea, and even Antarctica. Nobody has found a single stone. So this is our case. Was Atlantis a real place? Or did Plato invent it?

## The first report

Every detective starts with the first report. In this case, there are only two. Both are books by Plato, called *Timaeus* and *Critias*. They are the only ancient source for the story. Every film, novel, and theory about Atlantis goes back to these two texts.

Here is what they say. Atlantis lay in the ocean beyond the Pillars of Hercules. Today we call that place the Strait of Gibraltar, where the Mediterranean Sea meets the Atlantic. The island was huge. Plato says it was larger than Libya and Asia together.

The capital was built in rings. There was a ring of water, then a ring of land, then water again, like a target. Bridges and a canal joined the rings. In the middle stood the palace of the kings. Beside it was a temple to Poseidon, the god of the sea. The temple was covered in silver, with gold and ivory.

The people of Atlantis were rich and strong. They had hot and cold springs, a great navy, and 10,000 war chariots. But over many years, they became greedy for power. They attacked Athens, and Athens beat them.

Then the earthquakes and floods came. In “a single day and night of misfortune,” the island sank into the sea. Plato says that only a bank of mud was left.

That is the report. Now, who is the witness?

## A long chain of witnesses

Plato does not say that he saw Atlantis. In his book, a man named Critias tells the story. Critias says it came down through his family. The family got it from Solon, a great lawmaker of Athens. And Solon heard it from priests in the Egyptian city of Sais, between 590 and 580 BCE.

The priests told Solon that the war with Atlantis happened 9,000 years earlier. That puts it around 9600 BCE.

A detective would frown at this. The story went from the priests to Solon, then through a family, then to Critias, and then to Plato. Nobody in that chain saw the island. And outside Plato’s two books, no ancient source tells the story at all.

There is one more detail. In *Timaeus*, Critias says that the story is strange, but true. Does that prove anything? No. It is also what a good storyteller would say.

So the witness is weak. It is time to look at the suspects.

## Suspect one: a sunken continent

For most of history, readers saw Atlantis as a story with a lesson. That changed in 1882. A former United States congressman named Ignatius Donnelly published a book called *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World*.

Donnelly said that the island was real. He said that the old civilizations of Africa, Europe, and the Americas all began there. The book sold well. Scholars and scientists rejected it. But the hunt had begun.

Next came Edgar Cayce, an American psychic. He said that he saw Atlantis in trances. He is said to have predicted that part of it would rise near Bimini, in the Bahamas, in 1968 or 1969.

In 1968, a rock formation was found under the water there. It is called the Bimini Road. Believers said it was proof. Geologists looked closer. They said it is natural beachrock.

Then came the hardest evidence of all. In the 1960s, scientists worked out how the surface of the Earth moves. It is made of giant plates of rock that slowly move apart and push together. This idea is called plate tectonics. The Atlantic Ocean formed as these plates moved apart.

Ships, satellites, and small submarines have now mapped the floor of the Atlantic. It is made of young basalt rock, not the rock of a continent. Nobody has found a sunken land like Plato’s island. And continents cannot sink. They are made of light rock that floats on the heavier rock below, like a cork on water.

Suspect one is cleared. No giant island went down in the Atlantic. But could a smaller, real disaster hide behind the story?

## Suspect two: a real disaster

Look at the island of Thera. Today it is called Santorini, and it lies about 70 miles north of Crete. Around 1600 BCE, a volcano blew this island apart. Experts still argue about the exact date. It was one of the largest eruptions in human history.

The cloud of ash may have risen about 20 miles into the sky. That is more than three times the height of Mount Everest. Giant waves hit the north coast of Crete.

On Crete lived the Minoans. They were a great sea power, with a grand palace at Knossos and their own writing. Some experts think the eruption helped to bring them down. Others say that the Minoans recovered quickly.

An island people. A sea power. A sudden disaster. It sounds like Atlantis. But two clues do not fit. The date does not match Plato’s 9,000 years. And Thera is inside the Mediterranean, not beyond the Pillars of Hercules. Most scholars doubt the link.

There is a second disaster, and it is much closer to Plato. In 373 BCE, an earthquake and a giant wave destroyed the Greek city of Helike. Ancient writers say that the city sank and the sea covered it. Many people in Greece talked and wrote about it.

Plato was about 54 years old that year. In 2001, a team said that it had found the site of Helike, buried in an old lagoon. Some scholars think that this lost city helped to shape the story of Atlantis.

So Plato may not have needed Egyptian priests. A city had drowned in his own lifetime. That leaves one suspect.

## Suspect three: Plato himself

Look at how the story is built. In the story, Athens is brave and careful. Atlantis is rich, wasteful, and hungry for war. Each one is the opposite of the other, like a face in a mirror. Athens wins through courage. Atlantis falls through pride.

Real history is not so tidy. A lesson is.

Plato had done this before. In another book, *The Republic*, he described a perfect state. It was a model, not a real place. Many scholars think that Atlantis does the same job. It is a warning about what happens when a people becomes greedy for power.

Most experts on ancient Greece now agree. They believe that Plato invented Atlantis to explain his ideas.

## The case today

So what do we know? No continent sank in the Atlantic. Real disasters, like Thera and Helike, may have given Plato some of his ideas. And the story fits his teaching very well. The best answer is that Atlantis was never a place. It was a story.

But one part of the case stays open. Go back to that last page. Zeus calls the gods together and begins to speak. What did Plato want him to say? Why did Plato stop?

Nobody knows. The most famous lost city in the world ends in the middle of a sentence.
