# The Lost City of Atlantis

*Draft B: the friendly guide voice*

## A book with no ending

Imagine that you are reading a great adventure. You reach the most exciting part. You turn the page, and the rest of the book is not there.

That is how the story of Atlantis ends. Zeus, the king of the Greek gods, is angry with a rich and proud island. He calls all the gods to a meeting. He begins to speak. And then the writing stops, right in the middle of a sentence.

The writer was Plato, a famous thinker who lived in the Greek city of Athens. He wrote the story around 360 BCE, and he never finished it. That was 2,400 years ago, and people are still talking about his island.

They have looked for it in the Azores, the Bahamas, the North Sea, and even Antarctica. So far, nobody has found as much as one brick. So let us ask the big question together. Was Atlantis ever real?

## The island in the story

First, let us see what Plato wrote. He told the story in two books, called *Timaeus* and *Critias*. This part is important, so keep it in mind. These two books are the only ancient source for Atlantis. Every film, novel, and theory that you have seen began here.

Plato put his island in the ocean beyond the Pillars of Hercules. You can find that place on a map today. It is the Strait of Gibraltar, where the Mediterranean Sea meets the Atlantic. And the island was not small. Plato says it was larger than Libya and Asia together.

The capital city had a very unusual shape. Think of a target. In the middle was a hill. Around the hill was a ring of water, then a ring of land, then more water. Bridges and a canal joined the rings together.

On the hill stood the palace of the kings. Beside it was a temple to Poseidon, the god of the sea. The temple was covered in silver, with gold and ivory. The people even had hot and cold springs, a little like running water at home.

Atlantis was strong, too. It had a great navy and 10,000 war chariots. But here the story turns dark. Over many years, the people became greedy for power. They attacked Athens, and they lost.

Then the earthquakes and floods came. In “a single day and night of misfortune,” the whole island sank into the sea. Plato says that only a bank of mud was left.

It is a wonderful story. But where did Plato get it?

## Who told Plato?

This part is a little like the game where you whisper a message along a line of friends.

Plato does not say that he saw Atlantis. In his book, a man named Critias tells the story. Critias says that it came down through his family. The family got it from Solon, a great lawmaker of Athens. And Solon heard it from priests in the Egyptian city of Sais, between 590 and 580 BCE.

The priests said that the war with Atlantis happened 9,000 years before that. This puts it around 9600 BCE, which is a very, very long time for a story to travel.

Now count the steps. The story goes from the priests to Solon, then through a family, then to Critias, and then to Plato. Not one of these people saw the island. And outside Plato’s two books, no ancient source tells the story.

In *Timaeus*, Critias does say that the tale is strange, but true. Of course, many good stories start that way.

So we cannot be sure that the story was old when Plato wrote it. For a long time, most readers were happy with that. Then some people decided to go and look.

## The people who went looking

For most of history, readers saw Atlantis as a story with a lesson. That changed in 1882. A former United States congressman, Ignatius Donnelly, published a book called *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World*.

Donnelly was sure that the island was real. He said that the old civilizations of Africa, Europe, and the Americas all began there. Scholars and scientists did not agree with him. But the book sold well, and the great Atlantis hunt began.

Later came Edgar Cayce, an American psychic. He said that he saw Atlantis in trances. He is said to have predicted that part of it would rise near Bimini, in the Bahamas, in 1968 or 1969.

And in 1968, something was found under the water there. It was a rock formation, and people named it the Bimini Road. You can imagine how excited the believers were. But geologists studied it. They say it is natural beachrock, made by nature and not by people.

What does science say about the bigger idea? In the 1960s, scientists learned how the surface of the Earth moves. It is made of giant plates of rock. They move apart and push together very slowly. This is called plate tectonics. The Atlantic Ocean formed as these plates moved apart.

Since then, ships, satellites, and small submarines have mapped the floor of the Atlantic. It is made of young basalt rock, not the rock of a continent. Nobody has found a sunken land like Plato’s island.

There is also a simple reason why they will not find one. Continents cannot sink. They are made of light rock that floats on the heavier rock below, like a cork in a bath.

So a giant island did not go down in the Atlantic. But perhaps something real is still hidden in the story.

## Two real disasters

Let us travel to the island of Thera. Today it is called Santorini, and it lies about 70 miles north of Crete. Around 1600 BCE, a volcano blew this island apart. Experts still argue about the exact date. It was one of the largest eruptions in human history.

The cloud of ash may have risen about 20 miles into the sky. That is more than three times the height of Mount Everest. Giant waves hit the north coast of Crete.

The people of Crete were the Minoans. They were a great sea power, with a grand palace at Knossos and their own writing. Some experts think the eruption helped to end their power. Others say that the Minoans recovered quickly.

You can see why people think of Atlantis here. An island people ruled the sea, and then a sudden disaster came. But two things do not fit. The date does not match Plato’s 9,000 years. And Thera is inside the Mediterranean, not beyond the Pillars of Hercules. Most scholars doubt the link.

There is a second disaster, and I think it is the more interesting one. In 373 BCE, an earthquake and a giant wave destroyed the Greek city of Helike. Ancient writers say that the city sank and the sea covered it. Many people in Greece talked and wrote about it.

Here is the surprising part. Plato was alive at the time. He was about 54 years old. In 2001, a team said that it had found the site of Helike, buried in an old lagoon. Some scholars think that this lost city helped to shape the story of Atlantis.

So Plato did not need to look far for a drowned city. One was in the news of his own day.

## A story with a job to do

Now look at how Plato built his story. His Athens is brave and careful. His Atlantis is rich, wasteful, and hungry for war. Each one is the opposite of the other, like a face in a mirror. Athens wins through courage. Atlantis falls through pride.

Real history is usually messier than that. But a story with a lesson is often this tidy.

And Plato liked to teach with stories. In another book, *The Republic*, he described a perfect state. It was a model, not a real place. Many scholars think that Atlantis does the same job. It warns us about what happens when a people becomes greedy for power.

Most experts on ancient Greece agree about this today. They believe that Plato invented Atlantis to explain his ideas.

## So, was it real?

Let us put it all together. No continent sank in the Atlantic. Real disasters, like Thera and Helike, may have given Plato some ideas. And the story fits his teaching very well. So the best answer is a simple one. Atlantis was probably never a place. It was a story.

But what a story. It is 2,400 years old, and you have just read about it.

And one small mystery is still open. Remember the missing ending? Zeus calls the gods together and begins to speak. What did Plato want him to say? Why did Plato stop?

Nobody knows. Perhaps that is why we still cannot put the book down.
